# Life Decision
*Life Decision*

**Category:** [Pre-built Chains](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---

## Step 1 — First Principles: What do you actually value?

Before weighing options, articulate your values — not the values you think you should have, not the values your culture endorses, but the conditions under which you have been most alive, most engaged, and most satisfied. These are your first principles for the decision. The options that maximize your stated values (not your assumed values) are the ones worth exploring.

---

## Step 2 — Inversion: What would make you regret this choice?

Bezos's regret minimization framework: project yourself to 80 years old and imagine looking back at this decision. Which option is more likely to produce the statement "I wish I had..."? Most people regret inaction more than action, and over-caution more than bold risk.

---

## Step 3 — Thought Experiment: What would your ideal version of this look like?

Remove practical constraints for a moment and describe the ideal version of the path you're considering. This reveals what you actually want, separated from what you believe is available. Then ask: how far can I close the gap between ideal and achievable?

---

## Step 4 — Second-order Effects: What does each option make possible and what does it foreclose?

Major life decisions are not just outcomes; they are platforms. What future options does each choice keep open? What does each close off? The option that maintains the most optionality in the right direction is generally preferable at high levels of uncertainty.

---

## Step 5 — Circle of Competence: Am I the right person to make this decision alone?

For decisions that significantly affect others, or that involve domains where your judgment is less reliable (your own blind spots are hardest to see in personal decisions), seek input from people who know you well and will tell you the truth. Not people who will be supportive; people who will be honest.

---

## Step 6 — Sunk Cost: Am I staying on this path because it makes forward sense?

Explicitly separate what you have invested in the current path from what the current path offers going forward. The history is not a reason to continue; only the forward value is.

---

## Step 7 — Probabilistic Thinking: What are the realistic scenarios, not the tail scenarios?

Life decisions are often dominated by fear of the worst tail scenario or hope for the best. Ask: what is the base-rate outcome for people who make this choice? What does the realistic middle scenario look like, not the best or worst?

---

## Step 8 — Margin of Safety: Can you reverse this decision?

Reversible decisions (moving cities, taking a new job) deserve less analysis and faster action than irreversible ones (having children, selling an asset that cannot be repurchased). For truly irreversible decisions, the margin of safety principle argues for proportionally more deliberation, more consultation, and more explicit modeling of the downside scenarios.
