# Design and Launch an Innovation Program

**Category:** [Pre-built Chains](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## When to Use

Use this chain after completing the Innovation Capability Diagnostic — when the organization has committed to building a structured innovation capability and needs to move from diagnosis to design. The chain assumes an incumbent organization with an existing core business. It covers the seven decisions that determine whether an innovation program becomes real or remains theater.

---

## Step 1 — Dominant Logic: Declare what the program is explicitly not constrained by

Before designing anything structural, name the dominant logic constraints surfaced in the diagnostic and declare which ones the program is authorized to operate outside. An innovation program that is subject to all existing business model assumptions, approval processes, and performance metrics will reproduce the current organization. The sponsor — typically the CEO or a board member — must articulate: "This program may explore business models that compete with our core," or "This program may invest in opportunities below our current margin threshold." Without explicit authorization, the dominant logic will reassert itself within two quarters.

---

## Step 2 — Ambidextrous Organization: Choose and commit to a structural model

Select the structural model for the program — separated unit, contextual, or cyclical — and design it deliberately. For most incumbents with an existing innovation problem, structural separation is the right starting point: a distinct team with separate leadership, separate P&L or budget, and separate performance metrics. Define the integration mechanism explicitly: how will the explore unit connect to the exploit core? Who owns the handoff of validated ideas into deployment? What happens when an explore discovery threatens core revenue? These integration questions are harder than the structural ones and must be answered before launch, not after the first conflict.

---

## Step 3 — Organizational Slack: Secure real capacity before announcing the program

The most common failure mode is launching an innovation program with people who are already fully committed to operational work. Before announcing or recruiting, secure a genuine capacity commitment: dedicated people (not "10% of your time"), protected budget that is not subject to quarterly reallocation, and leadership time that is on the calendar and not displaced by operational escalations. The capacity commitment is the first test of organizational seriousness. If leadership cannot free up real capacity, the program will fail regardless of its design quality. Size the team for the learning goal, not the output goal — in early stages, three to five people with genuine dedication outperform twenty people with fractional commitment.

---

## Step 4 — Design Thinking: Build the customer discovery practice first

Before running any experiments, establish the habit of deep customer observation. The innovation program's first output should not be ideas or prototypes — it should be a clear, evidence-based understanding of the jobs customers are trying to do that the current organization serves poorly or not at all. Run structured discovery sessions: observe customers in context, conduct non-leading interviews focused on struggles and workarounds, and synthesize findings into specific unmet need statements. This step is skipped most often and is the most common reason innovation programs generate solutions in search of problems. Discovery is not a one-time input; it is an ongoing practice that feeds the pipeline.

---

## Step 5 — Build-Measure-Learn: Install the experiment discipline

Design the program's operating rhythm around short learning cycles rather than project milestones. Each team takes one assumption — the most critical unknown about whether a proposed solution creates value — and designs the smallest experiment that would test it. Measure against a pre-defined learning criterion, not output metrics. Debrief every cycle: what did we learn, what does that do to our confidence in the opportunity, what is the next assumption to test? The experiment discipline is the engine of the program; everything else is scaffolding. Instill the vocabulary: what is the assumption, what is the test, what would confirm or disconfirm it. This language change alone shifts the culture of what "progress" means.

---

## Step 6 — Open Innovation: Map what should be built versus accessed externally

Early in the program, run a deliberate make-vs-access review. For each capability area the program needs, ask: is this a core differentiation where internal development creates lasting advantage, or is it an enabling capability where partnering, licensing, or acquiring is faster and cheaper? Most innovation programs try to build too much internally. Identify two or three external sourcing relationships to establish in the first year: a university research partner, a startup ecosystem connection, a technology licensing relationship, or a corporate venture vehicle. Open sourcing expands the program's adjacent possible without proportionally expanding headcount.

---

## Step 7 — Innovation Accounting: Define what success looks like before the first cycle

Before the program produces any results, agree with the sponsoring executive on how it will be measured — and explicitly distinguish innovation metrics from operational metrics. Innovation accounting tracks learning velocity (how many validated insights per quarter), funnel health (ideas generated, experiments run, validations achieved, deployments made), and portfolio balance (how many bets at each stage). It does not track revenue or margin in the first 12–18 months unless a validated concept has been deliberately accelerated into deployment. Setting the wrong success metrics — applying H1 performance standards to an H3 exploration effort — is a governance decision that will kill the program. Get explicit sign-off on the metrics before launch, and revisit them at the six-month mark.

---

## Governance and Rhythm

Run a monthly program review with the sponsor covering: experiments completed and learned, pipeline movement, resource consumption vs. plan, and any structural issues requiring intervention (dominant logic pushback, capacity erosion, integration conflicts). Keep the review focused on learning rate, not activity rate. A quarterly portfolio review — separate from the monthly operational review — examines horizon balance and strategic fit of what's in the funnel. The program should produce a deployment or a deliberate kill decision on at least one significant bet within 18 months; anything older than that is almost certainly stalled.

**Key failure modes to avoid:** launching without genuine capacity commitment; applying operational metrics before the program has had time to validate anything; skipping customer discovery in favor of solution generation; failing to design the integration mechanism before the first successful experiment hits the deployment question.
