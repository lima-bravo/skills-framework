# Tactical Pause

**Category:** [Leadership](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

The Tactical Pause is a US Army practice: a deliberate, time-bounded halt in the middle of an operation when conditions have changed enough that continuing to execute the current plan may be worse than stopping to reassess. It is distinguished from indecision or hesitation by its deliberateness — a Tactical Pause is called explicitly, has a defined purpose, and ends with a decision to continue, adapt, or stop.

The military context is the "fog of war": the condition in which information is incomplete, the situation is ambiguous, and the plan developed before contact with the enemy no longer maps accurately to reality. In this condition, continuing to execute a plan that no longer fits is not discipline — it is a failure to exercise judgment. The Tactical Pause is the institutional mechanism for making judgment possible under pressure.

The organisational equivalent is the moment when a project is deep in execution and something has materially changed — a key assumption has been invalidated, a significant risk has materialised, the brief has shifted, or the team has discovered that the plan cannot produce the intended end state. In this moment, the path of least resistance is to continue moving: stopping feels like failure, and there is usually pressure to keep delivering. The Tactical Pause is permission — and a discipline — to treat stopping as the more intelligent choice.

The US Army's framing captures the asymmetry: a Tactical Pause "takes minutes and saves weeks." The cost of stopping briefly to recalibrate is almost always less than the cost of executing a wrong plan to its conclusion.

## When to Call One

A Tactical Pause is warranted when any of the following conditions are present:

- A key assumption embedded in the plan has proven false
- The scope, brief, or success criteria have changed materially since planning
- The team has encountered an obstacle that cannot be resolved within the current approach
- The team is moving fast but losing confidence that the destination is still right
- Significant new information has arrived that the original plan did not account for
- The team is about to commit resources at a decision point that cannot be undone

Notably absent from this list: normal difficulty, expected setbacks, or execution challenges that the plan anticipated. The Tactical Pause is for material changes in the situation, not for the ordinary friction of execution.

## Structure of a Tactical Pause

A Tactical Pause has a short, consistent structure:

1. **Stop** — explicitly halt current activity. Name what has changed: "We've hit a situation that our plan doesn't cover. We're pausing."

2. **Gather** — quickly collect what is known. What do we know now that we didn't know when we planned? What has changed? What is the current state of the end state — are we still on track to achieve it?

3. **Think** — reassess. Does the current plan still achieve the Leader's Intent? If not, what needs to change? What options are available? This step is time-capped: it is not a replanning exercise; it is a decision point.

4. **Move** — make an explicit choice: continue the current plan (the situation is recoverable), adapt (a specific element of the plan changes), or stop (the situation has changed enough that proceeding would be wrong). Whatever the decision, name it and recommit.

The entire cycle should take minutes for a small team, hours for a complex project. It is not a meeting; it is a decision-making sprint.

## The Permission Problem

The reason organisations need a named practice for stopping mid-execution is that the social and institutional forces against stopping are strong. In cultures that equate movement with progress, pausing reads as loss of momentum. Leaders may interpret a pause request as a capability or confidence failure. Teams may fear that raising the alarm is career-limiting.

The Tactical Pause addresses this by making stopping a disciplined, sanctioned action rather than an admission of failure. When it is a named protocol — "I'm calling a Tactical Pause" — it signals judgment, not hesitation. The framing shifts from "we have a problem" to "we are exercising the right discipline at the right moment."

This requires, as a precondition, that the Tactical Pause is genuinely encouraged by leadership and not punished. Leaders who respond to a Tactical Pause with "why didn't you just get on with it?" will quickly train their teams to skip it.

## Mental Model

Think of execution as driving on a motorway at speed. The default is to keep moving: braking feels like losing ground. But when visibility drops, road conditions change, or your GPS route has been invalidated by a closed road ahead, the intelligent response is not to maintain speed — it is to briefly slow down or stop, gather information, and then choose a route. Drivers who refuse to slow down in fog because they are "making progress" are not disciplined; they are dangerous.

The Tactical Pause is the organisational equivalent of slowing to assess before a decision that cannot be undone. The cost of the pause is small. The cost of the wrong decision at speed is large.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Call it explicitly and name what changed.** "I'm calling a Tactical Pause — our plan no longer covers the situation" converts stopping from a sign of weakness into a sanctioned act of judgment, which is the entire point of having a named protocol.
- **Reserve it for material change, not ordinary friction.** A Tactical Pause is for invalidated assumptions, shifted briefs, and irreversible decision points — not for the expected difficulty the plan already anticipated.
- **Time-cap the Think step.** The pause is a decision sprint, not a replanning exercise: gather what is now known, test the plan against the intent, and end with an explicit choice.
- **End with a named decision and recommit.** Continue, adapt, or stop — say it out loud and re-engage; a pause that drifts back into execution without an explicit choice is just hesitation.
- **Pre-register the triggers in the plan.** A good pre-mortem names the conditions that should cause the team to stop and reassess, so the pause is triggered by design rather than by someone working up the courage to raise a hand.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Never stopping because movement feels like progress.** In cultures that equate motion with achievement, the team executes a plan it already knows is wrong because pausing reads as failure — the exact failure the protocol exists to prevent.
- **Leadership punishing the pause.** A leader who responds with "why didn't you just get on with it?" trains the team to skip it, and the discipline dies on first use.
- **Pausing for ordinary friction.** Calling a halt at every expected setback turns the protocol into indecision and burns its credibility, leaving it unavailable when a real material change arrives.
- **Pausing without deciding.** Stopping to reassess and then resuming the same plan with no explicit continue/adapt/stop choice wastes the pause and leaves the team no clearer than before.

## Worked Example

A product team is three months into a six-month build when usage data from an early release invalidates the core assumption behind the roadmap: the workflow they are automating is not the one customers actually struggle with. The path of least resistance is to keep building — the plan is set, stopping feels like admitting the last three months were wrong, and there is pressure to ship. Instead the lead calls a Tactical Pause: stop (name that a load-bearing assumption has failed), gather (what does the data now show, and is the original end state still right?), think (does the current plan still achieve the intent? — no), move (adapt: re-scope the next three months around the workflow the data points to). The pause costs an afternoon. Continuing on the invalidated plan would have cost three months and shipped a feature few customers wanted — the asymmetry the Army captures as "takes minutes, saves weeks." The discipline was not in having the insight; it was in treating stopping as the intelligent move rather than the embarrassing one.
