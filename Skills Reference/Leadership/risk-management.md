# Risk Management

**Category:** [Leadership](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

Enterprise Risk Management (ERM): the systematic process of identifying, assessing, and responding to risks that could affect an organisation's ability to achieve its objectives. Three foundational concepts: **Risk appetite** (amount of risk the board will accept), **Risk tolerance** (acceptable variation around appetite), and the **Three Lines of Defence** — (1) operations owns risk, (2) risk/compliance provides oversight, (3) internal audit provides independent assurance.

The goal is not to eliminate risk — it is to hold the right risks at the right level.

## Mental Model

Think of risk management not as the elimination of risk but as deciding, deliberately and at the right level, which risks to hold and how much. Every organisation runs on risk; the question is whether the risks it carries are the ones it chose or the ones that accumulated by default. The discipline has two moving parts: setting risk appetite (how much the board will accept, in pursuit of what return) and ensuring the residual risk actually carried sits within that appetite. The Three Lines of Defence exist to keep both honest — operations owning risk where it is created, oversight checking that ownership, and independent assurance checking the checkers — because risk that no one explicitly owns is risk that grows unwatched until it materialises.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Set risk appetite at the top, explicitly.** The board defines how much risk the organisation will accept; without a stated appetite, "risk management" degenerates into ad hoc reactions to whatever is currently frightening.
- **Manage to residual risk, not inherent risk.** What matters is the risk that remains after controls; track whether it sits inside appetite, and treat controls as the thing that closes the gap between the two.
- **Use the Three Lines and keep them genuinely independent.** Operations owns the risk, risk/compliance oversees, internal audit assures — collapsing these (audit reporting to the people it audits) removes the independence that makes the model work.
- **Distinguish appetite from tolerance.** Appetite is the level you target; tolerance is the acceptable variation around it. Naming both prevents every minor deviation from triggering a crisis response.
- **Match the tool to the risk type.** Scenario planning for strategic and external risk, pre-mortems for specific initiatives, margin of safety for operational exposure — one instrument does not cover the whole landscape.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Trying to eliminate risk rather than hold the right risks.** Aiming for zero risk either paralyses the organisation or, more often, drives risk underground where it is no longer measured.
- **Risk registers as theatre.** Maintaining an elaborate register that is updated for audit and never used to make a decision; the document exists, the discipline does not.
- **Compromised independence in the third line.** When assurance reports to the executives it is meant to assure, the Three Lines collapse into one and the board loses its independent line of sight — the same epistemic capture that sinks governance.
- **Managing the measurable and ignoring the catastrophic.** Focusing on frequent, quantifiable risks while leaving the rare, high-impact tail events unexamined — precisely the risks that destroy organisations.

## Worked Example

A bank's risk function maintains a detailed register, hits every compliance deadline, and reports green across the board — yet carries a concentration of correlated exposures that no one has framed as a single risk because each piece sits within a different team's limits. When the correlated event arrives, the losses dwarf anything in the register. The failure is not missing process; it is process without the discipline behind it. Risk appetite was never set at the level of "how much correlated exposure will we hold in total," so residual risk was managed locally and never aggregated; the third line assured the existence of controls rather than their sufficiency; and the catastrophic tail sat outside the frequent, measurable risks the register was built around. Effective risk management would have started at the top — a board-level statement of appetite for concentration risk — and worked down through aggregated residual-risk reporting and genuinely independent assurance, so the risk the bank carried was a risk it had chosen, not one that accumulated unwatched.
