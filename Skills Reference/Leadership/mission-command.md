# Mission Command

**Category:** [Leadership](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

Mission Command (Auftragstaktik) is the doctrine that commanders should communicate **what** must be achieved and **why**, then leave **how** to the person with the best situational information. Developed by the Prussian military under Helmuth von Moltke the Elder, it was a rejection of rigid top-down orders in favour of adaptive, decentralised execution. Von Moltke's insight: no plan survives first contact with reality, and the only defence against an unpredictable environment is a force whose every member understands the commander's intent deeply enough to improvise intelligently when the plan breaks.

## Mental Model

Think of Mission Command as the difference between a GPS app and a printed turn-by-turn list. The list fails the moment you take a wrong turn; the GPS re-routes because it knows the destination.

Three layers make Mission Command work:
1. **Clarity of intent** — what does success look like at the end state?
2. **Boundaries** — what constraints (resources, ethics, timing) are non-negotiable?
3. **Trust and competence** — subordinates must be capable enough to fill in the gaps.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Brief the why before the what.** State purpose and end state first; the task list comes after and stays short. A team that knows why can improvise when the plan breaks; a team that only knows what cannot.
- **Define the non-negotiable boundaries explicitly.** Name the constraints — budget, ethics, timing, brand — that may not be crossed, then leave everything inside them to the team's judgment. Unstated boundaries get discovered by violation.
- **Calibrate delegation to competence, not hierarchy.** Mission Command assumes the person closest to the problem can decide; where that judgment is genuinely absent, build it before delegating rather than pretending the doctrine works regardless.
- **Verify intent was received — don't assume it.** Pair every intent briefing with a brief-back: have the team restate purpose and end state in their own words before they plan.
- **Resist re-taking control when the plan deviates.** Deviation from the plan is expected; only deviation from the intent should pull you back in.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Delegating method while leaving intent vague.** The worst of both worlds: the team is told how but not why, so it executes precisely toward the wrong end state the moment conditions change.
- **"Empowerment" without competence or boundaries.** Handing autonomy to a team that lacks the judgment or information to use it is abdication, not Mission Command — confident movement in the wrong direction.
- **Punishing good-faith improvisation that didn't work.** The first time a leader second-guesses a defensible call made under uncertainty, the team learns to wait for orders, and the doctrine is dead.
- **Confusing Mission Command with absence of leadership.** Setting clear intent and hard boundaries is more demanding than issuing orders, not less.

## Worked Example

L. David Marquet took command of the USS Santa Fe, the worst-performing submarine in its fleet, and found a crew trained to wait for orders. Rather than issue better orders, he changed the unit of communication: officers stopped asking permission ("Request permission to submerge") and started declaring intent ("I intend to submerge the ship"), stating the purpose and the conditions they had already checked. Marquet retained the authority to stop any action, but the default shifted to the person closest to the problem deciding and the commander verifying intent. Within a year the Santa Fe went from worst to first, and went on to produce a disproportionate number of officers who earned their own commands — the signature of Mission Command done well: it builds the very judgment it depends on.
