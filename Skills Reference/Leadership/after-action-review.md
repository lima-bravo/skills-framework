# After-Action Review

**Category:** Leadership
**Tagline:** Four questions, asked immediately after, by the people who did the work — the operational learning drill that compounds with every project

## Definition

The After-Action Review (AAR) is a structured learning drill developed by the US Army and conducted immediately after any significant operation or project by the people who executed it. It runs through four questions in sequence:

1. **What was supposed to happen?** — Reconstruct the intent and plan as understood at the start. This is not "what should have happened in hindsight" but "what did we expect and commit to." Establishes the baseline against which actuality is measured.

2. **What actually happened?** — Reconstruct events as they unfolded, with as much factual specificity as possible. Separate observation from interpretation at this stage. The goal is a shared account, not individual perspectives; where accounts differ, the difference is itself data.

3. **Why was there a gap?** — Analyse the difference between expected and actual. Was the plan wrong? Was execution poor? Did the situation change in ways that couldn't have been anticipated? Were there assumptions embedded in the plan that turned out to be false? This is the highest-value question and the one most likely to be rushed.

4. **What do we sustain, and what do we improve?** — Explicitly preserve what worked and address what didn't. The "sustain" half is as important as the "improve" half: knowing which practices were causal to success prevents them from being accidentally discarded in the next cycle of improvement.

The four-question structure is a drill, not a framework. Its value comes from regularity and speed, not from depth on any single occasion. An AAR conducted well in 20 minutes immediately after delivery produces more learning than a thorough retrospective run two weeks later when memory has degraded and the emotional temperature has changed.

## How the AAR Differs from a Post-mortem

The Post-mortem is an investigative process: deep, structured, often facilitated, typically run after a significant failure or incident. Its goal is to identify systemic causes and produce durable process changes.

The AAR is an operational habit: fast, immediate, run after every project by the practitioners themselves, with or without a facilitator. Its goal is to capture learning while it is fresh and to build a culture in which reflection is a normal part of execution.

They are complementary, not substitutes. The AAR is the daily practice; the Post-mortem is the deep investigation triggered when the AAR surfaces a pattern serious enough to warrant it.

Three specific distinctions matter:

**Immediacy** — The AAR is conducted immediately after the operation, while memory is fresh and the emotional context is present. The two-week delay typical of post-mortems allows memory reconstruction and narrative smoothing that reduces the accuracy of the account.

**Practitioners, not investigators** — The AAR is run by the people who did the work, not by a separate review function. This preserves tacit knowledge — the things people know but may not think to tell an external reviewer — and builds reflective capacity in the team rather than outsourcing it.

**Sustain as well as improve** — Post-mortems are triggered by failures; they systematically underrepresent successful practices. The AAR's explicit "sustain" question corrects this bias by treating every project as a source of both positive and negative signal.

## Mental Model

Think of the AAR as closing the feedback loop between intention and execution. Without it, experience accumulates but learning does not — the team has been through many projects, but the distance between what they plan and what happens stays roughly constant because the gap has never been formally examined. The AAR converts raw experience into structured knowledge at the point of maximum signal clarity, before the signal degrades.

The military phrase is "learning at the speed of operations." The civilian equivalent: every project is an experiment whose results should update the next project. The AAR is the mechanism that ensures the update actually happens.

## Facilitation Notes

**Who runs it:** Ideally a team member, not the leader. When the leader runs the AAR, the team unconsciously filters their observations through what the leader wants to hear.

**Who attends:** Everyone who did the work. Not stakeholders, sponsors, or observers — the practitioners.

**How long:** 20–45 minutes for a standard project. The length of question 3 (why the gap) scales with the significance of the gap. Do not compress it.

**The hardest part:** Question 3 (why the gap). Teams tend to attribute gaps to external factors ("the client changed the brief") rather than internal ones ("we didn't surface the ambiguity before we started"). Useful probing: "What could we have done differently that would have changed the outcome?" This shifts the frame from explanation to agency.

**What to do with the output:** Write it down. One paragraph per question. File it in a shared location. Review a set of AARs quarterly for patterns — repeated gaps across projects are the most valuable organisational learning signal available.

## Connections

- **Post-mortem** — complementary disciplines at different scales: AAR is the operational habit after every project; Post-mortem is the deep investigation triggered by a significant failure or a repeated pattern surfaced by AARs
- **Leader's Intent** — the AAR's first question ("what was supposed to happen") is the direct test of how well the intent was communicated and retained; poor answers to Q1 are a Leader's Intent failure, not an execution failure
- **Brief-Back Protocols** — the gap between Q1 and Q2 in the AAR is often traceable to misalignments that Brief-Back Protocols should have caught; AAR findings should improve briefing quality
- **Feedback Loops** — the AAR is the mechanism that closes the organisational feedback loop between execution and planning; without it, the loop is open and learning does not compound
- **Psychological Safety** — honest answers to questions 2 and 3 require an environment where admitting error is safe; an AAR run in a blame culture produces a sanitised account, not a useful one
- **Mission Command** — the AAR is the learning infrastructure that makes Mission Command sustainable over time; teams that practice it develop the judgment and calibration that Mission Command requires
- **Calibration** — the AAR generates the outcome data needed to improve calibration; without a learning cycle that compares predictions to results, calibration cannot develop

## References
