# Leader's Intent
*A three-part statement of purpose, key tasks, and end state that guides judgment when the plan no longer fits the situation*

**Category:** [Leadership](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---

## Definition

Leader's Intent is the US Army's structured tool for communicating mission context. It is a brief, three-part statement that a leader delivers when briefing a team on any significant piece of work:

**Purpose** — why this work matters. Not what we are doing, but why it matters in terms of the larger goal. The purpose is the anchor: when the plan breaks, the team navigates back to purpose to decide what to do next.

**Key tasks** — the few things that must happen for the mission to succeed. Not an exhaustive task list — that belongs in the plan. These are the two or three actions whose failure would make success impossible regardless of everything else done well.

**End state** — what success looks like when the work is complete. A concrete, observable description of the desired situation: what has changed, what is in place, what problem no longer exists. Not a metric — a picture.

The deliberate constraint is length: a Leader's Intent should be short enough that someone two levels down in the organisation can recall it from memory without notes. If it cannot be held in memory, it cannot guide judgment under pressure. The US Army's test: a soldier who has lost radio contact and cannot reach their commander should still be able to make a correct decision because they internalised the Leader's Intent at the briefing.

**Critically, Leader's Intent does not include how.** The method, the plan, the sequence of actions — all of that is left to the team. This is what makes it an enabling tool rather than a controlling one: it gives the team the information they need to exercise judgment, not instructions that remove the need for it.

## Why This Is Different from Mission Command

Mission Command is the philosophy: communicate intent, not method, and trust the person closest to the problem to decide. Leader's Intent is the craft that makes Mission Command operational — the specific template for how to construct and communicate intent well.

Many leaders endorse Mission Command as a principle and then fail at it in practice because they don't know how to write a Leader's Intent. They either over-specify (including method, which removes autonomy) or under-specify (omitting purpose, which removes the ability to adapt). The three-part structure — purpose, key tasks, end state — is the discipline that prevents both failure modes.

The test of a good Leader's Intent: hand it to someone who was not in the briefing and ask them what decision they would make if the plan broke and they could not reach you. If they give you the right answer, the intent was clear. If they hesitate or guess wrong, you have a communication problem, not a competence problem.

## Mental Model

Think of a Leader's Intent as a compass bearing, not a route map. A route map tells you which roads to take; it fails the moment a road is blocked. A compass bearing tells you which direction success lies; it remains valid regardless of which specific path you take to get there. The team's job is to plan the route. The leader's job is to set the bearing.

The three parts have a specific logic: purpose anchors *why* (so the team can prioritise), key tasks anchor *what cannot be skipped* (so the team knows where to hold firm), and end state anchors *what done looks like* (so the team knows when to stop and can judge whether their plan gets them there).

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Lead with purpose, then key tasks, then end state — and stop.** The three-part structure is the discipline; adding method collapses it back into a brief and removes the team's ability to adapt.
- **Make it short enough to recall from memory under pressure.** If someone two levels down cannot hold the intent without notes, it cannot guide judgment when the plan breaks and the radio goes quiet.
- **Describe the end state as an outcome, not an output.** "The leadership team is ready to decide between two costed options" guides improvisation; "deliver a slide deck" does not.
- **Name only the two or three key tasks whose failure would be fatal.** If everything is critical, nothing is, and the team loses the signal about where to hold firm versus where to flex.
- **Test the intent on someone who wasn't briefed.** Hand it to an outsider and ask what they would do if the plan failed and they couldn't reach you; hesitation or a wrong answer is a communication problem to fix before execution, not a competence problem.

## Common Failure Modes

**Confusing key tasks with the full task list** — a Leader's Intent that lists every task is not a Leader's Intent; it is a brief. Key tasks are the two or three actions whose absence makes success impossible. If everything is critical, nothing is.

**Describing end state in outputs rather than outcomes** — "deliver a slide deck" is an output. "The leadership team has a shared view of options and is ready to decide" is an end state. The difference matters when the team must improvise: output descriptions don't guide judgment in novel situations; outcome descriptions do.

**Including method** — "we will use the waterfall approach and present at the steering committee on the 15th" belongs in the plan, not the intent. When the method is baked into the intent, the team cannot adapt without feeling they are violating the brief.

**Treating it as optional for short projects** — the value of Leader's Intent is highest when the situation is uncertain, not when the work is long. A one-week project in a fast-changing environment benefits as much from clear intent as a six-month programme.

## Worked Example

A marketing director briefs a campaign team with a full task list and a fixed channel plan, then leaves for two weeks. Mid-campaign, the chosen channel's costs spike and a competitor pre-empts the core message. The team, holding a route map rather than a compass bearing, executes the now-obsolete plan because deviating would mean violating the brief — and the campaign underperforms. The same situation with a proper Leader's Intent runs differently: purpose ("shift perception among mid-market buyers ahead of the competitor's launch"), key tasks ("reach this segment at scale; land the differentiation message"), end state ("mid-market awareness measurably up and the differentiation owned before the competitor's launch"), and explicitly no method. When the channel breaks, the team reallocates spend and reframes the message itself, because it knows what success looks like and why it matters. The intent told the team where to navigate to; planning the new route was its job, and it could do that without the director in the room.
