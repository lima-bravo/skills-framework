# Board Governance
*The board sets direction and holds management accountable — it does not manage*

**Category:** [Leadership](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---

## Definition

Board Governance refers to the systems and principles by which organisations are directed and controlled at the highest level. Three fiduciary duties: **Duty of Care** (act with the diligence of a reasonably prudent person), **Duty of Loyalty** (act in the organisation's interest, not personal interest), **Duty of Obedience** (act in accordance with the organisation's purpose).

The central principle: separation between governance (board) and management (executives). Epistemic capture — believing you understand the organisation because management told you so — is the failure mode behind almost every major governance collapse.

## Mental Model

Think of the board as the instrument that holds management accountable without doing management's job — and the central tension is informational. The board carries the fiduciary duties and the ultimate accountability, but almost everything it knows about the organisation comes from the very executives it is meant to oversee. That asymmetry is the structural heart of governance: a board that accepts management's account uncritically has been captured epistemically, even if everyone is acting in good faith. The discipline of governance is therefore the discipline of maintaining an independent line of sight — through assurance functions, direct access to information, and the deliberate separation of governance from management — so the board can challenge the narrative it is given rather than merely ratify it.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Govern, don't manage — and police the line continuously.** The board sets direction, risk appetite, and accountability; the moment it starts taking executive decisions it loses both its independence and its ability to oversee them.
- **Build an independent line of sight.** Rely on internal audit, external assurance, and direct access to information rather than management's self-report alone; the second source is what makes oversight real.
- **Treat unanimous comfort as a warning sign.** A board that is never surprised and never dissents is usually being managed, not informing itself; structured challenge and red-teaming guard against epistemic capture.
- **Set risk appetite explicitly and require assurance against it.** The board's job is not to eliminate risk but to define the level it will accept and then verify that residual risk sits within it.
- **Separate the chair from the CEO where you can.** Concentrating both roles collapses the governance/management boundary into one person and removes the structural check the board exists to provide.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Epistemic capture.** Believing you understand the organisation because management told you so — the failure mode behind almost every major governance collapse.
- **Drifting into management.** Boards that get absorbed in operational detail lose the altitude and independence required to oversee the executives running those operations.
- **Rubber-stamping.** Treating board meetings as ratification of decisions already made, where dissent is socially costly and challenge is mistaken for disloyalty.
- **Over-reliance on quantitative reporting.** Governing by dashboard creates Goodhart pressure throughout the organisation and misses the qualitative signals financial metrics never capture.

## Worked Example

The collapses that produced modern governance codes follow one pattern. At Enron, a capable board received the account management chose to give it, approved the off-balance-sheet structures on the strength of that account, and never built the independent line of sight that would have shown the residual risk it was carrying. The failure was not, mainly, fraudulent directors; it was epistemic capture — a board that believed it understood the company because management told it so. The post-Enron architecture (Sarbanes-Oxley, strengthened audit independence, the Cadbury principles before it) is essentially a set of mechanisms to break that capture: independent audit committees, direct board access to assurance, and the separation of oversight from the people being overseen. The lesson for any board, public or private, is that good faith in management is not a substitute for an independent source of truth.
