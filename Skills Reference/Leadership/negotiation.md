# Negotiation

**Category:** [Leadership](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

Negotiation is the discipline of reaching an agreement with a counterpart whose interests genuinely conflict with yours on at least some dimension — price, scope, terms, timing — while each side retains a real alternative to no deal at all. It is distinct from building internal support (no one is required to agree with you) and distinct from a shared-goal collaboration (the interests are not fully aligned). The two classical modes are distributive bargaining (a fixed pie, where one side's gain is the other's loss) and integrative bargaining (finding trades across issues each side values differently, so both sides do better than a simple split). Most real negotiations are a bounded distributive core wrapped in a larger integrative structure that goes undiscovered unless someone looks for it.

---

## Mental Model

Most of a negotiation's outcome is decided before anyone sits down. Lax and Sebenius's three dimensions make this precise: tactics (what is said and countered at the table), setup (who is actually present, what is linked to what, what alternatives exist away from the table), and deal design (structuring the agreement itself so that trading across differently-valued priorities creates value neither side could claim alone). Tactics are the most visible dimension and the least leveraged one — a negotiator who walks in prepared only to argue a number has skipped the two dimensions that decide most of the outcome before the first sentence is spoken.

The single number that matters most is not the number you want. It is your BATNA — the best alternative to a negotiated agreement, what you get if this negotiation fails entirely. Every other move — the opening anchor, the walk-away point, the willingness to concede — is calibrated against that number. A negotiator who has not priced their own alternative is negotiating on vibes: unable to tell a good deal from a bad one, because there is no honest baseline to compare it to. The same logic runs in reverse — diagnosing the other side's likely BATNA, not their opening ask, is what tells you the real boundary of what is possible.

Distributive and integrative bargaining are not competing philosophies; they are two different questions asked of the same negotiation. Distributive: given a single fixed quantity, how does it split? Integrative: are there other issues in play that the two sides value differently, such that trading across them lets both sides do better simultaneously? A negotiator who only ever asks the first question leaves value on the table that a five-minute conversation about priorities would have surfaced.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Price your BATNA before anything else.** If you don't know what "no deal" is worth in concrete terms, you cannot tell a good deal from a bad one, and you will accept anchors that should have been rejected.
- **Diagnose the other side's likely BATNA, not just your own.** Their walk-away point — not their opening ask — sets the real boundary of what's achievable. Ask what happens to them if this deal falls through.
- **Anchor first, but anchor with a stated reason.** An unjustified extreme anchor invites an equally extreme counter and burns time; an anchor with visible logic behind it shifts the reference point without triggering a standoff.
- **Trade issues, don't just split them.** Before agreeing to split the difference on a single number, ask whether a second issue exists that the two sides value differently — that difference is where integrative value actually comes from.
- **Design the deal before the meeting, not during it.** The highest-leverage negotiating happens in who you bring to the table, what is linked to what, and what the deal is made contingent on — decided in advance, not improvised live.
- **Never let the other side's first offer anchor you silently.** Even an absurd opening number recalibrates your own sense of what's reasonable unless you consciously counter it with a prepared number of your own.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Negotiating without a priced BATNA.** Accepting or rejecting a deal on a gut feeling about fairness instead of comparing it to a real number for the walk-away alternative.
- **Treating every negotiation as purely distributive.** Assuming the pie is fixed and haggling over one number when a second issue, valued differently by each side, would have let both do better at once.
- **Confusing the opening anchor with the target.** Letting the other side's first number pull the whole negotiation toward their reference point instead of resetting it with an anchor of your own.
- **Winning the encounter, losing the relationship.** Extracting maximum value from a single deal at the cost of a counterpart who won't deal with you again, or who quietly claws it back during implementation.
- **Skipping the setup dimension entirely.** Walking into the room without having shaped who is present, what is linked, or what alternatives exist away from the table, then trying to win purely on tactics.

## Worked Example

A mid-size firm's enterprise software renewal comes up for negotiation. The vendor's account team opens at a 22% price increase, citing "market rate adjustments." Before the first call, the procurement lead prices the actual BATNA: switching to a competing product is real but bounded — an estimated four months of migration effort and a temporary productivity dip, not the vendor's implied catastrophe. That number, not a vague sense of leverage, sets the ceiling for what the firm will accept.

Rather than counter on price alone, the team looks for a second dimension. The vendor has a roadmap item — a compliance feature — the firm badly wants and would otherwise wait eighteen months for. The firm, in turn, has excess licensed seats the vendor would value converting into a published customer reference. The final deal: a smaller price increase (9%, not 22%) plus early access to the compliance feature, in exchange for a public case study and a three-year term instead of one. Neither side "won" on the original number — the trade moved value that haggling over price alone would never have surfaced, because no one would have found it without first asking what each side actually needed and why.
