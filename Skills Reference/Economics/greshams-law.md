# Gresham's Law

**Category:** [Economics](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

Gresham's Law, originally from monetary economics: "bad money drives out good money." When two forms of currency are treated as equivalent by law (legal tender) but one is intrinsically more valuable (gold vs. debased coins), people spend the debased coins and hoard the good ones. The good money disappears from circulation. The generalized principle: when a high-quality option and a low-quality option are forced to compete at the same price or in the same pool, the low-quality option tends to dominate because the incentive to preserve the high-quality option is stronger than the incentive to offer it.

---

## Mental Model

Gresham's Law operates anywhere quality differences are hidden and pricing is undifferentiated. The mechanism: actors who hold the higher-quality resource prefer to retain it (or sell it in markets where quality is rewarded), leaving only lower-quality resources in the undifferentiated pool. This dynamic appears in information markets, talent markets, health systems, and organizational communication.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **When you see quality degrading in a pool, ask who is selecting out.** The best employees, the most useful information, the highest-quality products — if they're being retained or diverted elsewhere, Gresham's Law is operating.
- **Create quality differentiation where it matters.** If high-quality and low-quality contributions receive identical rewards, quality contributions will decline over time. Differentiated pay, feedback, and recognition systems are structural responses to Gresham's Law in talent markets.
- **In information environments, bad information drives out good.** Social media and undifferentiated information feeds where rumor, outrage, and misinformation circulate alongside careful analysis systematically disadvantage the latter. The mechanism: low-quality content is cheaper to produce and faster to consume. Design information environments that reward quality.
- **Watch for Gresham's Law in meetings and group dynamics.** Low-quality, loud voices in an undifferentiated discussion environment drive out high-quality, quieter contributions. Structures that create space for quality input (written pre-reads, structured facilitation, anonymous input channels) are mitigations.
- **In procurement: differentiate on quality metrics, not just price.** Commodity procurement that treats all suppliers as equivalent on price while ignoring quality differentials will systematically select for the lowest-quality suppliers at the stated price point.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Applying the law only to financial markets.** Gresham's Law operates in any pool where quality differences are unpriced. Missing its operation in information, talent, and culture contexts is a significant blind spot.
- **Trying to fix quality problems through appeals.** Asking people to share better information, work harder, or maintain standards without changing the incentive structure that makes Gresham's Law operate. The incentive structure must change.
- **Over-differentiating.** Creating quality tiers so granular that the overhead of the system exceeds the value of the differentiation.

---

## Worked Example

A Slack workspace creates a single all-company channel. Initially, it contains valuable company updates, thoughtful discussions, and useful information. As the company grows, the channel fills with noise: announcements, casual chat, memes, and low-signal messages. The high-value contributors — who are selective about where they invest communication time — stop posting to the all-company channel. The low-value contributors continue because the signal-to-noise ratio doesn't bother them (they don't have to read as carefully). The channel becomes progressively noisier. Gresham's Law in information environments: the fix is channel differentiation (separate channels for different purposes, with different norms and moderation standards).
