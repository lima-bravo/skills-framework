# Trade-offs
*Every choice forecloses others*

**Category:** [Economics](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---

## Definition

A trade-off is the relationship between two desirable outcomes that cannot both be maximized simultaneously — gaining more of one requires sacrificing some of the other. Economics is fundamentally the study of trade-offs under scarcity: trade-offs between present and future consumption, between risk and return, between speed and quality, between cost and scope. The practitioner's discipline is making trade-offs explicit rather than pretending they don't exist, and making them deliberately rather than by default.

---

## Mental Model

The production possibility frontier (PPF) is the formal model: a curve showing the maximum achievable combinations of two outputs given fixed resources. Points inside the curve represent inefficiency; points on the curve represent the set of trade-offs available; points beyond the curve are not achievable without expanding resources or improving productivity. The practitioner's insight: most real decisions are about *which point on the frontier* to occupy, not about escaping the frontier itself.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Name the trade-off before making the decision.** "We are choosing to prioritize X over Y" is a better framing than "we have decided to do X." The naming makes the sacrifice visible and deliberate.
- **Ask "compared to what?"** Every choice looks good in isolation; the honest evaluation is relative to the alternatives foregone. This is the concept of opportunity cost.
- **Separate trade-offs that are fixed from those that are not.** "Speed vs. quality" feels like a permanent trade-off but often reflects process or resource constraints that can be removed. Ask: is this trade-off fundamental, or is it an artefact of how we're currently working?
- **Watch for false trade-offs.** "We can either grow fast or maintain culture" often turns out not to be a real trade-off — with the right design, both are achievable. Test whether the trade-off is genuine before accepting it.
- **Communicate trade-offs explicitly to stakeholders.** Decisions that trade one stakeholder's interests against another's create downstream conflict if the trade-off was not named upfront.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Trade-off denial.** Pretending both things can be maximized, then failing to deliver either. "We'll be the fastest and highest quality" without acknowledging the resource constraints that make this claim hollow.
- **Implicit trade-offs made by default.** The organization fails to make a deliberate choice, and the trade-off is made by whoever happens to control the relevant resources — usually in favor of the visible and urgent over the invisible and important.
- **Optimizing for the stated trade-off while violating an unstated one.** Maximizing speed while tacitly deprioritizing employee wellbeing. The unstated trade-off becomes a crisis later.

## Worked Example

A product team has three months before a major launch. They can ship a feature-complete product with known quality risks, or a scoped-down product with high quality confidence. The trade-off: scope vs. quality. Making this explicit allows a constructive decision: they identify which features are genuinely launch-critical, cut the non-critical ones, and ship a smaller but high-quality product. The decision they made was not "feature-complete vs. quality" — it was "which features are in scope?" — a much more tractable question.
