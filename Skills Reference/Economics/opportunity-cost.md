# Opportunity Cost

**Category:** [Economics](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

Opportunity cost is the value of the best forgone alternative when a choice is made — not the cash spent, but what you could have had instead had you chosen differently. It is the foundational concept underneath all trade-off reasoning: every use of a scarce resource (time, money, attention, capital) has a cost measured not in what was paid, but in what the next-best alternative use of that same resource would have returned. A decision can carry zero cash outlay and still have an enormous opportunity cost, and a decision can carry a large cash outlay and a trivial opportunity cost, if the alternative uses of that capital were poor.

---

## Mental Model

An experienced practitioner asks not "what does this cost?" but "what does this cost me, relative to the best thing I could do instead?" This reframes decisions that look costless — staying in a job, keeping an underperforming product line, holding cash — as expensive once the foregone alternative is named specifically, and reframes decisions that look expensive — paying for expert help, buying instead of building — as cheap once compared honestly to what the resource would otherwise have produced.

The discipline has one non-negotiable step: name the specific alternative, not a vague "something better." "I could be doing something else with this time" is not opportunity cost reasoning; "I could be closing the two enterprise deals currently stalled while I do this instead" is. Without a named, concrete alternative, opportunity cost collapses into an unfalsifiable feeling of missed potential — which is rhetoric, not analysis, and can be used to justify almost any decision or its opposite.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Name the specific next-best alternative before evaluating any option.** Not "something else" — the actual, concrete alternative use of the same time, money, or capital, specific enough that someone could disagree with it.
- **Compare against the best forgone alternative, not the worst.** Overstating opportunity cost by comparing against a fantasy option is as distorting as ignoring opportunity cost entirely.
- **Separate cash cost from opportunity cost explicitly in any evaluation.** A decision with low cash outlay and high opportunity cost, or the reverse, is common — collapsing the two into one number hides which one is actually driving the answer.
- **Watch for opportunity cost in "free" or "already-paid-for" resources.** Existing staff time, existing infrastructure, and "spare" capacity are not free just because no new invoice arrives — they have a real opportunity cost in what they could otherwise produce.
- **Recompute periodically.** The best forgone alternative changes as circumstances change; a decision's opportunity cost from a year ago is not this year's opportunity cost.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Treating "no cash outlay" as "no cost."** Using existing headcount or "spare" capacity as if it were free, when it has a real opportunity cost in what those resources could otherwise have produced.
- **Comparing against an unrealistic alternative.** Inflating the imagined foregone option, then using that inflated opportunity cost to justify a decision it doesn't actually support.
- **Confusing opportunity cost with sunk cost.** Sunk cost is money already spent, irrelevant to a forward decision; opportunity cost is the value of the next-best forward option. Conflating them produces both underinvestment (afraid to spend more) and overinvestment (protecting past spend).
- **Never naming a specific alternative.** Treating "opportunity cost" as a generic caution rather than a comparison to a specific, real option — the reasoning becomes rhetorical instead of analytical.

---

## Worked Example

A senior engineer is offered the chance to lead a high-visibility internal platform rebuild that will take roughly six months. The project has no direct revenue attached and comes with no extra budget or headcount, so leadership frames it as "no cost" beyond the engineer's existing salary.

The opportunity cost analysis says otherwise: during those six months, the engineer's next-best alternative use of their time was leading feature work on the product line generating the majority of the company's revenue growth — work projected, based on the current pipeline and the engineer's demonstrated close rate over the past year, to add roughly $2M in expansion revenue. The platform rebuild may be valuable. But "no cost" is wrong — the real cost is the $2M in expansion revenue not captured elsewhere, unless the rebuild is judged worth more than that specific, named alternative. Naming the number changes the conversation from "is this a good idea" to "is this better than the $2M we're giving up" — a question leadership had never actually been asked.
