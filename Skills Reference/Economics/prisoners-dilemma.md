# Prisoner's Dilemma
*Individually rational choices produce collectively irrational outcomes*

**Category:** [Economics](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---

## Definition

The Prisoner's Dilemma is a game theory scenario in which two players each independently choose to cooperate or defect. Each player receives a better individual outcome by defecting regardless of what the other player does — yet if both defect, both are worse off than if both had cooperated. The dilemma: the dominant strategy produces a worse equilibrium than the dominated strategy. It is the most important model for understanding why cooperation fails even among rational actors who would benefit from it.

---

## Mental Model

The structure shows up everywhere: price competition between firms, arms races between nations, quality-cutting between suppliers, and free-riding in teams. The diagnosis is always the same — each player is doing the individually rational thing, yet the outcome is collectively worse than the alternative. Understanding this matters for two reasons. First, it explains failure modes that look like bad faith or stupidity but are actually rational responses to the incentive structure. Second, it clarifies what actually needs to change: not the players' intentions or intelligence, but the payoff structure itself.

In a one-shot game the logic is inescapable — defect. But real situations are usually repeated games, and here the calculus changes dramatically. When players expect to interact again, defection invites retaliation and destroys future cooperation. The most successful strategy in repeated Prisoner's Dilemmas is Tit-for-Tat: cooperate first, then mirror what the other player did last round. It is forgiving, retaliatory, and clear — and it outperforms more sophisticated strategies in practice.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Diagnose the payoff structure before assuming bad faith.** When a counterpart defects, ask: is this the Nash Equilibrium of the situation, or is it a choice that could have gone the other way? Structural defection looks different from strategic defection.
- **In repeated games, signal clearly and cooperate first.** The best opening move in an ongoing relationship is cooperation — it invites reciprocity. Unclear signals (actions that could be read as either) are particularly dangerous.
- **Make commitments binding to escape the dilemma.** If both players can make credible, verifiable commitments to cooperate, the dilemma disappears. Contracts, transparency mechanisms, and third-party enforcement all serve this function.
- **Change the game when the incentive structure is broken.** If your team or market keeps landing in a defection equilibrium, the answer is redesigning incentives — not repeated appeals to good behaviour.
- **Watch for multi-party dilemmas.** The Prisoner's Dilemma logic extends to groups: one defector benefits at the expense of all cooperators, which incentivises others to defect, which unravels cooperation entirely.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Treating defection as a character flaw.** When a counterpart defects, attributing it to bad faith rather than examining the incentive structure. This produces blame instead of solutions.
- **Cooperating unilaterally in a one-shot game.** When there is no ongoing relationship and no enforcement mechanism, cooperation in a Prisoner's Dilemma is exploitable. Recognising the game structure is not cynicism — it is accuracy.
- **Forgetting that reputation extends across games.** What looks like a one-shot game rarely is. Word travels. An organisation that defects on suppliers in one negotiation may face worse terms in the next, because the game is actually repeated.
- **Over-applying the framework.** Not every cooperative situation is a Prisoner's Dilemma. When defection is not a dominant strategy — when the payoff structure genuinely rewards joint cooperation — the dilemma doesn't apply and different reasoning is needed.

## Worked Example

Two competing tech firms are each investing heavily in proprietary AI infrastructure. If both agreed to use shared open-source infrastructure, both would spend £20m instead of £80m and achieve similar capabilities. But each firm reasons: "If the other firm uses shared infrastructure and we use proprietary, we gain a competitive edge. If the other firm uses proprietary and we use shared, we're disadvantaged. Proprietary is our dominant strategy." Both invest £80m in proprietary systems and end up in the same competitive position they would have achieved for £20m each.

The escape: an industry consortium (commitment mechanism) that makes the cooperative choice binding and verifiable — moving from a one-shot dilemma to a coordinated game with enforceable rules.
