# Value Creation and Capture
*Creating value and keeping some of it are two different problems. Solving one doesn't solve the other.*

**Category:** [Economics](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---

## Definition

Value creation is the act of producing something worth more to a recipient than it costs to produce. Value capture is the act of converting a portion of that created value into revenue or other durable benefit for the producer. The two are analytically distinct: a business can create enormous value while capturing very little of it (many software tools, infrastructure, and platform businesses have this problem), or can capture value without creating much (rent-seeking, regulatory monopoly). Strategy must address both — what value is created, and what determines how much of it can be retained.

The concept originates in economics (producer and consumer surplus) and is central to competitive strategy. The key insight, formalised by Brandenburger and Stuart (1996), is that the amount of value a firm can capture is bounded by its added value — the increment of total value it contributes to the system it participates in. A firm with zero added value (easily replaced) captures zero value in competitive equilibrium regardless of how much it creates.

## Mental Model

Imagine a transaction: a customer pays £100 for something that costs £40 to produce and that the customer would have paid £150 for. Value created: £110 (the gap between willingness to pay and cost of production). Value captured by the producer: £60 (revenue minus cost). Consumer surplus: £50 (what the customer got above what they paid). The split between producer capture and consumer surplus is not fixed — it is determined by competitive dynamics, switching costs, negotiating power, and the availability of alternatives.

The strategic question is always: *what determines the split?* A firm with unique capabilities, strong switching costs, or network effects can shift the split toward itself. A firm that is easily replicated, operates in a commodity market, or faces powerful buyers will see the split shift toward customers (or toward platform owners, distributors, or other intermediaries).

The most common strategic mistake is confusing value creation with value capture. A product that delights customers and generates enormous consumer surplus may still be a poor business if the competitive structure prevents capture. Conversely, a mediocre product in an oligopolistic market may generate exceptional returns for years. Understanding *why* a business captures what it captures — not just *how much* — is the diagnostic question.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Identify where created value actually flows.** In any value chain, ask: who receives the value that this activity creates? The answer is often not the firm doing the creating. A manufacturer in a commodity supply chain may create substantial value that is captured by the retailer or the brand above them.
- **Map your added value, not just your value created.** Added value is what disappears from the system if you are removed. A firm that creates significant value but can be replaced at low cost has low added value and will be competed down to cost of capital. The question is not "are we valuable?" but "are we uniquely valuable?"
- **Assess capture threats before building.** Any time you are about to invest significantly in creating value, ask: who will capture it? Common threats to capture — your customers internalise the capability, a platform takes a growing cut, a competitor copies the approach without the cost structure, or regulators intervene. Identifying capture threats before investment changes what you build.
- **Switching costs and network effects are capture mechanisms.** The most durable forms of value capture come from structural features that make it costly or impossible for customers to shift to alternatives. Evaluate whether the business creates these features or relies on them being absent in competitors.
- **Price to the value created, not to the cost incurred.** Cost-plus pricing signals that you don't understand your value creation. If you create £1,000 of value for a customer at £100 of cost, pricing at £120 is not strategic — it is leaving most of the created value on the table.

## Common Failure Modes

**Building great products in structurally uncapturable markets.** Many technology companies create tremendous user value while failing to build capture mechanisms. "We'll monetise later" assumes a capture mechanism will emerge; it often doesn't. The time to design the capture mechanism is during product design, not after scale.

**Conflating market share with value capture.** A firm can win market share by passing value to customers (low prices, high features) while capturing little of the value created. Scale without capture is a path to a large, unprofitable business.

**Ignoring the intermediary capture problem.** In multi-sided markets and supply chains, intermediaries — platforms, distributors, retailers — often capture the majority of value even when they create a minority of it. A firm that builds for a platform without understanding how the platform's capture dynamics work is building value for someone else.

**Assuming current capture rates are permanent.** Competitive dynamics shift capture rates over time. A firm that captures 40% of the value it creates today may capture 10% in five years if the competitive structure changes. Wardley Mapping and scenario planning are the tools for assessing this trajectory.

## Worked Example

A digital health startup builds a medication adherence tool that measurably reduces hospital readmissions. Clinical evidence shows the tool saves the healthcare system £4,000 per patient per year in avoided readmission costs. The startup prices at £200 per patient per year — capturing 5% of the value it creates.

The capture problem: hospitals receive the readmission cost savings but don't pay for the tool (patients use it themselves). Patients benefit from better health outcomes but don't bear the cost of readmissions. The payer (insurer or NHS) bears the cost of readmissions but has limited contracting relationships with patients. The value is created in one place and the cost savings accrue in another — a capture misalignment.

Strategic options: (1) Sell to payers who bear the readmission cost — direct capture from the party who benefits most financially. (2) Sell to hospital systems under a shared-savings contract — align capture with clinical partnership. (3) Shift the product from B2C to B2B2C, routing through employers who self-insure. Each option restructures *who* captures value and *how* the firm accesses it. The clinical value creation is identical across all three; the capture varies dramatically.

## Connections

- **Unit Economics** — unit economics measures value capture at the customer level; this card explains the structural reasons capture rates are what they are
- **Pricing Logic** — pricing is the primary mechanism for setting the producer's share of created value; value-based pricing is the discipline of pricing to value created rather than cost incurred
- **Wardley Mapping** — evolution along the Wardley axis commoditises components and shifts capture toward those who build on top; mapping reveals where capture is flowing
- **Monopoly vs. Competition** — competitive markets drive capture toward zero; monopoly structures maximise capture; most real businesses operate between these poles
- **Network Effects** — network effects are one of the strongest capture mechanisms: as the network grows, switching costs rise and the share of created value that can be captured increases
- **Disruptive Innovation** — disruption often involves creating a new value creation/capture pairing that incumbent players can't match without destroying their existing capture mechanism
- **Business Model Canvas** — the revenue streams section of the canvas is the capture design; the value proposition section is the creation design; misalignment between the two is the most common business model failure
