# Schelling Points
*Coordination happens around salient focal points, even without communication*

**Category:** [Economics](../index.html#economics) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../index.html)**

---

## Definition

A Schelling Point (also called a focal point) is a solution that people tend to converge on in the absence of communication, simply because it is salient, prominent, or natural-seeming in a given context. Named after economist Thomas Schelling, the concept explains how coordination can occur without explicit agreement: when two parties must coordinate independently, they both choose the option that "stands out" — not because it is objectively best, but because it is the most prominent option in their shared mental landscape.

---

## Mental Model

When you need to meet someone in New York City but can't communicate, where do you go? Most people choose Grand Central Station, at noon. It's not arbitrary — it's the most prominent, obvious meeting point in the shared mental landscape of people who know New York. That prominence is what makes it a Schelling Point.

The deeper insight is that salience is culturally and contextually constructed. What's obvious in one context — noon, Grand Central, 50/50 split — may not be obvious in another. This means Schelling Points can be created deliberately by establishing prominence. Standards, conventions, round numbers, and anchor prices all function as manufactured Schelling Points: they make one option stand out so strongly that coordination coalesces around it even without negotiation.

For practitioners, Schelling Points explain a class of outcomes that looks irrational through a narrow lens but is entirely rational when coordination is the goal. Prices converge on round numbers, contracts converge on standard terms, and technology ecosystems converge on specific implementations — not because these are optimal but because they are salient enough to coordinate on.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Identify the coordination problem before proposing solutions.** When stakeholders seem to be stuck between several roughly-equivalent options, the problem is often coordination, not evaluation. Introducing a Schelling Point (a prominent anchor) often breaks the deadlock faster than further analysis.
- **Manufacture salience deliberately.** To establish a standard, convention, or norm, make one option prominent before the decision is made. First-mover advantage in standards battles is partly Schelling Point advantage: whoever defines what's "obvious" wins coordination.
- **Use round numbers, prominent precedents, and named standards.** These are reliable Schelling Points in pricing, negotiation, and policy. A price of £1,000 coordinates differently than £987, even if the difference is economically trivial.
- **Recognise when you're inside a Schelling Point dynamic.** When a negotiation converges on a round number or a "50/50 split," check whether that outcome is actually fair or whether it's just salient. Salience and fairness are not the same thing.
- **Exploit shared context to predict coordination.** In competitive intelligence, ask: "What is the obvious move for our competitor given what's salient in their context?" Schelling Point reasoning often predicts competitor behaviour better than rational-actor analysis.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Confusing salience with optimality.** The fact that everyone coordinates on an option doesn't make it the best option. QWERTY is a Schelling Point, not an optimal keyboard layout. Standards that emerge from Schelling dynamics often persist long past their usefulness.
- **Underestimating context-dependence.** What is salient to one group may not be salient to another. Assuming a shared Schelling Point when one doesn't exist leads to coordination failure despite everyone acting "rationally."
- **Ceding the salience-setting role.** In standards battles, market entries, and negotiation framing, whoever establishes what's "obvious" has disproportionate influence. Passively accepting another party's Schelling Point is a strategic concession.
- **Anchoring negotiations on the wrong focal point.** Opening a negotiation with a number that becomes the Schelling Point — without realising it — shapes the outcome before the negotiation begins. Anchors work partly through Schelling dynamics.

## Worked Example

Two companies are negotiating a revenue share for a co-developed product. Neither has a strong prior position; the economic analysis supports anything from 55/45 to 65/35 in favour of the larger company. The negotiation stalls.

The smaller company's advisor suggests anchoring on 60/40 — not because it's the most defensible split economically, but because it's the most prominent round-number split between 50/50 and 70/30. They introduce it early: "We'd suggest starting from a 60/40 framework, which reflects your larger distribution footprint." The larger company counter-proposes 65/35. The negotiation settles at 62/38 — close to the Schelling Point the smaller company introduced, rather than the economically unconstrained outcome that might have favoured the larger party more.

## Connections

→ [**Nash Equilibrium**](../Economics/nash-equilibrium.md) — when multiple Nash Equilibria exist, Schelling Points explain which equilibrium players coordinate on; salience selects among equivalent stable states.
→ [**Anchoring**](../Psychology/anchoring.md) — anchoring is the psychological mechanism underlying Schelling Points in negotiation: the first prominent number shapes all subsequent evaluation.
→ [**Framing**](../Art/framing.md) — framing creates salience; whoever controls the frame establishes the Schelling Points around which a conversation coordinates.
→ [**Network Effects**](../Network-Effects/network-effects.md) — technology standards are Schelling Points with network effects: once enough users coordinate on one option, switching cost makes it permanent.
→ [**Path Dependence**](../Systems/path-dependence.md) — Schelling Points often create path dependence: coordination around a focal point accumulates switching costs that lock in the initially arbitrary choice.
→ [**Prisoner's Dilemma**](../Economics/prisoners-dilemma.md) — in repeated games, Schelling Points help players coordinate on cooperative equilibria without explicit communication.
→ [**Overton Window**](../General-Thinking/overton-window.md) — what's inside the Overton Window is partly determined by which positions are salient enough to be thinkable; Schelling Points and the Overton Window both operate through prominence.
