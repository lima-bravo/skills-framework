# Requisite Variety

**Category:** [Systems](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

Ashby's Law of Requisite Variety (W. Ross Ashby, *An Introduction to Cybernetics*, 1956) states that *only variety can absorb variety*: to regulate a system, the controller must command at least as many distinct responses as the system can present distinct disturbances. Stafford Beer made it the cornerstone of organisational cybernetics, calling it "the most fundamental law" of how organisations stay viable. Stripped of the control-theory machinery, it is a blunt practitioner test: any mechanism meant to handle a varied environment must itself wield a matching range of responses. Where it cannot, the excess variety leaks through — as exceptions, escalations, edge cases, and failures — and the only honest options are to grow your own variety (amplify) or shrink the variety you face (attenuate).

---

## Mental Model

Picture the regulator and the regulated in a variety contest. If the world can do more genuinely distinct things than your responses can handle, the surplus does not disappear — it surfaces as the cases your rule, script, or process "didn't anticipate." There are exactly three moves, and only two of them work. You can **amplify** your variety: more options, more judgment, more autonomy at the edge, adaptive rather than fixed responses. You can **attenuate** the incoming variety: standardise, constrain the environment, reduce the inputs, simplify the problem at its source. Or you can do neither and simply **absorb the cost** of the mismatch — the failures you never close. Most chronic organisational dysfunction is an unacknowledged variety mismatch: a rulebook that cannot represent the situations staff face, a support script that cannot answer the real questions, a single decision-maker who is the bottleneck for too many distinct calls. The practitioner's question is not "how do we try harder?" but "does the variety of my responses match the variety of what I'm regulating — and if not, do I amplify or attenuate?"

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Count the variety on both sides.** Roughly: how many genuinely distinct situations can arise, versus how many distinct responses can your mechanism actually produce? The gap is your failure surface, and naming it beats guessing.
- **Remember there are only two real levers.** When you are overwhelmed, you can amplify your variety or attenuate theirs. Reach for them deliberately instead of working the same mismatched repertoire harder.
- **Push variety to where it lives.** Beer's rule: give the people closest to the variety the autonomy and repertoire to absorb it locally, rather than routing every exception up to a central rule that cannot hold them all.
- **Treat standardisation as attenuation.** It is the right move when the incoming variety is genuinely low or mostly noise, and the wrong move when it discards signal. Always know which you are doing.
- **Read recurring "edge cases" as a diagnosis.** A control system that keeps failing on edge cases is not failing on edge cases — it is under-varietied for its actual environment. The edge cases *are* the environment.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Mistaking a variety shortfall for an effort or discipline problem.** Telling people to "follow the process better" when the process structurally cannot represent the cases they meet. No amount of compliance closes a variety gap.
- **Over-attenuation.** Forcing a varied reality through a model so simple it discards the distinctions that matter — buying tidiness at the cost of the signal the system needed to act on.
- **Centralising variety.** Routing all the diverse decisions to one person, committee, or rule that cannot possibly hold the requisite variety, producing a bottleneck and a growing backlog of unhandled exceptions.
- **Adding variety where attenuation was needed.** Piling on options, configurations, and special cases when the right move was to constrain the environment. Variety for its own sake is cost, not capability.

## Worked Example

A customer-support organisation runs on a rigid decision-tree script. Resolution rates look fine on common issues but collapse on anything unusual; escalations balloon and customers with non-standard problems churn. The instinct is to coach agents to follow the script more closely. The requisite-variety diagnosis is different: the environment — the full range of customer problems — has far more variety than a fixed branching script can represent, so the surplus leaks through as escalations. There are two legitimate fixes, and they are not "try harder." **Amplify** the agents' variety: give experienced agents the authority and training to handle off-script cases, pushing the response repertoire to where the variety actually arises. **Attenuate** the incoming variety at its source: fix the product defects and the unclear billing that generate the unusual cases in the first place, shrinking the environment's variety before it ever reaches the script. Working the agents harder on the same script cannot close a gap that is structural — the script and the world have different amounts of variety, and only changing one of those two numbers helps.
