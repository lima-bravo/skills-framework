# System Archetypes

**Category:** [Systems](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

System archetypes are a small set of recurring causal structures — specific configurations of reinforcing loops, balancing loops, and delays — that produce the same characteristic behaviour wherever they appear, regardless of domain. Popularised by Peter Senge in *The Fifth Discipline* and catalogued by Donella Meadows as "system traps," they are the systems-thinker's pattern library: a manufacturing backlog, a public-health crisis, and a stalling product can share one underlying structure and therefore one underlying cure. The value is diagnostic economy — once you recognise the archetype, you inherit decades of accumulated knowledge about where its leverage point sits and which "obvious" intervention will backfire.

---

## Mental Model

Behaviour follows structure, not domain. The same handful of loop configurations recur everywhere because there are only so many ways a stock, a flow, a feedback loop, and a delay can be wired together. The practitioner learns to read past the surface story — "sales are down," "the site keeps crashing," "the team is burning out" — to the structure underneath, and to ask: *which archetype is this?* Each carries a signature symptom, a counter-intuitive trap, and a known leverage point:

- **Limits to Growth** — a reinforcing engine runs into a balancing constraint and growth stalls. Trap: push the engine harder. Leverage: relieve the constraint. *(S-Curves, Diminishing Returns, Bottlenecks.)*
- **Shifting the Burden / Fixes That Fail** — a symptomatic fix relieves pressure but erodes the fundamental capacity, deepening dependence. Leverage: protect the fundamental fix while the symptomatic one buys time. *(Fixes That Fail.)*
- **Eroding Goals** — under pressure the target is quietly lowered rather than the gap closed, and standards decay. *(Drift to Failure.)*
- **Escalation** — each party responds to the other's gains, producing an arms race neither wants. Leverage: change the game, not the move. *(Red Queen, Prisoner's Dilemma.)*
- **Success to the Successful** — early winners receive more resources, compounding their lead independent of merit. *(Power Law, Path Dependence.)*
- **Tragedy of the Commons** — individually rational use degrades a shared resource. *(Tragedy of the Commons, Free Rider.)*
- **Growth and Underinvestment** — capacity is never built ahead of demand, so quality erodes exactly when growth needs it. *(Organizational Slack.)*

The recognition itself is the deliverable: name the archetype, and the leverage point usually names itself.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Read the loops before the story.** Sketch the reinforcing loops, balancing loops, and delays before reaching for an archetype name. The structure tells you the pattern; the narrative often misleads.
- **Match the intervention to the archetype, not to the symptom.** Each archetype has a known high-leverage move and a known trap. "Push harder" is right for some structures and catastrophic for others — it deepens a Limits to Growth and feeds a Shifting the Burden.
- **Suspect Shifting the Burden whenever a fix works fast and the problem returns bigger.** The quick fix is usually eroding the capability that would have solved it permanently.
- **Account for delays — they hide the archetype.** Most archetypes only become legible once you include the lag between action and consequence; without it, the structure looks like bad luck or bad people.
- **Use the library to transfer solutions across domains.** Because behaviour follows structure, a remedy that worked on one instance of an archetype often transfers to an unrelated domain with the same structure.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Labelling instead of intervening.** Naming the archetype ("ah, classic Fixes That Fail") and stopping there. The label is worthless unless it changes where you push — the same trap the Cynefin card warns against.
- **Forcing the pattern.** Pattern-matching is seductive; not every problem is an archetype, and jamming a situation into the nearest one distorts the diagnosis. The loops have to actually be present.
- **Treating the archetype as the actor's fault.** Archetypes are structural — the behaviour would emerge from anyone placed in that loop configuration. Blaming the person guarantees you miss the leverage point.
- **Single-archetype tunnel vision.** Real situations often layer two or three archetypes; resolving one while another dominates produces partial, short-lived relief.

## Worked Example

A scaling company's platform team is in chronic firefighting: incidents climb, the team works nights, and leadership keeps approving contractor augmentation to clear the backlog. Read as a resourcing problem, the fix is obvious — more contractors. Read structurally, it is **Shifting the Burden**: the contractor fix relieves the symptom (backlog) while the fundamental capacity — a maintainable platform and an internal team that understands it — quietly atrophies, since each contractor cycle leaves less institutional knowledge and raises the next cycle's contractor need. The reinforcing loop is dependence, not demand. Naming the archetype redirects the intervention: deliberately cap the symptomatic fix and route the freed pressure into the fundamental one — platform refactoring and internal capability — accepting that it gets worse before it gets better. The leverage point was invisible until the structure was named, which is the whole reason for carrying the pattern library.
