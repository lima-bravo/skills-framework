# Catalysts
*Lower the barrier without being consumed*

**Category:** [Physics & Chemistry](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---

## Definition

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being consumed in the process — it lowers the activation energy barrier and then is available to facilitate the next reaction. The catalyst is reusable; it doesn't burn off. In organizations and systems, a catalyst is any intervention, person, tool, or process that enables valuable activity to happen faster or more easily without being depleted in doing so.

---

## Mental Model

The most valuable organizational investments are often catalytic rather than productive: a platform that makes every engineer more productive (catalyst) rather than a feature that directly serves customers (consumed resource); a manager who unlocks ten engineers' output (catalyst) rather than one who works harder themselves (consumed resource); a process that enables faster decisions (catalyst) rather than a person making decisions themselves (consumed). The catalyst multiplies; the direct input adds.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Distinguish catalytic investments from direct investments.** Both are valuable, but catalytic investments produce compounding returns while direct investments produce linear ones. Allocate according to the stage and nature of the problem.
- **Identify the rate-limiting step and ask: what would catalyse it?** The highest-value catalysts reduce the barrier on the bottleneck activity.
- **People can be organizational catalysts.** Certain individuals function as connectors, enablers, and accelerators — they make others more effective without themselves being the direct output generator. Recognize and protect catalytic people.
- **Design catalysts to be self-sustaining.** A catalyst that requires constant reapplication (a consultant who must be re-engaged repeatedly) is not truly catalytic — it's a recurring input. True catalysts leave behind changed capability.
- **Catalysts can also inhibit.** In chemistry, inhibitors reduce reaction rates. In organizations, management layers, bureaucracy, and low-trust cultures function as inhibitors. Identifying and removing inhibitors is the mirror image of identifying catalysts.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Consuming the catalyst.** Using a key enabling person, process, or tool for direct output production rather than enabling others — converting a catalytic asset into a consumed one.
- **Investing in catalysts before there's enough substrate.** Catalysts accelerate reactions that would happen anyway; in a very small organization with no established capability in an area, a catalyst has nothing to accelerate. Direct capability must exist first.
- **Mistaking activity for catalytic effect.** A busy connector who facilitates many conversations may or may not be producing net catalytic output. Measure outcomes enabled, not activity generated.

---

## Worked Example

A startup builds an internal developer platform: a standardized toolchain, deployment pipeline, and infrastructure templates that any engineer can use to ship a service in hours rather than days. The platform team of four engineers is not shipping customer features — they are building a catalyst. Within six months, the 20 product engineers are shipping at twice their prior velocity. The four-person catalyst team has multiplied the output of the 20-person substrate by a factor of two. This investment would look expensive in the short term (four engineers not shipping features) and produces enormous returns over any reasonable time horizon.
