# Leverage
*Multiply force through mechanism*

**Category:** [Physics & Chemistry](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---

## Definition

Leverage is the use of a mechanism to multiply input force into greater output force. In physics, a lever allows a small force applied over a large distance to move a large load over a small distance — mechanical advantage. In finance, leverage means using borrowed capital to amplify returns (and losses). In business and life, leverage is any mechanism that produces a disproportionate output relative to input: code that automates a task (one-time effort, indefinite returns), a persuasive argument (one conversation changes many outcomes), or a decision that shapes thousands of subsequent decisions.

---

## Mental Model

Every action has a leverage multiple: the ratio of output produced to input consumed. A one-to-one multiple (an hour of work producing one hour's output) is no leverage. A hundred-to-one multiple (one hour of work producing a hundred hours' equivalent output) is high leverage. The practitioner asks: *"Where are the highest leverage points in the system? What is my leverage multiple here compared to alternatives?"* Time is finite; leverage determines how much impact you can produce with it.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Identify the decision or action that would most change outcomes across the most downstream work.** These are the high-leverage interventions. Hiring the right founding team, choosing the right market, designing the right architecture — these decisions are highly leveraged because everything else builds on them.
- **Prefer leverage over throughput.** Doing more is low leverage; building systems, tools, processes, and relationships that multiply others' output is high leverage. Senior practitioner time is most valuable when spent increasing team leverage.
- **Writing is high leverage.** A well-written document — a strategy, a process, a specification — can shape the thinking and decisions of hundreds of people indefinitely. Writing scales in a way that verbal communication cannot.
- **Code is the highest leverage tool available in knowledge work.** Automating a recurring human decision or process produces an asset that runs indefinitely. Identify which of your team's recurring tasks are automation candidates.
- **Watch for negative leverage.** Management overhead, bureaucratic process, and coordination cost all consume input while producing less than one-to-one output. These are negative leverage points that should be minimized.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Confusing busyness with leverage.** High throughput (doing lots of things) feels like high leverage but often isn't. The question is not "how much am I doing?" but "what is the output per unit of input?"
- **Financial leverage without downside management.** Borrowed capital amplifies both gains and losses. Leverage without margin of safety (the ability to survive the adverse scenario) is the mechanism of financial ruin.
- **Investing in building leverage before validating the use case.** Automating a process that should be eliminated; building a platform for a product that hasn't found market fit. Build leverage on proven needs.

---

## Worked Example

A founder spends the first year of her company answering customer support personally. This produces great NPS and deep product insight. It is low leverage: she can handle 15 conversations per day; the company has 15 responses per day to customer problems. She writes a comprehensive customer support handbook based on the 200 most common questions, trains two support staff on it, and builds a self-serve knowledge base. Now 150 responses per day are handled by two people using the handbook, and she handles only genuinely novel issues. Her input (writing the handbook) produced continuing high-leverage output. The leverage multiple on the handbook investment is 10x and growing.
