# Double-Loop Learning

**Category:** [Learning](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

Double-loop learning, a concept from Chris Argyris and Donald Schön, distinguishes two fundamentally different responses to an error or a gap between intended and actual results. Single-loop learning detects the error and corrects the action while leaving the underlying governing variables — the goals, values, strategies, and assumptions that produced the plan in the first place — unquestioned and unchanged. Double-loop learning goes a level further: it treats the error as a signal that the governing variables themselves may be wrong, and revises the goal, strategy, or assumption, not just the action taken in service of it. Most operational improvement disciplines — PDCA, after-action reviews, empirical process control — are excellent single-loop tools: they make the existing frame execute better. None of them, by design, ask whether the frame itself is the actual problem.

---

## Mental Model

An experienced practitioner treats a recurring failure differently from a first-time failure. A single instance of missing a target is normally a single-loop problem: something in execution went wrong, and correcting the action — more resources, a tighter process, a clearer instruction — is the right response. But when the same category of failure recurs despite repeated, competent single-loop correction, that repetition is itself diagnostic: it signals that the governing assumption generating the plan is wrong, and no amount of better execution within that assumption will fix it.

The practitioner's discipline is to ask, explicitly, a question single-loop correction never asks: what would have to be true about our goals, our model of the situation, or our strategy for this same failure to keep happening even though we keep fixing the visible symptom? Answering that question honestly usually requires confronting something uncomfortable — a strategy that was a personal or political commitment, a goal that was never actually right, an assumption about customers or the market that has quietly stopped being true — which is precisely why double-loop learning is rare in practice even though it's conceptually simple: organisations and individuals have strong, often unconscious incentives to keep the governing variables unquestioned.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **When a failure recurs despite a prior fix, ask whether the fix addressed the action or the assumption.** A third occurrence of the "same" problem after two rounds of competent single-loop correction is a strong signal the governing variable, not the action, needs revision.
- **Name the governing variables explicitly before defending the plan.** What goal, strategy, or assumption is this plan actually built on — and is anyone allowed to question it, or has it quietly become untouchable?
- **Distinguish "we executed the strategy poorly" from "the strategy was wrong."** The first calls for single-loop correction; the second calls for double-loop revision, and treating the second as if it were the first produces an organisation that gets more efficient at doing the wrong thing.
- **Watch for defensive routines that protect governing variables from scrutiny.** Blaming individuals, adding process, or reframing a strategic failure as an execution failure are all ways organisations avoid the more uncomfortable double-loop question.
- **Build in a deliberate trigger for double-loop review.** Because it doesn't happen by default, name in advance the condition that forces it — a strategy that fails its stated goal after two single-loop correction cycles automatically escalates to a review of the goal itself, not a third attempt at the same fix.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Treating every failure as single-loop.** Responding to a recurring, structural failure with the same category of fix that already failed twice — more training, tighter process, better tools — when the actual defect is in the goal or assumption the process serves.
- **Protecting governing variables from examination.** Making it organisationally unsafe to question the strategy, the goal, or the founding assumption, so that only single-loop corrections — which don't threaten anyone's prior commitments — are ever proposed.
- **Confusing intensity of effort with the right loop.** Working harder within a wrong strategy, mistaking the resulting exhaustion for evidence the problem is being taken seriously, when the fix required stepping back, not pushing harder.
- **Double-looping on stable, working assumptions.** The opposite failure — treating every setback as proof the whole strategy is wrong, when a single, ordinary execution failure only needed single-loop correction.

## Worked Example

A software company's sales team consistently misses quota, quarter after quarter. Each quarter, leadership responds with single-loop fixes: more sales training, a new CRM tool, revised call scripts, an adjusted commission structure. Each fix produces a small, temporary uptick, followed by a return to the same shortfall — repeated correction with no durable improvement.

A double-loop review asks a different question: what assumption is the sales strategy built on, and is it still true? The strategy assumes the product's main competitive advantage is feature breadth, and the sales process is built entirely around demonstrating features. Customer interviews reveal the market has shifted: buyers now assume feature parity across vendors and are actually deciding based on implementation speed and support quality, which the sales process barely mentions. The governing assumption — "we win on features" — was the actual defect, not the training, the tool, or the script. No amount of single-loop improvement to a features-first sales process was ever going to fix a strategy built on an assumption the market had already outgrown.
