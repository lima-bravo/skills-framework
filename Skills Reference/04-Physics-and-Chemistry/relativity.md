# Relativity
*Measurement depends on the reference frame*

**Category:** [Physics & Chemistry](../index.html#physics-chemistry) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../index.html)**

---

## Definition

In physics, relativity describes how measurements of space, time, and motion depend on the reference frame of the observer — there is no absolute, observer-independent measurement of many physical quantities. The practical generalization: most judgments, evaluations, and perceptions are relative to a reference point, standard, or comparison set — not absolute. "Good performance," "high price," "fair offer," and "big company" are all relative assessments that change when the reference frame changes.

---

## Mental Model

Every evaluation implicitly contains a question: "compared to what?" The practitioner's discipline is to make the comparison set explicit and to ask whether it is the right comparison. Anchoring to the wrong reference frame — comparing your margins to the wrong industry, your growth rate to the wrong benchmark, your price to the wrong competitor — produces systematically misleading assessments.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Always name the reference frame when making a comparative judgment.** "Our margins are good" — compared to what? Last year? Competitors? The theoretical maximum? Different comparisons produce different conclusions.
- **Control which reference frame your interlocutor uses.** In negotiation, pricing, and persuasion, the reference frame dramatically influences perceived value. A £200 bottle of wine seems expensive next to a £20 bottle; it seems reasonable next to a £500 bottle. Setting the comparison context is a powerful influence tool.
- **Change the reference frame to see a problem differently.** If you are stuck on a problem evaluated against one set of comparisons, deliberately adopt different reference frames. "How would this look to a customer from a different country? A regulator? A first-time user?"
- **Be suspicious of evaluations that don't specify a reference frame.** "We're doing well" with no benchmark is unfalsifiable. "We're in the top quartile of our cohort on gross retention" is a claim you can check.
- **Use relative vs. absolute metrics intentionally.** Relative metrics (market share, NPS vs. competitors, rank in category) tell you about competitive position. Absolute metrics (revenue, absolute NPS) tell you about intrinsic performance. Both are needed; knowing which you're looking at matters.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Reference frame manipulation (anchoring).** The first number in a negotiation strongly influences all subsequent judgments. Being aware of this prevents being anchored by an opening position designed to establish an unfavorable reference frame.
- **Selecting a reference frame to confirm existing preferences.** Choosing benchmarks that make your performance look good, then using those benchmarks as justification for continuing current strategy.
- **Absolute thinking in relative contexts.** Treating a fixed cost as prohibitively expensive when it is cheap relative to the value it enables; treating a metric as bad when it is excellent relative to the base rate.

---

## Worked Example

A startup raises a £3M seed round. The founder announces this to the team as a significant achievement. One team member's reference frame: they came from Google, where individual product budgets were ten times larger. Another's reference frame: they came from bootstrapped companies where £3M is transformative. The same objective fact is experienced very differently. The founder's job: establish the right reference frame ("for a seed round in our sector at this stage, this puts us in a strong position to do X, Y, Z") so the team's energy is channelled correctly rather than distorted by mismatched comparisons.
