# How to Read a Skill Card
*A short guide for someone encountering the framework for the first time*

---

You've been sent a skill card because someone thought it was relevant to something you're working on or thinking about. You don't need to read a whole book or learn a framework. This note tells you how to get value from a single card in about ten minutes.

---

## What a skill card is

A skill card is a distillation of a mental model — a pattern that shows up repeatedly in business, strategy, and organizational life, described in a way that makes it actionable. Each card was developed from source material (books, research, professional practice) and condensed to what's actually useful in the moment.

The card you received isn't arbitrary. Someone noticed a structural parallel between the pattern it describes and the situation you're in.

---

## How to read it

A card has five sections. You don't need to read all five. Here's what each one is for and which to prioritize:

**Definition** — What the concept is and where it comes from. *Read this if the name is unfamiliar.* One to three paragraphs. Skip it if you already know the term.

**Mental Model** — How an experienced practitioner holds this concept in their head. *Read this first.* It's usually one paragraph and contains the card's core insight. If you read nothing else, read this.

**Practitioner Heuristics** — Specific things to do. *Read this second.* These are the actionable rules: "when you see X, do Y." Skim for the one or two that connect most directly to your situation.

**Common Failure Modes** — How smart people misapply this model. *Read this before acting.* Knowing the failure mode is often more valuable than knowing the correct application, because it tells you what to watch out for.

**Worked Example** — A concrete case that illustrates the model in practice. *Read this if the concept still feels abstract.* Look for structural parallels to your situation rather than surface-level similarity — the example is rarely in your exact industry or context.

**Connections** — Links to related mental models in the same framework. *Skip this for now* unless you want to go deeper. These are for practitioners who have internalized the broader deck and want to chain models together.

---

## A suggested ten-minute engagement

1. Read the **Mental Model** paragraph. Ask yourself: what is the core insight in one sentence?
2. Read the **Practitioner Heuristics**. Identify the one heuristic most relevant to your situation.
3. Read the **Common Failure Modes**. Ask: am I already falling into one of these?
4. Apply it: in two or three sentences, write down what this model reveals about your situation that you hadn't already articulated.

That last step is not optional. Reading produces recognition; writing produces understanding. The two sentences don't need to be polished — they just need to be honest.

---

## What to do with it

The goal is not to fit your situation into the model. It's to use the model as a lens that sharpens your view of the situation. If the model doesn't illuminate anything, set it aside — not every model applies to every situation.

If it does illuminate something, the most useful next step is usually to share that insight with someone who's working on the same problem: "I read this card on [concept], and I think what's happening in our situation is actually [specific insight]. Does that match what you're seeing?"

Mental models become useful tools when they connect to real situations. The first connection is the one that counts.
