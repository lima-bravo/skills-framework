# Coaching Stance

**Category:** [Consulting Craft](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

Coaching stance is the deliberate choice of a consultant or advisor to engage with a client primarily through questions, inquiry, and reflection rather than through the delivery of expert answers. It is the alternative to **expert stance** — the default mode in which the consultant's value is defined by what they know and diagnose. Edgar Schein's *Humble Inquiry* (2013) and *Humble Consulting* (2016) articulate why expert stance, while appropriate for technical interventions, systematically fails for complex adaptive problems: the client does not develop the diagnostic capability to address the next problem, the consultant's diagnosis is only as good as the access the client allows, and the relationship remains transactional rather than genuinely collaborative. Peter Block's *Flawless Consulting* (1981/2011) frames the same choice as the difference between a pair-of-hands engagement (client specifies the work), a collaborative engagement (shared problem-solving), and an expert engagement (consultant diagnoses and prescribes). Coaching stance is not a permanent alternative to expert stance — it is the stance that builds lasting client capability.

---

## Mental Model

Two consultants finish the same engagement. The first delivered a 120-slide diagnosis, a 40-page implementation roadmap, and a strong recommendation. The client agreed, paid the invoice, and six months later called back with a similar problem — because the diagnosis and roadmap were the consultant's, not the client's, and the client's ability to diagnose and navigate had not changed. The second consultant spent three of eight weeks asking questions the client had never asked themselves, facilitating sessions where the leadership team built their own diagnosis, and coaching the project sponsor to present the recommendation to the board rather than presenting it herself. The deliverable looked lighter — fewer slides, more conversation notes. But when a similar problem appeared eight months later, the client did not call back. They already had the frame. The first consultant maximised revenue from the engagement; the second maximised the client's capability, which is what the engagement was actually for.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Default to inquiry, not diagnosis.** In the first meeting, ask more questions than you answer. The temptation to demonstrate expertise early ("the pattern I'm seeing is X") forecloses the client's own sense-making before they've engaged it. Their diagnosis, built with your questions, is more durable than your diagnosis delivered to them.
- **Name the stance you're taking.** Clients sometimes experience coaching stance as a lack of expertise ("why aren't you just telling us?"). A brief explicit framing — "I'm going to spend today asking questions, because I want your team's understanding of the problem to develop alongside mine" — prevents the confusion and builds trust in the method.
- **Know when to switch to expert stance.** Coaching stance is not appropriate for every moment. When the client needs a specific technical answer, a risk assessment, or a decision under time pressure, expert stance is correct. The skill is switching deliberately between stances — not adopting one as an identity.
- **Build diagnostic frames, not diagnoses.** Rather than delivering "the problem is X," teach the client the framework for diagnosing X. A question like "how would you distinguish between a structural problem and an execution problem here?" delivers both the immediate frame and the capability to apply it next time.
- **Measure capability transfer, not deliverable count.** At the end of an engagement, ask: can this team diagnose the next version of this problem without us? If the answer is no, the engagement delivered output but not capability. Make capability transfer a named project objective from the kickoff.
- **Watch for dependency as a success signal.** An engagement that leads to an immediate follow-on may reflect strong client satisfaction — or it may reflect that the client was left unable to operate without the consultant's diagnosis. Distinguish the two; the former is partnership, the latter is dependency built on expert stance.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Expert stance as the path of least resistance.** Clients often actively pull for expert answers ("just tell us what to do"). Coaching stance requires the consultant to hold a different frame under social pressure. Without deliberate intent, the engagement defaults to expert stance within the first week.
- **False coaching.** Asking questions while already knowing the answer you intend to lead the client to is not coaching stance — it is expert stance in disguise. Clients detect it quickly, lose trust in the process, and stop contributing genuine thinking.
- **Coaching stance at the wrong moment.** A client in crisis, a board presentation due tomorrow, a regulatory deadline in three days — these are moments for expert stance. Applying coaching stance here is not principled; it is unhelpful.
- **Coaching without contracting.** Using coaching stance without explicitly agreeing with the client that capability transfer is a goal leads to disappointment on both sides. The client expected an answer; they got questions. Contract for the stance before the work begins, not during it.
- **Confusing humility with passivity.** Coaching stance is not the absence of perspective. It involves strong opinions about what questions matter, active facilitation, and deliberate challenge — delivered as questions and reflection, not as prescriptions.

## Worked Example

A consulting team is brought in to redesign a large organisation's operating model. In week one, the lead partner identifies the core problem — misaligned incentives between regional and central functions — and prepares a diagnosis slide. Before presenting it, the lead partner instead runs a two-hour structured session with the executive team using the Three-Question Ritual: "What is actually happening? Why does this pattern persist? What would need to be different for this to change?" The executives' own answers surface the same incentive misalignment — but formulated in their own language, anchored to their own examples. The diagnosis slide is retired. The leadership team now owns the diagnosis; the consulting team's role shifts to providing frameworks and options for addressing it, not to delivering the answer. When the engagement closes six months later, the client runs the next phase of the redesign internally, using the structured sessions the consulting team introduced. They call back eighteen months later — not because they need more consulting, but because they want to license the facilitation approach for their own internal consulting team.
