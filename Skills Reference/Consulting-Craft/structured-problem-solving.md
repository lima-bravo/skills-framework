# Structured Problem Solving

**Category:** [Consulting Craft](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

Structured problem solving is the integrating posture beneath disciplined consulting work — the operating mode in which five component disciplines are practiced *together*, as a single coherent way of working, rather than as separate techniques deployed in isolation. The five components are: **answer-first** communication (lead with the recommendation, then the support), **hypothesis-led** framing (treat the work as a testable bet, not an open-ended search), **MECE structure** (decompose problems exhaustively and without overlap before analyzing them), **80/20 focus** (find the vital few drivers before exhaustively examining the trivial many), and **decision-orientation** (every output should exist to enable a specific choice someone is about to make). Individually, each is a recognizable mental model. Combined and practiced as a single posture, they become something more than the sum: a default mode of engaging with ambiguity that consistently produces clearer thinking, faster convergence, and outputs that survive contact with a skeptical room.

---

## Mental Model

Most of the individual components of this posture are not exotic. Most experienced operators have heard of leading with the conclusion, hypothesizing before gathering data, structuring problems exhaustively, and focusing on what matters most. What separates someone who has *heard of* these ideas from someone who *works this way* is not knowledge of the parts — it is the habit of reaching for all five, together, by default, the moment a messy problem appears.

Consider what happens without the integration. A team that is hypothesis-led but not MECE produces a sharp, testable guess that turns out to rest on an incomplete decomposition — they tested the wrong sub-claims because they never mapped the full space of possible explanations. A team that is MECE but not 80/20-focused produces an exhaustive, perfectly logical issue tree and then spends six weeks examining all of it with equal care, when three of the eleven branches accounted for nearly everything that mattered. A team that does excellent analysis but presents it chronologically — methodology, then findings, then implications, then (eventually, on slide forty) the recommendation — loses the room's attention precisely at the moment the room most needed to engage. Each of these is a team that has one or two of the five components and is missing the others; the work is competent and the outcome disappoints anyway, because the components are interdependent. MECE structure is what makes a hypothesis testable rather than just plausible. 80/20 focus is what keeps hypothesis-led work from boiling the ocean even while *appearing* structured. Answer-first communication is what makes decision-orientation legible to the person who has to act on it. Pull any one thread and the fabric of the other four loosens.

The posture, properly internalized, changes the *first move* a practitioner makes when facing a new, ambiguous problem. Instead of "let me go gather information," the move becomes: "what decision is this for, what would I guess the answer is if forced to commit today, how would I break that guess into testable pieces, which two or three of those pieces would move the needle most if I got them right, and how would I lead with the answer once I have it?" That single compound question — asked reflexively, before any analysis begins — is what this card is naming.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Ask "what decision is this for?" before "what's the answer?"** A finding that doesn't connect to a choice someone is about to make is interesting trivia dressed as analysis. The decision-orientation component is the filter that should run *first*, because it determines how much rigor the other four components need to apply — and where.
- **Write the day-one answer, then build the structure to test it — not the other way around.** This is the practical fusion of hypothesis-led framing and MECE structure: the hypothesis gives the decomposition a *direction* (which branches are worth examining first), and the decomposition gives the hypothesis *testability* (specific sub-claims that can be confirmed or falsified, rather than a vague directional guess).
- **Rank the branches before you examine them — by how much they'd matter if you were right about them.** This is where 80/20 focus does its real work: not as a vague injunction to "prioritize," but as the specific discipline of asking, of every branch in an otherwise-complete MECE tree, "if this turned out to be the answer, how much of the gap would it close?" — and spending time in proportion to that answer, not in proportion to how interesting or visible the branch is.
- **Draft the headline before the supporting analysis is finished — and let the analysis change the headline.** Answer-first communication is not "decide the conclusion early and defend it" — that's advocacy wearing structure's clothes. It is "state your best current answer in one sentence, build the analysis to test it, and be willing to rewrite the sentence when the analysis disagrees with it." The discipline is in the willingness to rewrite, not in the early draft.
- **When one component feels like it's slowing you down, check whether you've actually skipped a different one.** A hypothesis that won't resolve is often a sign the decomposition underneath it isn't MECE. An issue tree that keeps growing is often a sign 80/20 focus hasn't been applied — every branch is being treated as equally important because no one has ranked them. The five components catch each other's failure modes; when one stalls, look at its neighbors before assuming the whole posture is wrong for the situation.
- **Notice when the posture doesn't fit — and say so.** Genuinely novel, low-precedent situations (the [Cynefin Framework](../General-Thinking/cynefin-framework.md)'s "complex" domain) resist hypothesis-led framing, because there isn't yet enough pattern to generate a credible day-one guess. In that domain, the right move is small, safe-to-fail probes — not a confident hypothesis dressed up to look like one. A practitioner who has internalized this posture as a *reflex* rather than a *toolkit* will try to force-fit it where it doesn't belong; the mark of real mastery is recognizing the boundary.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Performing the parts without integrating them.** Producing a polished MECE tree, a crisp hypothesis, an answer-first headline, and a decision-framed conclusion — each one individually well-executed, but assembled in sequence rather than woven together, so that (for instance) the hypothesis was never actually tested against the tree, or the headline doesn't match what the 80/20 analysis actually found.
- **Hypothesis as a costume for advocacy.** Writing the day-one answer, discovering contradicting evidence, and quietly shaping the analysis to protect the original guess rather than update it — the posture's most corrosive failure mode, because it looks identical to disciplined work from the outside while being its precise opposite underneath. See [Confirmation Bias](../Psychology/confirmation-bias.md).
- **MECE perfectionism that swallows the 80/20 discipline.** Building an immaculately exhaustive decomposition and then feeling obligated to examine all of it with equal rigor, because "we already did the work to map it out." The map is not the territory worth visiting in full — it is the tool for deciding *which part* of the territory is worth visiting in full.
- **Decision-orientation as an afterthought.** Doing the analysis first and asking "so what should they do with this?" at the end — producing findings shaped by what was interesting to discover rather than by what the decision-maker actually needed to know to choose well.
- **Mistaking confidence for structure.** A confidently delivered, beautifully formatted answer-first headline can mask analysis that skipped the MECE and 80/20 steps entirely. The posture is not a presentation style that can be bolted onto unstructured thinking after the fact — it has to govern the thinking itself, or the polish is theater.

## Worked Example

A retail chain's leadership asks for help deciding whether to close twelve underperforming stores. A team without this posture would gather store-level financials for all 140 locations, build a model, rank the bottom performers, and present a closure list — arriving, six weeks later, at a technically defensible recommendation that the room immediately starts arguing with, because no one agreed in advance about *what "underperforming" should mean* or *what decision the analysis was actually meant to inform*.

A team practicing the integrated posture starts differently. **Decision-orientation first:** the real decision isn't "which twelve stores look worst on a P&L" — it's "where should we stop investing management attention and capital, and where should we double down?" That reframe changes everything downstream. **Hypothesis, stated day one:** "the stores that look worst on raw profitability are mostly stores in markets we're exiting strategically anyway — the closure decision and the market-strategy decision are the same decision wearing two names." **MECE decomposition** built to test that specific claim: segment all 140 stores by (a) raw profitability, (b) strategic market trajectory, (c) real-estate exit cost, and (d) brand-halo effect on nearby locations — four mutually exclusive lenses that together cover every reason a store might be worth keeping or closing. **80/20 focus:** of those four lenses, the team ranks strategic-market-trajectory as the one most likely to overturn the naive "close the worst twelve" answer, and spends disproportionate time there first — finding, in fact, that five of the twelve "worst" stores sit in markets the company is actively expanding into, where short-term losses are an expected cost of building share.

**Answer-first delivery:** the recommendation leads with "close seven stores, not twelve — and treat the other five as a deliberate, time-boxed investment in markets we're committed to, with a name on the calendar for when we'll revisit them." The supporting analysis follows, but the room already has the answer, the logic for the five exceptions, and a date by which the bet will be checked — which is exactly what the actual decision required, and nothing the naive financial ranking would have surfaced.
