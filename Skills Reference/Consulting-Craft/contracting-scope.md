# Contracting & Scope

**Category:** [Consulting Craft](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

Contracting is the structured conversation — and resulting written agreement — that aligns consultant and client on what the engagement is for, what is in and out of scope, what success looks like, how decisions are made, and how the engagement will end. Distinguish from "the contract" (the legal document): contracting is the alignment process; the contract is one artifact of it. Peter Block (*Flawless Consulting*) treats contracting as the most under-invested phase of consulting work — most engagements fail because of bad contracting, not bad delivery. Contracting includes both the formal-business kind (statements of work, scope clauses) and the human-business kind (working agreements, escalation paths, mutual expectations).

---

## Mental Model

A surgeon and patient contract before surgery: what procedure, what risks, what the recovery looks like, who decides if scope changes during the operation. The conversation is uncomfortable but the surgery is impossible without it. Most consulting engagements skip the equivalent: signed SOWs that describe deliverables ("a report and three workshops") without ever agreeing on what success looks like, what the consultant has authority to decide, what the client has authority to decide, or what happens when reality diverges from plan. The omission is what makes engagements feel like a slow-motion misunderstanding.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Contract on outcomes, not deliverables.** "Reduce escalation rate from 14% to 8%" is testable; "deliver a process redesign" is not.
- **Name the decisions the consultant can make.** Decisions you cannot make should be escalation triggers; decisions you can make should be visible.
- **Surface assumptions in writing.** Every engagement runs on a set of implicit beliefs ("we will have access to the right people"). Make them explicit so violations are visible.
- **Re-contract when reality diverges.** Two months in, the world has changed. A 30-minute re-contracting conversation is cheaper than three months of misalignment.
- **Contract the end.** What does "done" look like? Who decides? On what evidence? Without this, engagements drift past their useful life.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **SOW as contract.** Treating the legal document as the entire alignment exercise misses 80% of what contracting is.
- **Outcome avoidance.** Both sides find it easier to agree on deliverables than on outcomes; deliverables ship, outcomes are harder to commit to.
- **Implicit authority.** Consultants assume they can shape decisions; clients assume the consultant will defer. The mismatch is discovered in the middle of a conflict.
- **No re-contracting mechanism.** Implicit "we'll figure it out" replaces a stated cadence for revisiting scope.

---

## Worked Example

A regional health system engages a consultancy to "improve operational efficiency in outpatient services." The SOW lists deliverables. The team insists on a contracting workshop before kickoff. Outputs: a stated outcome ("reduce average patient wait time from 73 minutes to under 45 minutes within nine months"), explicit scope boundaries (no inpatient, no IT systems replacement, focus on the three highest-volume clinics), named decision rights (consultant decides analytical approach; client decides implementation sequence; both jointly approve resource reallocations above $250k), and a re-contracting trigger (formal check at months 3, 6 to update scope). At month 4, the data reveals that 30% of wait-time variance comes from physician scheduling, which was nominally out of scope. The re-contracting conversation expands scope cleanly, with leadership buy-in, rather than scope-creep by stealth. The engagement lands the outcome on schedule.
