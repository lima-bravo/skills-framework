# Stakeholder Pre-Wiring

**Category:** [Consulting Craft](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

Stakeholder pre-wiring is the discipline of engaging the people who must say yes to a recommendation — individually, in advance, and in a sequence designed to surface and resolve resistance — before the formal decision meeting where the recommendation will be presented for approval. It goes beyond mapping who holds influence (the analytical question [Stakeholder Mapping](../Business/stakeholder-mapping-salience-model.md) and [Power Mapping](../Business/power-mapping.md) answer) to the engagement-planning question: what does each of these people need to *believe* before the meeting, where will their resistance come from, and what conversation needs to happen — with whom, in what order, saying what — so that the formal meeting ratifies a decision that has, in substance, already been made.

---

## Mental Model

There is a persistent fantasy in how organizations imagine decisions get made: a recommendation is presented, in the room, to a group of stakeholders who are encountering it for the first time, and — on the strength of the analysis and the case — they are persuaded, on the spot, to say yes to something significant. This essentially never happens, and experienced operators know it. By the time a consequential recommendation reaches a decision meeting, the people in the room have usually already decided, privately, roughly where they stand — and the meeting's real function is to *ratify* a decision, not *make* one. A presenter who treats the meeting as the persuasion event has shown up to the wrong stage of the process.

Pre-wiring is the deliberate, sequenced work of moving people *before* that moment — having the one-on-one conversation where a skeptical stakeholder can voice an objection privately (and have it actually addressed, rather than performed-around in front of peers), where a hesitant ally can be given the specific piece of evidence that would let them advocate with confidence, where a constituency that would otherwise be blindsided in the room gets to feel consulted rather than ambushed. None of this is manipulation in the pejorative sense — it is the same courtesy and care that any well-run relationship runs on, applied deliberately to the moment where a recommendation's fate is actually decided. The alternative — walking into the room "fresh," hoping the logic alone carries the day — is not boldness. It is an unwillingness to do the less visible half of the work.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Map the decision path before mapping the people.** Who has to say yes, in what order, and does a "no" from any one of them kill the decision or just slow it down? The engagement plan should mirror the actual approval structure — not the org chart, which frequently diverges from it.
- **For each stakeholder, ask "what do they need to *believe*?" — not "what do they need to *know*?"** Two people can receive the identical set of facts and arrive at opposite conclusions, because what stands between them and agreement isn't information — it's a prior commitment, a competing priority, or a concern the facts don't speak to. Identify the belief gap specifically, and address *that* — not a generic restatement of the case.
- **Find resistance before the room does.** A one-on-one conversation lets a stakeholder raise an objection in a setting where it can be explored rather than defended against. The same objection raised for the first time in the decision meeting puts both the stakeholder and the presenter in a position where backing down looks like losing — which makes resolution far harder than it needed to be.
- **Sequence the conversations deliberately.** Talk to the stakeholder whose support will make the next conversation easier *before* the one whose skepticism would make it harder. A credible early ally, secured quietly, can change how a later skeptic receives the same recommendation — this is the engagement-planning analogue of [Coalition Building](../Leadership/coalition-building.md).
- **Give resistant stakeholders something to do with their concern.** A stakeholder who voices a real objection and watches it disappear without a trace learns that raising concerns is pointless — and either disengages or waits to relitigate it in the room. A stakeholder whose concern visibly shapes the final recommendation — even in a small way — has a reason to support what emerges, because part of it is now theirs.
- **Prepare the objection before someone else raises it.** The pre-wiring conversations are also a research operation: each one surfaces a version of the resistance the recommendation will face. By the time the formal meeting happens, the objections in the room should be ones the presenter has already heard, already thought through, and already has a considered response to — not ones they're encountering live.

## Common Failure Modes

- **The cold-open pitch.** Walking into the decision meeting with a polished case and no prior conversations — betting that the strength of the logic will overcome positions that, in most cases, were already set before the meeting started.
- **Surprise as strategy.** Deliberately withholding the recommendation from a stakeholder who would object, on the theory that momentum in the room will carry the decision past their resistance. This usually produces the opposite: a stakeholder who feels ambushed digs in publicly, in a setting that makes backing down look like losing face — and the decision stalls in a much worse position than if the conversation had happened privately first.
- **Treating "informing" as "engaging."** Sending a pre-read deck to stakeholders and considering them "looped in." A document that arrives without a conversation gives someone the facts and none of the context for *why this matters to them specifically* — which is precisely the gap that determines whether they show up supportive or skeptical.
- **Performing consultation without practicing it.** Asking for input, ignoring it, and presenting the unchanged recommendation — which stakeholders notice, remember, and price into how seriously they engage with the *next* request for input.
- **Confusing the org chart with the influence map.** Sequencing pre-wire conversations by formal seniority rather than by actual influence on the decision — and discovering, in the room, that the person whose quiet skepticism actually mattered was never in the loop because their title didn't suggest they would be.

## Worked Example

A VP of Product needs the executive team to approve a significant reallocation of engineering capacity away from a feature roadmap that Sales has been promising to customers for two quarters, toward a platform investment with a longer payoff horizon. Walking into the quarterly planning meeting cold would put Sales leadership in the position of objecting, in front of peers, to a plan they're hearing for the first time — a setup that all but guarantees a defensive "no" regardless of the argument's merits.

Instead, the VP runs a two-week pre-wire sequence. First, a private conversation with the CFO — whose support the VP is confident of, given the cost-efficiency argument — secures an explicit, considered ally before anyone else is approached. Second, a conversation with the Head of Sales — not to present the decision as made, but to genuinely surface the concern: which specific customer commitments are at risk, and what would make the trade-off bearable from where Sales sits? The conversation reveals that three accounts are the real pressure point — not the whole roadmap, as the VP had assumed — and that a six-week delay (rather than an indefinite one) on those three would be workable. The recommendation is revised to include a specific commitment on those three accounts, addressing the actual concern rather than a generic version of it.

By the time the planning meeting happens, the CFO is a confirmed advocate, the Head of Sales has had the specific objection heard and partly resolved — and walks in prepared to say so rather than to raise it cold — and the VP has already rehearsed, in two real conversations, the two strongest objections the room is likely to raise. The meeting runs long enough to ratify the decision and short enough that no one leaves feeling it was railroaded through.
