# Situation Assessment

**Category:** [Consulting Craft](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

Situation assessment is the discipline of building a fact-based, MECE current-state baseline — across financial performance, market position, customer momentum, competitive posture, operating health, and organizational constraints — before debating what to do about it. It is the consulting equivalent of a doctor's intake exam: establish what is actually true, separate that from what is merely believed, and only then move to diagnosis and treatment. The output is not a recommendation. It is a fact base, a momentum read, and an issues list precise enough that a recommendation can be built on top of it without re-litigating "what's actually going on."

---

## Mental Model

Most strategy conversations skip this step and pay for it later. A leadership team convenes, someone states the problem ("we're losing share in the mid-market"), and the room moves directly into solutioning — pricing changes, new hires, a repositioning campaign — without ever agreeing on what is actually happening. Three months later, the initiative stalls because half the room never believed the original framing of the problem; they were arguing about the solution because they couldn't yet see a way to argue about the diagnosis.

Situation assessment interrupts this. It forces a separation that most organizations blur under time pressure: **facts** (what the data shows), **interpretations** (what we think the data means), and **open questions** (what we don't yet know but would need to, to be confident). A current-state view that keeps these three categories visibly distinct does something subtle but powerful — it lets people disagree about interpretation without having to first fight about facts, and it makes the genuine unknowns visible instead of quietly papered over with assumptions. This is the foundational move beneath every other consulting skill in this framework: you cannot run a hypothesis-driven engagement, build MECE options, or construct a credible business case on a current-state view that half the room privately doubts.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Separate fact, interpretation, and open question — visibly, in the output.** "Revenue is down 12% year over year" is a fact. "We're losing because of pricing" is an interpretation. "We don't know whether churn or acquisition is driving the decline" is an open question that should be on the page, not buried. A fact base that conflates these three will be read as advocacy the moment someone disagrees with the interpretation.
- **Scan all six lenses before drilling into one.** Financial performance, market position, customer momentum, competitive posture, operating health, and organizational constraints are mutually reinforcing — a financial problem is often a downstream symptom of a customer or operating problem. Drilling into the first plausible lens (usually the financial one, because the data is closest to hand) produces a partial diagnosis that looks complete.
- **Lead with momentum, not snapshot.** A point-in-time number tells you where things are; a trend tells you where they're going and how fast. "Revenue is $40M" is much less useful than "$40M, down from $46M two years ago, with the rate of decline accelerating in the last two quarters." Decision-makers act on trajectory, not position.
- **Find the two or three facts that actually matter for the decision at hand.** A complete fact base is not the goal — a fact base sized to the decision is. If the question is "should we expand into the mid-market," most of the financial fact base is context; the two or three facts about mid-market customer economics and competitive intensity are what the decision turns on. Name them explicitly so they don't get lost in the completeness.
- **End with implications and the next diagnostic question — not a recommendation.** A situation assessment that smuggles in a recommendation ("...and therefore we should...") has skipped a step and will be read, correctly, as advocacy wearing a diagnostic costume. The honest output names what the facts imply is now *open for decision*, and what would need to be true to resolve the open questions — and stops there.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Skipping straight to solutions.** The team that begins with "here's what we should do" has not built a shared view of "here's what's actually happening" — and will spend the rest of the engagement re-litigating the diagnosis disguised as debating the plan.
- **Snapshot bias.** Reporting the current number without the trend manufactures false confidence (if momentum is improving) or false alarm (if a one-quarter dip looks like a structural decline). Either error sends the strategy conversation in the wrong direction.
- **Interpretation creep.** Facts and interpretations blur together until the "fact base" is actually a set of conclusions wearing a data costume — at which point anyone who disagrees with the conclusion has no way to engage except by rejecting the entire document.
- **Single-lens diagnosis.** Treating a multidimensional situation (e.g., "growth has stalled") as if it were single-cause ("it's a sales execution problem") because that lens was the easiest one to examine, not the one the evidence actually pointed to.
- **The bottomless fact base.** Gathering everything because nothing has been ruled in or out yet — the boil-the-ocean failure mode that [Hypothesis-Driven Consulting](hypothesis-driven.md) exists specifically to prevent. A situation assessment without a decision in view will expand to fill all available time.

## Worked Example

A mid-sized B2B software company asks for help: "growth has stalled and we need a new go-to-market strategy." The team's instinct is to jump to GTM options. Instead, they spend the first week building a situation assessment across the six lenses.

**Financial performance:** revenue growth has fallen from 34% to 6% over six quarters — a momentum signal, not a snapshot fact. **Market position:** the addressable market is still expanding at ~18% annually — so the slowdown is not a market-wide phenomenon. **Customer momentum:** new-logo acquisition is flat, but net revenue retention has dropped from 118% to 96% — existing customers are churning or contracting. **Competitive posture:** two new entrants have launched in the last 18 months, both targeting the company's core mid-market segment with a lower-priced offering. **Operating health:** support response times have doubled over the same period. **Organizational constraints:** the customer success team has not grown headcount despite a 40% increase in customer count.

The fact base reframes the question entirely. The presenting problem ("we need a new GTM strategy to drive growth") was an interpretation smuggled in as a fact. The actual situation: the company has a *retention* problem caused by an under-resourced customer success function, compounded by new competitive pressure — and a new GTM motion aimed at acquisition would have poured more customers into a leaking bucket. The open question the team flags for the next phase: is the retention drop concentrated in the segment facing new competition, or is it broader — and that single question determines whether the fix is competitive repositioning or operational investment in customer success.
