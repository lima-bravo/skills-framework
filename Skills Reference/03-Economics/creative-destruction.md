# Creative Destruction
*New things displace old things structurally*

**Category:** [Economics](../index.html#economics) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../index.html)**

---

## Definition

Creative destruction, coined by economist Joseph Schumpeter in 1942, describes the process by which capitalism perpetually revolutionises itself from within — new products, processes, and firms destroying existing ones, generating new value in the process. The "destruction" is real: established firms, jobs, and industries are eliminated. The "creative" is also real: the destruction frees resources for higher-value uses and produces net improvement in aggregate productivity and living standards.

For practitioners, creative destruction describes the fundamental competitive dynamic in technology-driven markets: no position is permanent, disruption comes from outside current industry boundaries, and the firms that fear disruption most are typically the ones best positioned for it (incumbents with the most to lose).

---

## Mental Model

Industries evolve through S-curves: a new technology or business model is introduced, grows slowly, then rapidly, then saturates and faces disruption from the next S-curve. Creative destruction is the transition between S-curves. The existing S-curve participants — who have the most resources, the best distribution, and the strongest customer relationships — are often the worst positioned to ride the new S-curve because their advantages are liabilities in the new paradigm (their costs are optimized for the old model, their customers have been trained on the old product).

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Always scan from outside your current industry boundaries for the next disruptor.** Disruption rarely comes from direct competitors; it comes from adjacent industries, new technologies, or new business models that make the current approach obsolete.
- **Build the capability to self-disrupt before someone else does.** Amazon disrupted its own retail business with Amazon Marketplace; Netflix disrupted its DVD business with streaming before Blockbuster could. The firms that survive creative destruction are those that disrupt themselves.
- **Distinguish sustaining from disruptive innovation (Christensen).** Sustaining innovation makes existing products better for existing customers; disruptive innovation starts with a different, often worse product aimed at a different customer, and improves until it displaces the incumbent. These require very different strategic responses.
- **Value destruction is part of the process.** Trying to prevent creative destruction by protecting incumbent positions (through regulation, exclusivity, lock-in) may succeed temporarily but increases the magnitude of eventual disruption.
- **For incumbents: separate "defend the core" from "build the next core."** The correct response to creative destruction is not to abandon the existing business (which funds investment) but to simultaneously invest in the next paradigm before it arrives.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **The innovator's dilemma in practice.** Incumbents allocate resources to serving existing customers, who don't value disruptive innovations in their early stages. By the time the disruptive technology reaches the mainstream, the incumbent has missed the transition.
- **Disrupting without being able to scale.** The disruptor that succeeds at displacing an incumbent may themselves face creative destruction from the next wave before reaching scale. Innovation is not a one-time act.
- **Confusing feature additions with disruption.** Launching a new feature for existing customers in the existing model is sustaining innovation; creative destruction requires a new model, often for new customers.

---

## Worked Example

Kodak invented the digital camera in 1975 and buried the invention because it threatened the highly profitable film business. They continued to earn monopoly returns from film through the 1990s while digital photography grew. When the transition happened, it happened faster than Kodak's ability to restructure. The company that invented the disrupting technology was destroyed by it. The failure wasn't technical; it was organizational: their incentive systems, resource allocation, and mental models were all optimized around the old paradigm. Creative destruction rarely fails on the upside; it fails on the incumbent's inability to acknowledge the transition is real.
