# AI Epistemic Grounding

**Category:** [Applied AI](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

AI Epistemic Grounding is the discipline of treating any claim generated by an AI system — a fact, a citation, a summary, a synthesised conclusion — as unverified by default, regardless of how fluent, confident, or internally consistent it sounds, until it has been independently traced to a real source or checked against ground truth. It is the general epistemic principle underneath every more specific AI-governance practice in this framework: AI Tool Teardown applies a version of it when evaluating a vendor's citations before a procurement decision, and AI Evaluation Discipline applies it when measuring a deployed system's aggregate accuracy — but the principle itself operates one level down from both, at the moment any single AI-generated claim is encountered, in any context at all, not just a formal evaluation.

---

## Mental Model

An experienced practitioner separates two properties of any AI output that feel like the same thing but are generated by entirely different processes: how plausible and fluent the claim sounds, and whether the claim is actually true. A language model's fluency is a function of what token sequence is statistically likely to follow the preceding context — it optimises for sounding right, not for being right, and the two happen to coincide often enough that it's easy to stop checking. A confident, well-structured, specific-sounding claim carries no more evidence of truth than a hedged, vague one; the model's tone is not a calibrated signal of its own reliability, because nothing in how the text was generated ties confidence-of-phrasing to accuracy-of-content. The practitioner's discipline is to ask, of any AI-generated claim that matters, a question that has nothing to do with how it reads: can this be traced to a real, checkable source, or independently confirmed — and if not, it is an unverified assertion, not a fact, no matter how authoritative it sounds.

The corollary that trips up even careful practitioners: asking the same system "are you sure?" or "can you double-check that?" does not constitute verification. The second answer is generated by the same process as the first — another plausible completion, not an independent check — and it will often confidently reaffirm a wrong answer or, just as unhelpfully, walk back a right one, with the same fluent tone either way. Genuine verification requires a source or a check external to the system that produced the original claim.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Treat every specific factual claim from an AI system as needing independent confirmation before it's repeated, published, or acted on.** A statistic, a citation, a quote, a named source — fluency is not confirmation.
- **Distinguish the claim from its presentation.** A claim delivered with hedges and uncertainty language is not automatically less accurate than one delivered with confidence, and a confident one is not automatically more accurate. Check the substance, not the tone.
- **Never treat "are you sure?" as a verification step.** Asking the same model to re-check its own claim produces another generated answer, not independent evidence; if the claim matters, verify it against a source outside the system that produced it.
- **For anything citation-shaped, check that the citation exists and says what it's claimed to say.** A specific-sounding reference — a name, a year, a page number — can be entirely fabricated while reading exactly like a real one; specificity is not evidence of existence.
- **Calibrate your trust to the task's reversibility and consequence, not to the system's fluency.** A low-stakes, easily-checked draft can tolerate more unverified synthesis than a claim that will inform an irreversible decision or be repeated to someone else as fact.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Mistaking fluency for verification.** Accepting a claim because it was stated clearly, specifically, and confidently, without checking it against any source independent of the system that generated it.
- **Fabricated but plausible citations.** A named source, quote, or statistic that reads exactly like a real one and turns out, on checking, not to exist or not to say what it was claimed to say — the most common and most consequential failure mode of unverified AI output.
- **Treating self-correction as external verification.** Asking the same model to double-check its own output and accepting the second answer as confirmation, when it is generated by the identical unverified process as the first.
- **Propagating an unverified claim downstream.** Repeating an AI-generated fact in a document, a decision, or a conversation with someone else, without flagging that it hasn't been independently checked — the claim then acquires unearned credibility simply by being repeated by a human who sounded sure.

## Worked Example

An analyst asks an AI assistant to summarise the regulatory landscape for a new product launch and include supporting citations. The output reads cleanly: five specific regulations are named, each with a citation to a numbered section and a one-line description of its relevance. The analyst, working under deadline, copies the summary into a client-facing memo, checking only that the prose reads well and the regulations sound plausible for the industry.

A colleague, applying epistemic grounding as a habit rather than a formal review step, spot-checks two of the five citations against the actual regulatory text. One is accurate. The second cites a real regulation number but attributes to it a requirement the actual text doesn't contain — the model synthesised a plausible-sounding rule from adjacent, real provisions, and presented it with the same confident, specific phrasing as the accurate citation. Nothing about how the two claims were phrased distinguished the correct one from the fabricated one; only checking the source did. The memo goes out with the second citation corrected and a note that all five have now been verified against primary sources — a step that would have taken the same twenty minutes whether the fabrication had been caught or not, which is precisely the point: verification has to happen regardless of how confident the output sounds, because confidence was never the signal that mattered.
