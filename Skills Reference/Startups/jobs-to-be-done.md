# Jobs to Be Done
*People don't buy products — they hire them to make progress in a specific moment of struggle.*

**Category:** [Innovation & Entrepreneurship](../index.html#innovation--entrepreneurship) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../index.html)**

---

## Definition

Jobs to Be Done (JTBD) is a framework for understanding why customers adopt products and services. The central claim is that customers do not buy products for their features or because of their demographic profile — they hire products to make progress in a specific circumstance. The "job" is the functional, social, and emotional progress the customer is trying to achieve. Understanding the job — and the context in which it arises — predicts switching behavior, reveals true competition, and guides feature development far more reliably than product-centric or demographic analysis.

The framework was developed by Clayton Christensen and Bob Moesta. Christensen established the core insight about jobs and circumstances; Moesta's elaboration — particularly the demand-side mechanics of how and why switches actually happen — gives the framework its operational depth. The two contributions are complementary and should be understood together.

---

## Mental Model

An experienced practitioner thinks about every product and feature decision through the lens of the job: what is the customer trying to accomplish, in what specific circumstance, and what are the functional, social, and emotional dimensions of that goal?

**The struggling moment.** Moesta's key contribution is identifying the struggling moment as the actual unit of demand. Jobs don't arise from demographics or abstract needs — they arise from a specific, felt moment of disruption: the point at which a customer's current situation becomes intolerable enough that they are willing to bear the cost, effort, and anxiety of changing. This is not a rational assessment ("my current solution is suboptimal") but an experiential one ("I can't keep doing it this way"). Without a struggling moment, there is no active job being hired for, regardless of how good the product is or how well-understood the job category. Demand begins with push, not pull.

**The four forces.** Whether a customer switches from their current solution to a new one is governed by four forces pulling in opposite directions:

- **Push** — the frustrations, anxieties, and dissatisfactions with the current situation that drive someone to seek a change. This is the struggling moment made explicit.
- **Pull** — the attraction of the new solution: what it promises, what it enables, what progress it makes visible.
- **Anxiety** — fear that the new solution will not work as promised, that the switch will be costly, that they will regret it.
- **Inertia** — the gravitational pull of the current way of doing things: habit, familiarity, existing relationships, the sunk cost of the current setup.

A switch happens only when push + pull overcome anxiety + inertia. Most product thinking focuses almost entirely on pull — features, benefits, messaging — while ignoring the other three forces. This explains a common and frustrating failure mode: customers who understand the product, like what it offers, and still don't switch. Their anxiety or inertia is stronger than the pull, and their push is not yet strong enough. The practical implication is that demand creation is not solely about making your product more attractive. It requires finding customers whose push is strong enough, reducing their anxiety, and helping them articulate the struggling moment that makes switching worth it.

**Competition is defined by the job, not the product category.** A milkshake doesn't compete with other milkshakes — it competes with everything a commuter could hire to make a boring drive more engaging. The switch decision is the richest source of insight: customers who recently switched — to your product or away from it — carry the highest-fidelity information about what job was being done, why the previous solution failed, and what the new solution does better.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Find the struggling moment before selling the solution.** In interviews, ask: "Tell me about the moment you first realized something had to change." Push for specificity — a date, a situation, a feeling. The struggling moment is where the job becomes real. Abstract dissatisfaction is not a struggling moment; a specific event that caused enough friction to trigger search behavior is.
- **Map all four forces, not just the pull.** For any significant adoption decision, explicitly ask: What is pushing the customer out of their current situation? What is pulling them toward the new one? What are they anxious about? What makes staying feel easier than switching? Diagnose which forces are weakest before deciding what to address.
- **Interview around the purchase decision, not the product.** Ask: What were you doing when you first thought you needed this? What did you try before? What made you finally decide to switch? This reconstructs the timeline — first thought, passive looking, active looking, deciding, first use — and reveals where in that sequence the job sharpened and what resolved the anxiety.
- **Identify all three dimensions of the job.** Functional (what task is being accomplished), social (how does this affect how others perceive the customer), and emotional (how does it make the customer feel). Products that address all three build deeper loyalty and are harder to replace.
- **Define your real competition by the job.** Ask: "What else could someone hire to do this same job?" Include non-consumption — doing nothing, doing it themselves — as a competitor. The strongest competition is often the customer's current workaround, not a rival product.
- **Design features around job completion, not feature parity.** The question is not "what do competitors have?" but "what does a customer need to get the job done reliably and satisfyingly?" — and "what reduces their anxiety enough to make the switch?"

## Common Failure Modes

- **Focusing only on pull, ignoring push.** Building a better product and investing in messaging that makes it more attractive addresses only one of the four forces. If the customer's push is weak — if there is no active struggling moment — a stronger pull will not create demand. The customer will appreciate the product and not buy it.
- **Defining the job too narrowly.** "Help me drill a quarter-inch hole" misses the job (hang something on the wall) and the emotional layer (make my space feel like home). Narrow job definitions produce incrementally better drills rather than stud finders, hooks, or command strips.
- **Defining the job too broadly.** "Help me be happier" is too abstract to generate product insight. The job must be specific enough to reveal what "done" looks like and what failure looks like.
- **Ignoring the switch moment.** Most product teams talk to current satisfied users. The most actionable insight comes from recent switchers — people who just adopted the product or who just left for a competitor. Their memories of the struggling moment are freshest.
- **Mistaking interest for intent.** A customer who has a job, likes the product, and has not switched is not a near-conversion waiting to be nudged. They have strong anxiety or inertia. Treating them as a pull problem (more features, better pricing) when it is an anxiety or inertia problem leads to wasted effort.
- **Feature-washing.** Reframing existing feature roadmaps as "jobs" without changing the underlying analysis. The framework produces genuine insight only when it genuinely changes how customer needs are investigated — specifically, when it surfaces the struggling moment and the four forces rather than confirming pre-existing assumptions.

## Worked Example

McDonald's wanted to improve milkshake sales. Conventional market research — demographic profiling, flavor testing — produced no actionable insight. Christensen's team shadowed customers and found that nearly half of all milkshakes were purchased before 9am by solo commuters. The job: hire something to make a long, boring commute more engaging, while keeping one hand on the wheel, without getting hungry by 10am.

Applying the four forces: the **push** was the tedium and hunger of the commute with nothing engaging to consume. The **pull** of the milkshake was that it lasted a long time, was filling, and could be sipped one-handed. The **anxiety** was low — milkshakes are familiar, cheap, and low-risk. The **inertia** was also low — commuters were already stopping at McDonald's. This combination (strong push, meaningful pull, minimal anxiety and inertia) explains why the job was actually being hired for, despite the product having no obvious claim on "commute entertainment."

The competing frame made the product insight clear: the milkshake competed not with other milkshakes but with bananas (finished too fast, not filling), bagels (too crumbly, require two hands), and donuts (messy). To do the job better, the milkshake needed to be thicker (longer engagement time), more interesting in flavor (reduce boredom), and available faster at the drive-through (reduce friction at the decision point). None of these insights came from asking customers what they wanted in a milkshake — they came from understanding the struggling moment, the job it triggered, and the forces that governed the hire decision.
