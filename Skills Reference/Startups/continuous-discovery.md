# Continuous Discovery
*Customer understanding as a weekly practice, not a project phase*

**Category:** [Innovation & Entrepreneurship](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---

## Definition

Continuous discovery, developed by Teresa Torres, is the practice of conducting regular, structured customer interviews on a weekly cadence — not as a one-time research phase at the start of a project, but as an ongoing team discipline that continuously updates the team's understanding of customer needs, behaviors, and contexts. The output is not a research report but a living opportunity space: a shared, evolving map of what customers are trying to accomplish, where they struggle, and what that implies for what to build or explore next.

The contrast is with episodic discovery — the common pattern where teams conduct customer research at the start of a project, produce a findings document, and then spend months executing against it without returning to customers. Episodic discovery treats customer understanding as a stable input. Continuous discovery treats it as a perishable asset that decays as the market, customer base, and competitive environment evolve.

---

## Mental Model

Think of customer understanding as a live signal rather than a stored fact. In a stable environment, episodic discovery is sufficient — you study the territory once and navigate from the map. In environments that move (which includes virtually all innovation contexts), the map becomes outdated faster than you can act on it. The team that discovered customers' needs six months ago and has been building against that understanding is increasingly navigating by an outdated map.

Continuous discovery installs a feedback loop at the team level: every week, at least one team member speaks with at least one customer. The findings update the opportunity space. The opportunity space informs what to build and test next. What gets built and tested produces new questions for the next round of interviews. The loop runs indefinitely, not until "we know enough."

---

## The Practice in Structure

**The weekly interview commitment.** Torres's minimum viable practice: at least one customer interview per week, conducted by a product team member (not a researcher acting as intermediary). The interview is not a usability test or a satisfaction survey. It follows the continuous interviewing format: open-ended questions about what the customer is trying to accomplish, what gets in the way, and how they currently handle it. The goal is to generate stories about actual behavior, not opinions about hypothetical features.

**The opportunity space as a living artifact.** Findings are not written up as reports — they are added to a shared opportunity map, typically organized as an Opportunity Solution Tree. Each interview either confirms an existing opportunity, adds nuance to it, or surfaces a new one. The team reviews the opportunity space regularly and asks: are we learning new things each week, or are we hearing the same stories? Saturation on a branch means it is well-understood; persistent novelty means the space has more to reveal.

**Tri-weekly cadence.** Torres's full cadence model: weekly interviews feed the opportunity space; bi-weekly assumption testing (small experiments) tests whether proposed solutions address real opportunities; monthly synthesis connects what was learned to the product or innovation roadmap. The three cadences run in parallel, with the interview cycle always upstream of the experiment cycle.

**Interview recruiting as infrastructure.** The most common reason teams don't maintain a weekly cadence is that recruiting interview participants is hard. Continuous discovery requires solving this once as an infrastructure problem rather than repeatedly as a logistics problem: a standing opt-in panel of customers who have agreed to periodic contact, a lightweight scheduling system, and a norm that interview time is protected on the calendar. Teams that recruit ad-hoc will do so inconsistently; teams that have recruiting infrastructure maintain the cadence.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **The team does the interviews, not a research team.** The point of continuous discovery is that the people making build decisions maintain direct contact with customers. When discovery is mediated through a research team, the nuance that shapes good product decisions gets filtered out. A designer who heard a customer describe a frustrating workaround in their own words has different intuitions than one who read a researcher's synthesis of twenty similar stories.
- **Distinguish what customers say from what they do.** Continuous discovery is behavior-first: the interview surfaces what customers are trying to accomplish and what they actually do, not what they say they want or would use. "I would definitely use that" is an opinion. "I spent three hours last Tuesday trying to figure out how to do X" is a behavior. Only behaviors are evidence.
- **Don't interview to validate — interview to discover.** Teams under pressure to confirm existing plans run interviews that confirm existing plans. Continuous discovery works when the team enters each interview genuinely open to hearing something that changes the current direction. A weekly interview that only reinforces existing beliefs is a signal that the questions are too narrow or the participant pool is too homogeneous.
- **Share raw interview observations, not just conclusions.** When one team member interviews a customer, the whole team should hear the key stories directly — ideally through a short debrief with quotes and verbatim observations — not just a synthesized takeaway. Direct exposure to customer language builds shared intuition faster than summary.
- **Track interview-to-insight conversion.** Count how many interviews produce at least one new insight (a finding that updates the opportunity space) per week. A rate below 50% suggests the team is either interviewing the wrong customers, asking too narrow questions, or has genuinely saturated a portion of the space and should shift focus.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Discovery as a pre-launch phase.** The most common failure is treating continuous discovery as a label applied to the discovery work done before development starts. This is episodic discovery with a new name. The test: is the team interviewing customers this week, right now, regardless of where the product or innovation program is in its lifecycle?
- **Researcher-mediated discovery.** When all customer contact is routed through a UX research team, product teams lose the direct exposure to customer reality that makes discovery generative. Research teams produce excellent synthesis but cannot replace the team member who heard the customer's words and felt the friction in real time.
- **Interview fatigue and scheduling drift.** Weekly cadences degrade without deliberate maintenance. The second customer interview is always harder to schedule than the first; the tenth is harder than the fifth. Without infrastructure (a standing panel, protected calendar time, a recruiting norm), continuous discovery reverts to episodic within two to three months.
- **Confusing continuous discovery with continuous user testing.** Usability testing evaluates whether customers can use a product that has been built. Discovery interviews explore what customers are trying to accomplish before anything is built. Running usability tests on a weekly cadence is not continuous discovery — it is continuous usability testing, which answers a different question.

## Worked Example

Spotify's product squads operate a continuous discovery model in which each squad maintains a standing pool of users who have opted into ongoing research contact. Researchers and product managers alternate conducting weekly interviews, with findings fed directly into squad-level opportunity backlogs. The opportunity backlog — their version of the opportunity space — is reviewed in squad-level weekly syncs and informs experiment prioritization for the following two weeks. Crucially, squad members rotate through interview duty rather than leaving discovery to a specialist: engineers, designers, and product managers each conduct several interviews per quarter. The explicit goal is that every squad member maintains enough direct customer contact to have genuine intuitions, not just received findings.
