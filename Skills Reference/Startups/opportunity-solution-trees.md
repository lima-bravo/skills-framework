# Opportunity Solution Trees
*See the full space before committing to a path*

**Category:** [Innovation & Entrepreneurship](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---

## Definition

An Opportunity Solution Tree (OST) is a visual framework developed by Teresa Torres for connecting a desired outcome to the customer opportunities that might produce it, the solutions that might address each opportunity, and the experiments that would test each solution. It was designed to solve a specific and pervasive failure mode in product and innovation work: teams collapse too quickly onto a single solution for each problem, treat experiment failure as opportunity failure, and lose sight of the full space of possibilities they should be exploring.

The tree makes the space explicit. It forces teams to distinguish between the outcome they are pursuing, the customer needs (opportunities) that could contribute to it, and the solutions they are considering — keeping all three levels separate until learning justifies committing to one path.

---

## Mental Model

Most teams work as a linear path: we have a goal, we think of a solution, we build it, we measure whether it worked. When it doesn't work, the implicit conclusion is that the goal was wrong or the opportunity was wrong — and a new path begins from scratch. The tree reveals what this linear logic hides: there are typically many opportunities beneath any outcome, many solutions beneath any opportunity, and many experiments beneath any solution. Failing one branch does not invalidate the tree. It produces learning about that branch and shifts attention to the next.

The tree is not a planning tool — it is a learning navigation tool. It doesn't tell you which branch to pursue; it makes all the branches visible so that experiment results move you through the tree intelligently rather than randomly.

---

## Structure

**Outcome (root).** The business or product goal the team is accountable for. Stated as a measurable change in customer behavior: "increase the percentage of users who complete a second purchase within 30 days." Not a feature, not a metric target — a change in what customers do. The outcome is held fixed while the tree below it is explored.

**Opportunities (first branch level).** Customer needs, pain points, or desires that, if addressed, would contribute to the outcome. Derived from discovery — interviews, observation, JTBD analysis — not from internal brainstorming. Each opportunity is a distinct customer problem space: "customers don't know what to buy next," "customers find the returns process too risky," "customers don't trust the quality descriptions." Multiple opportunities sit beneath a single outcome; the team does not need to pick one before exploring.

**Solutions (second branch level).** Specific approaches to addressing a particular opportunity. Each opportunity can have multiple solutions. "Customers don't know what to buy next" might be addressed by a recommendation engine, a curated collection, a post-purchase quiz, or a social proof mechanism. Solutions are generated for one opportunity at a time, held lightly, and not committed to until tested.

**Experiments (leaf level).** The specific tests designed to evaluate whether a solution addresses the opportunity. Each experiment tests one assumption — typically the most critical unknown about whether the solution works. Experiments at the leaf level are cheap and fast; commitment to building follows, not precedes, learning.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Derive opportunities from discovery, not meetings.** Opportunities that come from customer interviews and observation are grounded in actual behavior. Opportunities generated in internal brainstorming sessions are hypotheses about customer needs, not observations of them. Label them differently on the tree — it changes how you hold them.
- **Hold at least three solutions per opportunity before testing any.** The single-solution trap is the most common failure mode the tree is designed to prevent. If a team has only one solution for an opportunity, it is likely in love with the solution rather than committed to the opportunity. Generating three forces genuine exploration of the solution space.
- **Let experiments inform which branch to pursue, not intuition.** The tree creates the temptation to pick the most promising-looking branch. Resist it. Design cheap experiments that distinguish between branches and let results navigate. The branch that looked least promising often turns out to be richest.
- **Update the tree as you learn.** The tree is not a document produced once — it is a living map of the current state of exploration. New opportunities discovered in interviews get added. Solutions that fail get marked and explained. Opportunities that turn out not to connect to the outcome get pruned. A static tree is a plan; a dynamic tree is a learning system.
- **Use it across teams to share the opportunity space.** In a multi-team innovation program, each team can own branches of a shared tree. This makes cross-team learning visible: team A's experiment on one solution informs team B's work on an adjacent opportunity. The tree becomes the structure for the learnings library.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Confusing solutions with opportunities.** "Add a recommendation feature" is a solution. "Customers don't know what to buy next" is an opportunity. Teams that put solutions at the opportunity level lock themselves into a solution before they've understood the problem. The tree only works if the distinction is maintained rigorously.
- **Pruning the tree based on opinion rather than evidence.** Leadership reviews where promising-looking opportunities are cut because they feel unlikely, or where solutions are eliminated because they conflict with current strategy, destroy the tree's value. Pruning decisions should be based on experiment results, not intuition about which branches are worth exploring.
- **Treating the tree as a backlog.** Product backlogs are prioritized lists of things to build. Opportunity solution trees are maps of things to learn. Treating the tree as a backlog imports the wrong operating logic — teams start optimizing for throughput rather than learning quality.
- **One opportunity per outcome.** A tree with one opportunity beneath the outcome is a linear plan with extra visual complexity. The value of the tree comes entirely from representing multiple paths simultaneously.

## Worked Example

A fintech team is accountable for an outcome: increase the percentage of customers who actively save (make at least one discretionary transfer to savings) within their first 90 days. Customer discovery produces four distinct opportunities: customers don't have a compelling reason to start saving (motivation gap); customers don't know how much they can afford to save (calculation friction); customers start but lose momentum after the first transfer (habit gap); customers distrust that their money is accessible if needed (anxiety barrier). Each opportunity generates three or more solution candidates. The team designs cheap experiments — a calculator tool test, a goal-setting prompt test, a social proof message test, an instant withdrawal guarantee — one per critical assumption per branch. Two experiments return strong positive signals; two return clean negatives. The tree makes it clear that the habit gap and the anxiety barrier are the real constraints, and the motivation gap experiments, though they failed, produced an insight: customers already have motivation — what they lack is permission. That insight opens a new solution branch the team hadn't considered before running the experiments.
