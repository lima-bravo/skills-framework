# Pivot or Persevere
*Structured decision when the current approach isn't working*

**Category:** [Startups](../index.html#startups) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../index.html)**

---

## Definition

The pivot-or-persevere decision is the structured assessment of whether to change strategy (pivot) or continue executing the current one (persevere) based on learning from experiments and the market. Eric Ries defined a pivot as "a structured course correction designed to test a new fundamental hypothesis about the product, business model, or engine of growth." It is not giving up; it is the deliberate application of learning to direction.

---

## Mental Model

The pivot-or-persevere decision is a Bayesian update: given what we have learned, has our confidence in the current direction increased or decreased? If the learning consistently validates the direction, persevere. If it consistently disconfirms, pivot. The discipline is having a clear threshold in advance and applying it honestly rather than interpreting ambiguous results in whichever direction preserves the existing strategy.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Set a review cadence and hold to it.** Scheduled pivot-or-persevere reviews (monthly for early-stage, quarterly for later-stage) impose the discipline of regular reassessment that organic execution does not produce.
- **Separate the pivot decision from the performance evaluation.** A pivot is not a failure assessment; it is a strategic update. Conflating the two creates defensiveness that distorts the analysis.
- **Identify what types of pivot are available.** Common pivot types (Ries): zoom-in pivot (one feature becomes the product), zoom-out pivot (the product becomes a feature of a larger offering), customer segment pivot, customer need pivot, platform pivot, business model pivot, engine of growth pivot, channel pivot, technology pivot. Naming the type clarifies the decision.
- **Maintain what's working while pivoting.** Few pivots require abandoning everything. The discipline is identifying what evidence supports continuation (preserve those elements) and what evidence requires change (pivot those elements).
- **Distinguish perseverance from inertia.** Continuing without clear supporting evidence is inertia, not perseverance. Perseverance requires active evidence that the direction is correct.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Pivoting from emotional exhaustion rather than evidence.** Changing direction because it's frustrating, not because the evidence points elsewhere. Evidence-based pivots improve outcomes; emotional pivots usually don't.
- **Persevering from sunk cost rather than evidence.** "We've come too far to change" is sunk cost reasoning. The decision should be made from current evidence.
- **Pivoting so frequently that no learning accumulates.** Each experiment needs enough time to generate valid data. Weekly pivots produce no learning.

---

## Worked Example

Instagram began as Burbn — a check-in app with gaming elements. The data showed one feature was used by almost all users: photo sharing. Everything else was barely used. The pivot was not a failure; it was a data-informed decision to strip the product to its single point of strength. The pivot preserved the user base, the team, and the core technology. Everything not supported by the usage data was cut. The result: a product with exceptional retention and word-of-mouth because it did one thing excellently.
