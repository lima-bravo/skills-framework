# Context-Switching Cost
*The hidden tax on fragmented attention*

**Category:** [Delivery & Flow](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---

## Definition

Context-switching cost is the productivity loss incurred when a worker (or team) shifts attention between unrelated tasks. The loss has three components: the time to put one context away, the time to load the next, and a tail of degraded performance while the new context fully reloads. Gerald Weinberg's *Quality Software Management* (1992) estimated that two simultaneous projects cost roughly 20% of capacity to switching; three projects cost roughly 40%; five projects roughly 75%. The exact numbers are contested; the rough shape — non-linear and severe — is robust across studies.

---

## Mental Model

Think of a chef working on three meals at once versus one at a time. Each time the chef moves from one pan to another, they must remember salt levels, heat settings, and what step they were on. The "switch" is invisible if you're tasting the food; it shows up as the meal arriving later than it should have. Knowledge work is the same with one wrinkle: the switching cost grows with the difference between contexts. Switching between two related design problems is cheap; switching from a finance review to a hiring conversation to a customer escalation is expensive.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Batch by context.** Group the day into thematic blocks (financials, customers, team) instead of scattering across the week.
- **Defend a quorum for each context.** Two hours on a single context is roughly the floor below which switching tax dominates active work.
- **Treat interruptions as expensive.** A 5-minute Slack interruption can cost 20–30 minutes of effective work via reload time.
- **Make switching visible.** Time-track for one week; the gap between "hours logged" and "hours of progress" is the switching tax.
- **Limit concurrent projects at the team level.** The team-level analogue of the same individual mechanism.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **"I can multi-task."** Most people cannot, and the people who claim it loudest are usually the worst at it.
- **Calendar full = productive.** A calendar of seven 30-minute meetings is seven context switches; net cognitive output may be near zero.
- **Tolerating chronic Slack-driven attention.** Real-time messaging without protected blocks erodes deep work continuously.
- **Treating switching as an individual problem.** It is a workflow design problem; the org's expectation for response latency drives the switching.

---

## Worked Example

A product lead reports being "fully booked but never finishing anything." A two-week diary study reveals 47 distinct context-switches per day on average, with the longest uninterrupted block being 18 minutes. Three interventions: (1) move 1:1s and reviews into two-day clusters so the other three days have multi-hour open blocks; (2) move team Slack response expectations from "within an hour" to "within four hours" for non-urgent topics; (3) introduce a "no-meetings Wednesday" norm. Six weeks later: deep-work blocks average 90 minutes, total meeting time is unchanged, output is up — measurably so, because three initiatives that had been "stuck for months" cleared in two weeks.
