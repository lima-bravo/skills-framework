# A3 Problem Solving
*One page, structured, written for thinking and alignment*

**Category:** [Delivery & Flow](../index.html#delivery-flow) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../index.html)**

---

## Definition

An A3 is a single sheet of A3-sized paper (or its digital equivalent) that frames an operational problem from background through countermeasures and follow-up. The format was developed at Toyota as a thinking and coaching tool: the constraint of one page forces the author to compress the situation into a few high-leverage facts and to think causally. The structure (background → current state → goal → analysis → countermeasures → plan → follow-up) is canonical; the value is the discipline of writing it. Codified in John Shook's *Managing to Learn* (2008).

---

## Mental Model

The A3 is to operational improvement what a one-page memo is to decision-making: it forces a kind of thinking that slides decks cannot. A 40-slide deck about a delivery problem typically obscures three crucial facts in a sea of context; an A3 demands that those three facts be on the page, named, and connected by causal arrows. The form is also a coaching artifact — a senior practitioner reviews the A3 with the author and improves the thinking, not the slides. The result is a generation of operational leaders who reason in causes and countermeasures rather than narratives.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Start with current state, not solutions.** The most common failure is jumping to countermeasures; an A3 is more useful when the current-state diagnosis takes the bulk of the page.
- **Show the gap.** State the goal and the current state in the same units; the visible gap is the work.
- **Distinguish countermeasures from solutions.** Countermeasures address specific root causes; "solutions" usually attack symptoms.
- **Reserve space for follow-up.** A countermeasure without a date and an owner is decoration. The A3's right-hand side is the management commitment.
- **Use it for coaching, not for approval.** The A3 is the artifact of a learning conversation; if it becomes a sign-off document, the form has been captured.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **PowerPoint A3s.** A 12-slide deck called an A3 has missed the point — the constraint is the discipline.
- **Pre-loaded answers.** Filling in countermeasures before the analysis is the most common pathology; teach authors to leave the right side blank until the left side is honest.
- **A3 as report.** A finished A3 framed on a wall as evidence of "lean" is a museum piece. Live A3s are messy, marked up, and short-lived.
- **One A3 for too many problems.** If the gap is "increase revenue by 30%," the A3 is meaningless. Tighten the problem first.

---

## Worked Example

A claims-processing function has cycle time of 14 days against a target of 7. A first A3 attempts to "fix claims processing" and produces vague countermeasures. A second A3 narrows to "reduce wait time between intake and adjudication for property claims, which is responsible for 60% of the gap." Current state: average intake-to-adjudication wait is 5.5 days; goal is 1.5; root-cause analysis traces it to a batched daily handoff. Countermeasures: continuous handoff with a WIP-limited queue. Follow-up dates and owner named. Eight weeks later, wait time is 1.2 days; cycle time has dropped to 8 days; the next A3 picks the next slice.
