# Socratic Thinking

**Category:** [General Thinking](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

The Socratic method is a form of cooperative dialog in which a series of probing questions is used to expose assumptions, surface contradictions, and guide the interlocutor toward clearer thinking or a better-grounded conclusion. Named after Socrates, whose practice was to claim ignorance and ask questions until the other person's position either became well-founded or collapsed under its own contradictions. In practice, Socratic thinking is both a technique for examining others' claims and a discipline for examining one's own.

It is distinct from First Principles thinking in that it is dialogic and questioning rather than constructive — it tears down and tests, where First Principles builds up from the ground.

---

## Mental Model

The Socratic practitioner operates from a position of genuine (or feigned) ignorance. Every assertion — especially from authority or convention — is treated as a hypothesis to be examined: "What do you mean by that exactly? How do you know? What would a counter-example look like? What follows from that?" The goal is not to win an argument but to arrive at a more accurate understanding. The method works because most confident claims are based on imprecise definitions, unexamined assumptions, or narrow evidence.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Ask for definitions before engaging with claims.** "What do you mean by 'better'?" or "How are you defining 'success' here?" — imprecise terms are the source of most apparent agreement and most real disagreement. Defining terms first resolves a surprising fraction of disputes.
- **Follow each answer with "how do you know?"** The chain from claim to evidence to interpretation to conclusion is where the weak links live. Trace it deliberately.
- **Use hypothetical counter-examples.** "Is there a case where that wouldn't be true?" is often more productive than direct contradiction. A single valid counter-example doesn't destroy a claim but forces it to be refined.
- **Apply to your own thinking before applying it to others.** Internal Socratic dialog — "what exactly am I claiming here, and how do I know?" — is the highest-value use of the method.
- **The goal is examination, not humiliation.** Socratic questioning used aggressively to embarrass rather than to discover is a social weapon, not a thinking tool. Maintain genuine curiosity as the orientation.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Socratic aggression.** Using relentless questioning to make a person feel stupid rather than to genuinely examine a claim. This destroys the psychological safety required for honest dialog.
- **Question without intent.** Asking questions you don't actually want answered, using the form of inquiry to appear rigorous while guiding toward a predetermined conclusion.
- **Infinite regress.** Questioning the questions. Every answer can be questioned; the skill is knowing when sufficient clarity has been reached to make a good decision.

---

## Worked Example

A product team claims "users want more features." Socratic examination: "Which users? How many? How do you know it's features they want rather than the outcomes the features might provide? What did they say exactly? When they used the product without the features, what specifically went wrong? Has any user churned specifically because of missing features vs. other reasons?" Within this questioning chain, "users want more features" typically resolves into "three enterprise customers mentioned X in interviews, but churn data suggests the problem is onboarding, not feature depth." The original confident claim was a conflation of weak signals.
