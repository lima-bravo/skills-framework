# Circle of Competence
*Know the edge of what you know*

**Category:** [General Thinking](../index.html#general-thinking) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../index.html)**

---

## Definition

The Circle of Competence is the domain within which a person has genuine, earned knowledge — understanding not just of the facts but of the failure modes, the edge cases, and the nuance that only comes from direct experience and study. The concept, articulated by Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger, holds that exceptional performance does not require a large circle; it requires knowing precisely where the boundary of your circle lies.

The critical distinction is between knowledge and the feeling of knowledge. Many domains feel familiar — we have opinions, we have read articles, we have seen the results — without our having the depth to distinguish correct from plausible-sounding-but-wrong. The practitioner's discipline is maintaining a brutally accurate map of their own competence boundary.

---

## Mental Model

Draw three concentric rings. The inner ring is your genuine deep expertise — where you know the failure modes, where you've been wrong and understand why, where you can generate new insights rather than just recall received ones. The middle ring is informed familiarity — you understand the basics and can ask good questions, but should not make final calls without expert input. The outer ring is the vast territory you know little about. The razor insight: most people draw their inner ring far too large. The value of the model comes entirely from honest calibration.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Track your predictions.** The only reliable way to calibrate your circle is to make explicit predictions in a domain and observe outcomes. Feeling confident is not evidence; track record is.
- **Distinguish knowledge from exposure.** Reading extensively about surgery does not put surgery inside your circle. Ask: have I made consequential decisions in this domain, been wrong, understood why, and updated?
- **The boundary is more valuable than the center.** Knowing your limits precisely lets you seek expert input exactly when needed — not reflexively (which is expensive) and not never (which is dangerous).
- **Use the circle to frame, not to fence.** The goal is not to refuse to think about things outside your circle but to explicitly flag those thoughts as lower-confidence and seek calibration.
- **Shrink your circle under pressure.** In high-stakes, time-pressured decisions, operate within the most conservative definition of your circle. The cost of overreach is highest when there is least time to recover.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **The Dunning-Kruger entry point.** Early learning in any domain produces false confidence before real competence is established. The circle feels large exactly when it is smallest.
- **Static mapping.** The circle changes over time — expertise deepens in some areas and atrophies in others. A circle mapped five years ago may no longer be accurate.
- **Using the circle to justify intellectual laziness.** "That's outside my circle" is not a reason to avoid developing an informed view; it is a reason to hold that view with appropriate humility and seek expert input.

## Worked Example

A successful SaaS founder raises a fund and begins making angel investments. Her operational circle — product, B2B sales, SaaS unit economics — is genuinely deep. She begins making investments in biotech because she finds the science fascinating and has read widely. Three investments fail in ways she did not anticipate: regulatory approval timelines, IP disputes, and clinical trial design issues. Her circle in SaaS was deep; her circle in biotech was wide but shallow. The expensive lesson is that reading widely creates the feeling of the circle without the substance.
