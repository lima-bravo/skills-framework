# Chesterton's Fence

**Category:** [General Thinking](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

Chesterton's Fence, from a passage by G.K. Chesterton, is the principle that a rule, structure, or process should not be removed until the reason for its existence is understood — because "no one currently knows why" is a statement about the observer's knowledge, not about whether the thing has a function. The parable: a reformer comes across a fence in the middle of a field with no obvious purpose and wants to tear it down. Chesterton's reply — go away and find out why it was put there first, and only then, if you can explain its purpose, may you decide it's safe to remove. The principle is the necessary counterweight to any aggressive-deletion instinct: it doesn't argue against removing things, it argues against removing things you don't yet understand.

---

## Mental Model

An experienced practitioner distinguishes two situations that look identical from the outside: a structure that has genuinely outlived its purpose, and a structure that is quietly doing necessary work that isn't currently visible precisely because the structure is handling it. "I don't know why we do this" is evidence about the observer, not about the thing — the original problem the fence solved may have been forgotten, moved to someone else's job, or become invisible precisely because it's no longer occurring, thanks to the fence. The practitioner's discipline is to trace the rule back to its origin before removing it: who put this here, what problem were they solving, and does that problem still not exist, or does it not exist right now only because the fence is still up? Only after answering that question honestly is deletion a safe, informed choice rather than a bet made blind.

This is not an argument for never removing anything — accumulated rules, process, and structure genuinely do need pruning, and an organisation that keeps every fence forever for fear of what it might be for will drown in inherited cruft. The discipline is sequencing: understand, then decide, rather than decide by assuming that not understanding is the same as there being nothing to understand.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Before removing a rule, process, or structure, ask explicitly: who built this, and what problem were they solving?** If no one can answer, that's a research task, not a green light.
- **Distinguish "this looks pointless" from "I have confirmed this is pointless."** The first is an observation about your current knowledge; only the second, reached after investigating the origin, is a basis for removal.
- **Look for the fence's function in what currently isn't happening.** A safety process with no recent incidents, a review step that hasn't caught an error in years, an approval gate no one has been rejected by lately — these can look pointless precisely because they're working.
- **Set a time-boxed investigation before deciding, not an indefinite one.** The point isn't to freeze all change until every historical rationale is perfectly reconstructed — a reasonable, bounded effort to find the original reason is enough to move from "unknown" to "informed."
- **Once you understand the fence's original purpose, feel free to remove it if that purpose no longer applies.** Chesterton's Fence licenses removal once understood; it only forbids removal while still misunderstood.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Removing something because "no one remembers why we do this."** Treating institutional forgetting as evidence of institutional pointlessness, when it's often just evidence that no one currently working there was around for the original incident.
- **Using Chesterton's Fence as an excuse to never change anything.** Demanding an impossibly complete historical reconstruction before allowing any deletion, which turns a reasonable caution into a permanent veto on simplification.
- **Confusing "this rule is annoying" with "this rule has no function."** An inconvenient control can be both irritating to the people it constrains and genuinely necessary — annoyance is not evidence of purposelessness.
- **Investigating the wrong question.** Asking whether the rule is currently being followed correctly, instead of asking why it exists at all — a rule can be followed perfectly and still be safe to remove, or ignored constantly and still be protecting against something real.

## Worked Example

A newly appointed operations lead reviews the finance team's expense approval process and finds a step that requires any expense over a small, seemingly arbitrary threshold to be co-signed by someone outside the requester's direct department. No one currently on the team can explain why the threshold is set where it is, and it slows down routine approvals. The instinct is to raise the threshold or remove the cross-department sign-off entirely.

Before doing so, the lead pulls old incident records and finds the answer: three years earlier, a department head had approved a series of invoices to a vendor that turned out to be a shell company set up by a since-departed employee, discovered only because an unrelated audit happened to catch it. The cross-department sign-off was added specifically to ensure no single department could approve and disburse funds to a new vendor without a second set of eyes from outside that department's existing relationships — a control aimed exactly at the fraud that had actually occurred, not an arbitrary bureaucratic addition. The threshold itself, however, was set based on transaction volumes from three years ago and hasn't been adjusted for growth since; it now applies to routine expenses well below the level anyone would consider a fraud risk. The lead keeps the cross-department control intact — the fence has a real purpose that still applies — but recalibrates the threshold to match current transaction sizes, removing the part of the friction that no longer matches the actual risk while preserving the part that does.
