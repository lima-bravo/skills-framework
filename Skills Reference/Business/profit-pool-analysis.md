# Profit Pool Analysis
*Revenue tells you where the money moves through. Profit pools tell you where it stops.*

**Category:** [Business](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---

## Definition

Profit pool analysis is the discipline of mapping where profit — not revenue — is actually created and captured across a market, value chain, product set, or customer base, and explaining the structural reasons it concentrates where it does. It separates the *revenue pool* (where money flows) from the *profit pool* (where it accumulates after costs), surfaces concentration and leakage, and connects the resulting map to where-to-play, where-to-avoid, and how-to-improve-capture decisions. The discipline was popularized by Gadiesh and Gilbert's work at Bain in the 1990s, built on a single observation that still surprises most strategy conversations: the part of a market that generates the most revenue is very often not the part that generates the most profit.

---

## Mental Model

Picture a value chain as a series of connected pools joined by pipes, with money flowing through from end customer back to raw input. Most strategic conversations look at the *size* of each pool — which segment, product, or activity has the most revenue — and treat that as a proxy for attractiveness. Profit pool analysis asks a different question: which of these pools, regardless of size, is where the *money actually stays*? A large, fast-flowing pool that drains as fast as it fills (high revenue, thin margin, intense competition) is far less attractive than a smaller pool with a dam at the bottom (lower revenue, high margin, defensible position).

The canonical illustration is the U.S. automotive industry in the 1990s: vehicle manufacturing generated the overwhelming majority of *revenue* in the industry, but financing, leasing, insurance, and after-sales service — a fraction of the revenue — generated the majority of *profit*. A strategist looking only at revenue share would have recommended doubling down on manufacturing share; the profit pool map pointed in nearly the opposite direction. The map is only useful, though, if it goes one layer deeper than "where is the money" to "*why* does it concentrate there" — scale economics, pricing power, switching costs, structural cost position, or competitive intensity. Without that structural explanation, a profit pool map is just a colorful description of where things currently stand, with no guidance about whether that will still be true in three years, or whether a new entrant could occupy the same pool.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Map revenue and profit side by side — never profit alone.** The contrast between the two maps *is* the insight. A pool that is large in revenue and small in profit is telling you something different than a pool that is small in both, and the difference only becomes visible when both maps sit next to each other.
- **Explain concentration structurally, not descriptively.** "This segment is profitable" is an observation; "this segment is profitable because switching costs are high and only three players have the scale to serve it economically" is an explanation that tells you whether the pool will persist, shrink, or be contested. See [Wardley Mapping](wardley-mapping.md) for the broader discipline of explaining *why* a position holds, not just *that* it does.
- **Look for leakage, not just concentration.** Profit pools have edges where value is being created but captured by someone else — a supplier with pricing power, a channel partner taking an outsized margin, a customer segment whose service cost exceeds what they pay. Leakage points are often the highest-leverage finding in the entire analysis, because they represent value that already exists and is simply flowing to the wrong place.
- **Distinguish "attractive to enter" from "attractive to hold."** A profit pool that is attractive today because of a temporary supply constraint or a regulatory quirk is a different strategic object than one that is attractive because of a durable structural advantage — see [Long Tail](../Economics/long-tail.md) and [Power Law](../Mathematics/power-law.md) for how concentration can be structural (durable) or merely current (fragile).
- **Connect the map to a decision — every time.** A profit pool map that ends at "here is where the profit is" has produced an interesting fact, not a strategic input. The map should resolve into where-to-play, where-to-avoid, and how-to-improve-capture recommendations, or it has stopped one step short of being useful.
- **Re-draw the map at the *right* unit of analysis.** "The industry" is usually too coarse; "this one product line in this one geography for this one customer segment" is sometimes too fine. The unit of analysis should match the unit at which the strategic decision will actually be made.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Revenue-as-proxy-for-attractiveness.** The single most common error: assuming the biggest piece of the pie is the best piece to chase, when it is frequently the most contested, thinnest-margin, most commoditized piece precisely *because* everyone else made the same assumption.
- **Snapshot without structure.** Producing a map of where profit sits today without explaining the structural forces that put it there — which leaves the strategist unable to say whether the pool will still exist, in the same place, by the time an investment pays back.
- **Ignoring the leakage edges.** Focusing entirely on where profit concentrates and missing where it's escaping — often the more actionable finding, because recapturing leaking value requires no new market entry, only a renegotiation of who captures what within a relationship that already exists.
- **Wrong unit of analysis.** Mapping at the industry level when the real decision is product-line-by-geography, or vice versa — producing a map that is technically accurate and strategically useless because it doesn't align with where choices actually get made.
- **Treating the map as static.** Profit pools migrate — often toward the part of the value chain that controls the customer relationship, the data, or the platform. A map that doesn't ask "where is this pool moving" gives a strategist a photograph when they needed a forecast.

## Worked Example

A mid-size industrial distributor is deciding where to invest growth capital: expanding its core product distribution business (60% of revenue, the segment everyone in the company identifies with) or building out its parts-and-service business (12% of revenue, viewed internally as a "nice add-on").

The profit pool map reframes the choice entirely. Core distribution generates 60% of revenue but only 22% of profit — margins are thin because the segment is commoditized, customers are price-sensitive, and three large competitors fight over the same accounts. Parts and service generates 12% of revenue but 41% of profit — margins are high because customers who already own the equipment have high switching costs, urgency dominates price sensitivity when something breaks, and the distributor's installed base gives it a structural advantage no new entrant can quickly replicate.

The structural explanation is what makes the map actionable rather than merely interesting: the parts-and-service pool is profitable *because* it rides on an installed base that took decades to build — a durable advantage — whereas core distribution is profitable in inverse proportion to how easily a competitor can match the offer, which is to say, not very. The recommendation shifts from "expand the business everyone identifies with" to "treat the installed base as the strategic asset, and grow the service capacity that monetizes it" — with core distribution reframed from a growth engine to the channel that builds the installed base the service business depends on.
