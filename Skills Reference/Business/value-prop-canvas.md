# Value Prop Canvas
*Match the product to the job to be done*

**Category:** [Business](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---

## Definition

The Value Proposition Canvas (Osterwalder, Pigneur, Bernarda) zooms in on two boxes from the Business Model Canvas: the Customer Segment and the Value Proposition. The customer segment is described through three lenses: Jobs (what they're trying to get done), Pains (the negative experiences, risks, and obstacles they face), and Gains (the outcomes and benefits they desire). The value proposition is described through: Products and Services (what you offer), Pain Relievers (how you alleviate pains), and Gain Creators (how you create gains). Fit occurs when the pain relievers and gain creators match the most important pains and gains of the customer segment.

---

## Mental Model

The canvas prevents the fundamental product mistake: building something that is technically impressive but not matched to what customers are actually trying to achieve. The Jobs-to-be-Done framing (Clayton Christensen) is the heart of the customer side: customers don't buy products, they hire them to get a job done. Understanding the job — not just the desire for the features — produces the most durable product insights.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Start with the customer side, not the product side.** Map the jobs, pains, and gains from customer evidence before mapping any products and services. This prevents solution-first thinking contaminating the diagnosis.
- **Prioritize pains and gains by intensity and frequency.** Not all pains are equal. Rank them by how severe (how much does this pain cost the customer?) and how frequent (how often do they experience it?). Focus pain relievers on the top-ranked pains.
- **Look for the jobs customers are doing with workarounds.** The job that customers are "hiring" multiple imperfect solutions to do is a high-value opportunity.
- **Test the fit hypothesis directly.** For each pain reliever and gain creator, ask: do customers actually experience the pain we're relieving? Do they actually want the gain we're creating? These are empirical questions requiring customer evidence.
- **The "job" is the stable part; the solution evolves.** The job of "getting from here to there" has been served by horses, trains, cars, and planes. The job is stable; the hire-able solution changes with technology and context.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Mapping gains and pains from assumptions rather than research.** The value prop canvas only works if the customer side is filled in from evidence. Internal team assumptions about customer pains are notoriously inaccurate.
- **Over-indexing on gains, under-indexing on pains.** Research consistently shows that pain relief (avoiding a negative) drives action more strongly than gain creation (achieving a positive). Products that primarily create gains often underperform expectations.

---

## Worked Example

A team building project management software maps the customer side for their primary segment (small engineering teams). Jobs: coordinating work across the team, tracking progress, identifying blockers. Top pains (from 30 user interviews): losing context when switching between tools, uncertainty about who is doing what, and wasted time in status meetings. Top gains: knowing at a glance what everyone is working on. The product map focuses on: deep integrations with tools they already use (pain reliever: context switching), clear ownership indicators (pain reliever: who-is-doing-what uncertainty), and async status updates that replace the meeting (pain reliever + gain creator). Every feature maps to a ranked pain or gain.
