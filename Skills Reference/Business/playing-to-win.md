# Playing to Win
*Five choices. One cascade. Each enables the next.*

**Category:** [Business](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---

## Definition

Playing to Win is a strategy framework developed by A.G. Lafley and Roger L. Martin (*Playing to Win*, 2013) that defines strategy as five integrated choices arranged in a cascade: **Winning Aspiration** (what does winning look like, and for whom?), **Where to Play** (which markets, customers, channels, and geographies?), **How to Win** (what is the source of competitive advantage in those arenas?), **Must-Have Capabilities** (which activities must be performed distinctively well?), and **Enabling Management Systems** (what structures and norms sustain those capabilities?). The cascade constraint is the core claim: each choice is only valid if it flows from the choice above it. A how-to-win that doesn't match the where-to-play is not a strategy; it's two unrelated choices.

---

## Mental Model

Two ways to run a strategy process. First: assign each level of the organization a strategic question, let them answer it, and assemble the answers into a plan. Result — five internally coherent answers that, taken together, contradict each other. The capability investments required by the enterprise segment are incompatible with the cost structure required to win in SMB; the management systems built to run the existing business actively block the new capabilities the strategy requires. Second: build the cascade from top to bottom, treating each choice as a constraint on the one below. Winning aspiration shapes where-to-play; where-to-play constrains how-to-win; how-to-win determines which capabilities are "must-have"; capabilities determine which management systems must be built or dismantled. When the cascade is complete, any management-system decision can be traced back to a choice about winning aspiration — and the connection is not arbitrary. That traceability is what makes a strategy real rather than a goal list.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Start with winning aspiration, not financial goals.** "Grow revenue 15%" is an outcome, not an aspiration. "Be the most trusted workflow standard for compliance-sensitive financial-services firms" is an aspiration that shapes every downstream choice. Financial goals are outputs of a good cascade, not inputs to it.
- **Make where-to-play choices binary.** Either you are competing in a segment or you are not. "We'll explore mid-market opportunities" is not a choice; it is an excuse to avoid one. Fuzzy where-to-play boundaries guarantee incoherent capability investments.
- **Test how-to-win against where-to-play.** A premium where-to-play combined with a cost-leadership how-to-win is a broken cascade, not a nuanced strategy. The two arenas require different capability investments and different management systems — you cannot serve both from the same base.
- **"Both" is not a how-to-win.** "We will win through best-in-class product AND lowest total cost of ownership" is a refusal to choose. Every major capability investment will then be contested, because the two winning logics pull toward different priorities.
- **Reverse-engineer competitor cascades.** What your competitor's where-to-play and how-to-win imply about their capabilities tells you where they can and cannot respond — and where the white space is.
- **Test the cascade as a system.** The right question is not "is this a good where-to-play choice?" but "do these five choices reinforce each other?" A cascade that breaks at any link is not a strategy.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Choices made at each level independently.** The aspiration team, the where-to-play team, and the capability team each do their work well — and the assembled strategy is internally inconsistent. No one was responsible for the connections.
- **Aspiration stated as financial outcome.** Defining "win" as "$2B revenue" has no implication for where to play or how to compete. Every where-to-play choice looks equally valid, because none of them is actually constrained.
- **Capabilities treated as inventory.** Organizations list the capabilities they already have, rather than asking which capabilities the chosen how-to-win requires them to build or acquire. The strategy becomes a restatement of current strengths.
- **Management systems left as-is.** The most common failure: the top four cascade choices are made, and then the management systems — compensation, planning cycles, governance processes — are left unchanged. The old systems were designed to win in the old arenas; they actively prevent winning in the new ones.
- **Confusing the cascade with a planning template.** The cascade is a logic of constraint, not a slide template. Filling in five slides does not constitute a strategy; each choice must visibly constrain the one below it.

## Worked Example

A B2B software firm's strategy process produces a comprehensive plan: winning aspiration ("become the market leader"), where to play (enterprise, mid-market, and SMB simultaneously), how to win ("best product, lowest TCO, best customer success"), capabilities (engineering, sales, service). A cascade review finds it broken at every link — "all segments" is not a choice, "best product and lowest TCO" is contradictory, and the capability list matches no specific competitive logic. A refactoring session rebuilds the cascade from the top: winning aspiration — "be the compliance workflow standard for mid-market financial services"; where to play — mid-market financial services only; how to win — deepest regulatory-feature integration in the segment; must-have capabilities — compliance product management and partner-ecosystem depth for integrations; management systems — hire compliance PMs, restructure partner program, reprice for the segment's TCO expectations. The cascade now holds. Two previously approved initiatives (SMB self-serve feature set, enterprise security tier) are killed because they do not flow from the cascade — and the freed capacity accelerates the compliance roadmap.

## References

- *Playing to Win: How Strategy Really Works* — A.G. Lafley & Roger L. Martin (2013) — the source text for the five-choice cascade; built from P&G's strategy practice and documented as a generalisable method.
- *The Design of Business* — Roger L. Martin (2009) — Martin's knowledge-funnel model of how businesses move from mystery to algorithm; the epistemological foundation for how-to-win choices.
- *Good Strategy / Bad Strategy* — Richard P. Rumelt (2011) — the complementary framework; where Playing to Win specifies the cascade structure, Rumelt specifies what makes the diagnosis and guiding policy coherent.
