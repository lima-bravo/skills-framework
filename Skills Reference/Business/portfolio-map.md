# Portfolio Map

**Category:** [Business](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

A portfolio map is a visual instrument that plots an organization's full set of initiatives — products, projects, bets, investments — against two decision-relevant axes, so the set can be evaluated and balanced as a system rather than approved one at a time. The axes vary by purpose: time horizon × uncertainty (the Three Horizons cut), product-newness × market-newness (Nagji & Tuff's Innovation Ambition Matrix), growth × market share (the BCG matrix), or risk × return. The framework on the axes matters less than the function the map performs, which is constant: make concentration, gaps, and capacity conflicts visible on one surface.

The map is a *tool*, not a theory. Its job is not to tell you which bet is good — that judgment happens elsewhere — but to show you the shape of everything you have funded, together, so you can see what no single approval meeting reveals.

---

## Mental Model

The defining problem a portfolio map solves is that initiatives get approved sequentially and each is defensible on its own. No one ever signs off on "twelve near-term, low-risk bets and nothing speculative" — but that is what a year of individually reasonable approvals quietly produces. The portfolio map is the only place the aggregate becomes visible. It converts a pile of separately-justified decisions into a single picture you can judge as a whole.

Two things the picture forces into the open. First, **the gap**: the quadrant or region with no bets in it, which is usually the most important signal the map gives — far more than where any individual dot sits. Second, **hidden correlation**: dots that look spread out but share a single point of failure (the same scarce team, the same channel, the same untested assumption), so the portfolio is far less diversified than it appears. Neither is visible until you put everything on one surface and size each bet by the resources actually behind it.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Choose axes that map to a real decision.** The two axes are not decoration — pick the dimensions on which you would actually reallocate (time-to-payoff, uncertainty, strategic fit, required capability). If moving a dot along an axis wouldn't change a funding decision, it's the wrong axis.
- **Plot what you fund, not what you intend.** Size each bet by the resources actually committed — people and budget, not enthusiasm or slide count. The map of your spending is your real strategy; the map of your intentions is the one in the deck.
- **Look for the empty region first.** Before debating the placement of existing bets, ask what isn't on the map at all. An absent category is a strategic choice you may not have meant to make.
- **Check for hidden correlation.** Trace what each large bet depends on. If several "diversified" initiatives rely on the same team, platform, or assumption, the portfolio carries concentrated risk that the spread of dots disguises.
- **Rebalance on a cadence, not in a crisis.** Set a regular review (quarterly, half-yearly) to re-plot and reallocate. Portfolios left untouched drift toward the safe and near-term, because that is where individually-approved bets accumulate.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **The map as decoration.** Built once for a board deck, admired, and never used to move a single dollar. A portfolio map that doesn't drive reallocation is a chart, not an instrument.
- **Plotting intent instead of resource.** Placing bets by how strategic they sound rather than by what they actually consume. The map then shows the strategy you describe, not the one your spending reveals — and hides the very imbalance it exists to expose.
- **Over-precision on placement.** Arguing whether a dot sits at 0.6 or 0.7 on an axis. The map earns its value by surfacing gross imbalance and gaps, not by pinpoint coordinates; false precision wastes the meeting.
- **Confusing a full map with a balanced one.** Every region having *some* bet is not the same as the allocation being right. A token speculative bet too small to learn anything is an empty region wearing a dot.

---

## Worked Example

A 200-person software company lists every funded initiative — fourteen of them — and plots each on two axes: time-to-payoff (this quarter → two-plus years) and uncertainty (proven → speculative), sizing each dot by headcount. Two things jump out that no individual approval had revealed. First, twelve of the fourteen dots cluster in the near-term, low-uncertainty corner; the company has almost no speculative bets, despite a strategy deck that names "innovation" as a top priority — the spending tells the true story. Second, three of the four largest dots, though spread across different product lines, all depend on the same six-person platform team; what looked like a diversified portfolio has a single point of failure. The map changed two decisions: one near-term initiative was paused to seed two genuine longer-horizon bets, and the platform dependency was made explicit and staffed before it became the bottleneck. The framework labeling the axes mattered far less than the discipline of seeing all fourteen bets on one surface at once.
