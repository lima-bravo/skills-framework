# Portfolio Map
*Manage a balanced set of bets across horizons*

**Category:** [Business](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---

## Definition

A portfolio map (McKinsey's Three Horizons framework is the most common version) organizes an organization's initiatives across time horizons and risk profiles. Horizon 1: optimize and extend the core business (near-term, high confidence). Horizon 2: build emerging businesses that will become the new core (medium-term, moderate uncertainty). Horizon 3: explore and create viable options for future growth (long-term, high uncertainty). The portfolio should have active investment at all three horizons; over-concentration in any single horizon is a strategic risk.

---

## Mental Model

The portfolio map is a visual representation of strategic optionality. H1 generates today's cash flows. H2 generates medium-term growth. H3 generates future optionality — low probability but potentially high-magnitude outcomes. The distribution of investment across horizons reflects risk tolerance, competitive position, and strategic intent. The portfolio is healthy when H1 is strong enough to fund H2 and H3 exploration.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Don't let H1 starve H2 and H3.** Organizations under pressure from H1 performance systematically cut H2 and H3 investment. This is rational in the very short term and dangerous over any medium-term horizon.
- **Don't let H3 distract from H1.** Exciting H3 initiatives can absorb disproportionate senior attention relative to their investment and near-term impact. Protect H1 execution.
- **Expect most H3 bets to fail.** A portfolio with five H3 bets succeeding 100% is insufficiently exploratory. A portfolio with three successes from 20 bets is a healthy exploration rate.
- **Stage transitions are where most value is lost.** Ideas that are strong in H3 exploration often fail in the transition to H2 (scaling from proof of concept to emerging business requires different capabilities and investment levels). Plan the transition explicitly.
- **The horizon timeline is industry-relative, not absolute.** In fast-moving technology markets, H1 may be 6-12 months; H3 may be 2-3 years. In stable industries, H1 is 3-5 years; H3 is 10+ years. Calibrate to your competitive environment.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Tunnel vision on H1.** Pure exploitation of the current model with no exploration investment is a delayed death sentence.
- **H3 theater.** Running exploration programs that are too small to produce real options — too small to hire the talent, build the capability, or reach sufficient scale to generate valid learning.

---

## Worked Example

Google's resource allocation explicitly accounts for the 70-20-10 rule: 70% of resources to H1 (search, advertizing), 20% to H2 (Gmail, Maps), 10% to H3 (moonshots, experimental projects). This is not an accident — it is a deliberate portfolio strategy. The 10% generates the optionality that becomes the 20% of the next cycle. YouTube, Android, and Google Cloud each began as sub-20% investments that became H1 businesses.
