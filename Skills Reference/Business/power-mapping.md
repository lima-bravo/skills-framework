# Power Mapping
*The org chart shows who is accountable. The power map shows who decides.*

**Category:** [Business](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---

## Definition

Power mapping is the practice of identifying and visualising the formal and informal influence structure of an organisation or stakeholder system — who holds decision authority, who shapes the opinions of decision-makers, who controls access and information flow, and where the real veto points sit. It is distinct from stakeholder mapping, which analyses interests, salience, and support levels. Power mapping analyses *influence structure*: not what people want, but how much ability they have to make things happen or stop them.

The distinction matters because organisational power rarely matches organisational hierarchy. Junior people can hold significant informal influence through expertise, relationships, or information control. Senior people can be largely bypassed in practice. Formal authority and real decision-making power diverge in almost every large organisation, and initiatives that navigate the formal structure without understanding the informal structure routinely fail.

*Note: this card is a prerequisite for [Influence Without Authority](../Leadership/influence-without-authority.md) and [Coalition Building](../Leadership/coalition-building.md). Power mapping tells you what the landscape looks like; those cards describe how to navigate it.*

## Mental Model

Every organisation has two structures: the formal structure (the org chart, the RACI, the governance framework) and the informal structure (who people actually go to for a decision, whose approval is silently required, whose objection will kill an initiative regardless of formal authority). Initiatives that only navigate the formal structure succeed if the informal structure happens to align. When it doesn't — which is most of the time in complex organisations — the initiative stalls or fails for reasons that are never officially named.

A power map makes the informal structure visible and workable. It asks: who formally decides? Who actually decides? Who influences the decider? Who has veto power even if they lack formal authority? Whose silence is interpreted as consent, and whose public support will be followed by private opposition? The map is not permanent — power structures shift as people, roles, and priorities change — but at any given moment, it is the terrain on which organisational change actually happens.

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Map formal authority and informal influence separately, then compare.** Begin with the formal structure (who is the accountable decision-maker per the governance framework?), then layer informal influence (who does the decision-maker go to before deciding? whose opinion are they known to weight heavily?). The gap between the two layers is where most initiatives get stuck.
- **Identify gatekeepers and shapers, not just decision-makers.** Power in organisations flows through three roles: decision-makers (who formally decide), shapers (who influence the decision-maker without deciding), and gatekeepers (who control access to information or to the decision-maker). Failing to map shapers and gatekeepers is the most common power-mapping gap.
- **Look for informal veto holders.** In most organisations, there are individuals who lack formal authority to approve but whose opposition is sufficient to kill. They may sit in legal, finance, risk, or IT; they may be informal advisors to the CEO; they may be respected technical experts whose concerns the organisation takes seriously regardless of hierarchy. Map them explicitly.
- **Update the map when the situation changes.** Power structures are dynamic. A reorganisation, a leadership change, a significant success or failure — any of these can shift informal influence dramatically. A power map built six months ago may be materially wrong today.
- **Use the map to sequence conversations.** The primary practical use of a power map is determining who to talk to, in what order, before an initiative is formally proposed. Talk to shapers before decision-makers; neutralise or convert veto holders before the public discussion; ensure that by the time the decision is formally made, the informal structure has already moved.

## Common Failure Modes

**Mistaking the org chart for the power map.** The most common failure: assuming that formal authority equals real influence, and planning stakeholder engagement based on hierarchy rather than actual decision dynamics. This produces technically correct engagement (all the right people were consulted) that is strategically wrong (the real influencers were ignored or approached too late).

**Mapping positions rather than people.** Power is personal, not positional. The CHRO in one organisation may be a powerful informal advisor to the CEO; the CHRO in the next organisation may be ignored in all but HR decisions. The map must reflect the actual person, not the role title.

**Ignoring negative space.** A power map typically focuses on who has influence. Equally important: who is conspicuously absent from discussions where they should be present? Absence from key conversations can signal political marginalisation, deliberate exclusion, or a governance gap — all of which create risk for initiatives that depend on those parties.

**Using the map to manipulate rather than to understand.** Power mapping is an analytical tool, not a manipulation playbook. Its purpose is to understand the influence landscape so that legitimate engagement can be sequenced effectively. Using it to bypass important stakeholders, to engineer artificial consensus, or to outmanoeuvre opposition without addressing the substance of their concerns is both ethically problematic and strategically fragile.

## Worked Example

A digital transformation team is proposing a major change to the firm's core data platform. The formal governance structure shows the CTO as the decision-maker, with a steering committee of four C-suite members as advisors. The team presents to the steering committee, secures the CTO's provisional support, and schedules a board update.

Before the board update, the initiative stalls. Investigation reveals the informal power map: the CTO defers to the Head of Architecture on any technical decision above a certain complexity threshold. The Head of Architecture has not been engaged. Additionally, the CFO — formally one of four advisory committee members — is the person the CEO goes to for an independent read on technology investment proposals. The CFO has concerns about the cost model that were raised in committee but not taken seriously by the team.

Revised approach: a detailed technical session with the Head of Architecture (who raises two significant design concerns, both addressable); a separate working session with the CFO on the cost model, resulting in a revised presentation that leads with risk-adjusted NPV rather than cost reduction. Both conversations happen before the rescheduled board update. The initiative is approved at the board.

## Connections

- **Stakeholder Mapping (Salience Model)** — the complementary tool: stakeholder mapping analyses interests and salience (power, legitimacy, urgency); power mapping analyses influence *structure*. Use both: stakeholder mapping to prioritise who matters and why, power mapping to understand how they relate to each other. *These cards address adjacent but distinct questions — read both.*
- **Influence Without Authority** — the interpersonal skills for navigating the power map once it is drawn; power mapping is the diagnostic, IWA is the intervention toolkit
- **Coalition Building** — the structural approach to assembling sustained support; power mapping identifies who needs to be in the coalition and what relationships between them already exist
- **Principal–Agent Problem** — power gaps often correspond to principal–agent misalignments; the people with formal authority (principals) and the people with real influence (agents) may have different incentives
- **RACI Framework** — the formal accountability layer that power mapping sits beneath; when RACI and power map diverge significantly, the organisation has a governance design problem
- **Narrative Instinct** — informal power often flows to those who tell the most compelling organisational story; identifying who shapes the dominant narrative is part of mapping informal influence
