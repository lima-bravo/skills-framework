# Dominant Logic

**Category:** [Innovation & Entrepreneurship](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

Dominant logic is the shared mental model — the set of assumptions, beliefs, and decision rules — that a successful organization installs over time about how value is created, who the customer is, what competition looks like, and what capabilities matter. Identified by C.K. Prahalad and Richard Bettis, it describes how an organization's past success encodes a theory of the business into its processes, incentives, hiring criteria, and strategic conversations. Over time this logic becomes invisible — it is no longer a theory, it is simply "how things work."

The danger is not that dominant logic exists — every organization needs shared assumptions to operate — but that it persists beyond its useful life. When the environment shifts, the dominant logic filters out signals that don't fit, misdirects resources toward familiar moves, and causes the organization to interpret new threats through the lens of the old model.

---

## Mental Model

Think of dominant logic as a lens ground to a specific focal length. It brings into sharp focus everything that matches the organization's historical success pattern — existing customer segments, familiar competitive moves, known technology trajectories. Everything outside that focal length appears blurry or invisible. The organization doesn't ignore new signals out of laziness; it literally cannot see them clearly with the current lens.

The lens is installed through success, not failure. The more thoroughly the organization has won with a particular model, the more deeply the logic is encoded — in who gets promoted, what gets funded, which arguments land in strategy meetings, and which data points are treated as signal versus noise.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Name the dominant logic explicitly before diagnosing innovation failure.** Ask: what does this organization believe, without question, about who its customer is, what they value, how we make money, and what good performance looks like? Write it down. The items that feel too obvious to write down are the dominant logic.
- **Look for what the organization consistently doesn't fund.** Resource allocation is a revealed logic. The initiatives that reliably fail to get investment — regardless of how well they are argued — are threatening the dominant logic, not just the budget.
- **Test whether new data changes minds or gets explained away.** In organizations with a dominant logic under stress, disconfirming evidence is consistently reinterpreted to fit the existing model. "Yes, but our customers are different." "Yes, but that's not our segment." These are dominant logic defenses.
- **Use outsiders as mirrors.** People hired from different industries, customers from adjacent segments, or new board members often see the dominant logic immediately because they don't share it. Their confusion is diagnostic data.
- **The antidote is not awareness alone.** Telling an organization its dominant logic is limiting does not change it. The logic is encoded in processes, incentives, and power structures. Changing it requires changing those systems, not just running a workshop.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Confusing dominant logic with culture.** Culture is broader — values, norms, behaviors. Dominant logic is specifically the theory of the business: the assumptions about value creation and competition. They interact but are distinct. Changing culture without changing the underlying logic of the business doesn't break the blindspot.
- **Treating it as a leadership problem.** The dominant logic is often most deeply held by the organization's most successful people — those whose careers were built on the current model. The CEO may understand the new environment; the next two layers of management, whose careers are vested in the old model, filter it back out.
- **The acquired company problem.** Acquisitions made to import new capabilities frequently fail because the acquired company is absorbed into the acquirer's dominant logic within 18–36 months. The people who built the new capability leave; the processes that enabled it are standardized away.

---

## Worked Example

Kodak is the canonical case, but the mechanism is worth examining precisely. Kodak invented the digital camera in 1975. The problem was not that leadership was unaware of digital photography — they were. The problem was that Kodak's dominant logic encoded film as the unit of value: manufacturing scale in film chemistry, retail relationships structured around consumables, profitability models built on recurring film and processing revenue. Digital photography didn't just threaten a product line; it invalidated the entire theory of the business. Every time the digital opportunity was analyzed inside Kodak, it was evaluated using the dominant logic's metrics — and it came up small, low-margin, and threatening to existing relationships. The logic didn't ignore digital. It accurately concluded that digital was bad for the Kodak business model. It just couldn't ask whether the Kodak business model needed to change.

Nokia's decline follows the same structure. Nokia understood touchscreens and app ecosystems technically before the iPhone launched. Their dominant logic — built on hardware scale, carrier relationships, and device proliferation — could not accommodate a model where the software platform was the value, and the hardware was the commodity.
