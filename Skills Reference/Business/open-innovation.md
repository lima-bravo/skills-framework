# Open Innovation

**Category:** [Innovation & Entrepreneurship](../../docs/deck.html) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../../docs/deck.html)**

---
## Definition

Open innovation, introduced by Henry Chesbrough, is the practice of deliberately using external knowledge flows — from outside the firm's boundaries — as inputs to the innovation process, and allowing internal knowledge to flow outward when it can create value elsewhere. The contrast is with the closed innovation model, where a firm generates, develops, and commercializes ideas entirely within its own boundaries, treating its R&D as a proprietary competitive moat.

The shift matters because the conditions that made closed innovation rational — scarce external talent, limited research infrastructure outside large firms, slow information flows — have largely reversed. Knowledge workers move between organizations; universities and startups produce commercially relevant research; information travels at near-zero cost. In this environment, the firm that insists on inventing everything internally is not protecting a competitive advantage; it is paying a premium for unnecessary self-sufficiency.

---

## Mental Model

Think of the organization as sitting inside a permeable membrane rather than behind a wall. Ideas, talent, technology, and business models can flow in from outside — through partnerships, licensing, acquisitions, venture investments, ecosystem participation, and crowdsourcing. Ideas developed internally can flow out — through licensing, spin-offs, corporate venturing, and open-source — to create value and returns that internal commercialization could not achieve.

The strategic question shifts from "how do we invent more?" to "how do we identify, access, and integrate the best ideas regardless of where they originate?"

---

## Inbound and Outbound Flows

**Inbound open innovation** is the deliberate sourcing of external knowledge: scouting emerging technology from startups and universities, licensing IP that accelerates internal development, acquiring teams or companies for their capabilities, running open challenges and competitions, building ecosystem partnerships that bring complementary capabilities close.

**Outbound open innovation** is deliberately allowing internal knowledge to create external value: licensing technology that the firm cannot commercialize in all markets, spinning out ventures from internal projects that don't fit the core business model, contributing to open-source ecosystems to attract talent and establish platform standards, and deploying corporate venture capital to maintain windows into emerging spaces.

The most sophisticated practitioners manage both flows as a portfolio — sourcing externally in areas outside core competence, commercializing internally in areas of competitive advantage, and licensing or spinning out where the internal deployment path is blocked.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Map your innovation boundary deliberately.** Which capabilities are genuinely core — where internal development creates durable competitive advantage? Which are enabling but non-core — where access is sufficient and ownership adds cost without proportional benefit? Open innovation applies primarily to the latter.
- **Build a pipeline for external scouting, not just internal R&D.** Technology scouting, startup partnerships, university relationships, and ecosystem participation require organizational muscle. They don't happen by sending a team to a conference once a year.
- **Integrate or die.** Inbound open innovation fails most often at the integration stage: the external idea or acquisition is identified and acquired but cannot be absorbed. The absorptive capacity of the receiving organization — its ability to recognize, understand, and apply external knowledge — is the limiting factor.
- **Protect core IP while opening enabling IP.** Not all internal knowledge should flow out equally. The test: does releasing this externally erode our differentiation with customers, or does it create ecosystem value that comes back to us in other forms?
- **Watch for NIH syndrome.** "Not Invented Here" — the organizational tendency to discount or resist external ideas — is the most common barrier to inbound innovation. It is rarely stated explicitly but shows up in how external proposals are evaluated, how acquired teams are treated, and which projects receive resources.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Open innovation as outsourced R&D cost reduction.** When firms pursue open innovation primarily to reduce R&D spend rather than to access better ideas, they typically get what they pay for: commodity external work that doesn't produce competitive differentiation.
- **Ecosystem extraction without contribution.** Organizations that participate in ecosystems to extract value without contributing — taking open-source without contributing, partnering to learn then competing, running innovation challenges without meaningful prizes or deployment — destroy the ecosystem trust that open innovation depends on.
- **The absorption gap.** The ability to source externally far exceeds the ability to integrate. A robust external scouting capability without a matching internal integration capability produces a large inventory of interesting leads and very few deployed innovations.
- **Licensing as an afterthought.** Outbound licensing and spin-out programs require dedicated management. Organizations that don't structure them explicitly leave significant value on the table and often let valuable IP expire or deteriorate.

---

## Worked Example

Procter & Gamble's Connect + Develop program is the defining case. In the early 2000s, P&G set an explicit goal that 50% of innovations should involve a significant external partner. They built an external scouting network, created a licensing infrastructure, and made external sourcing a formal performance metric for innovation leaders. Within a decade, the productivity of their R&D investment had doubled while the cost had fallen — not because they cut internal R&D, but because they connected it to a much larger global knowledge base. The Swiffer Duster is the often-cited example: the core technology came from a Japanese company via a licensing relationship; P&G provided the consumer insight, brand development, and distribution. Neither party could have produced the product alone at the speed the market required.
