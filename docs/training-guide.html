How to Use the Skills Framework

What This Guide Is For

You have access to 270 skills across 16 categories (248 mental-model cards plus 22 pre-built decision chains), organized three ways: a master reference document (Skills Framework.md), a searchable card deck (docs/deck.html), and individual deep-dive files under Skills Reference/. The framework is only as useful as your ability to retrieve and apply it under pressure. This guide teaches you how to do that.

It covers four things:

  1. How the framework is structured and why
  2. How to learn individual skills
  3. How to chain skills together for complex problems
  4. How to build a daily practice that makes these models automatic

Part 1 — Understanding the Structure

Four layers

The framework has four layers, each suited to different moments:

The connection graph (graph.html) is your discovery layer — the recommended first stop when you don't already know which skill you need. It shows all 270 skills as an interactive network, with edges representing documented connections between models. Click a node to see its connections; click a connected node to follow the thread. This layer is most valuable when the problem is open-ended, when you sense a concept is relevant but can't name it, or when you want to find the models that cluster around one you already know. The graph reveals which models work together in ways that a category list cannot.

The card deck (docs/deck.html) is your working surface. Open it when you know roughly what you're looking for — search by keyword, filter by category, and expand a card to read the mental model and heuristics. Use it in the moment when facing a specific decision or problem.

The individual skill files (General-Thinking/, Systems/, etc.) are your deep-dive layer. When a skill feels relevant but half-understood, open the full file. Read the definition, the failure modes, and the worked example. These are the files you return to when a model becomes important to your practice.

The master document (Skills Framework.md) covers the core categories in one file — best for sequential study or for searching with your editor's find function.

The two types of content in the deck

Mental model cards (248 cards across 15 categories) are standalone conceptual tools. Each one describes a pattern — how a specific class of situation tends to work — and gives you practitioner heuristics for applying it. You use them individually or chain them together.

Pre-built chains (17 chains in the Pre-built Chains category) are structured multi-step workflows for specific recurring situations. They sequence multiple mental models in the right order. Use a chain when you are facing one of the situations it names — don't build your own chain from scratch for those situations.

The distinction matters: if you are thinking about whether to enter a new market, reach for the Market Entry chain. If you are diagnosing why your team's delivery is slow, reach for Improve Delivery Flow. If you are evaluating an AI deployment proposal, reach for AI Use Case Autonomy Assessment. For everything else, start with individual cards.

The anatomy of a skill entry

Every skill has six components. Learn to use each one differently:

Definition — Read this when you're learning the skill for the first time, or when you need to explain it to someone else.

Mental Model — This is the core: one paragraph describing how an experienced practitioner holds this concept in their mind. Read this carefully. If you understand only one part of a skill, understand this part.

Practitioner Heuristics — These are actionable rules. Once you understand the mental model, these are what you actually do. Read them before entering a situation where the skill applies, and return to them afterward to check how you did.

Common Failure Modes — Read these before you apply a skill for the first time. Knowing how smart people misapply a model is often more valuable than knowing how to apply it well.

Worked Example — Read when the definition feels abstract. The example makes the skill concrete. If you're struggling to see how a model applies to your situation, look for structural parallels in the worked example.

Connections — Read when you have a rich enough understanding of the framework to chain models together. The connections section points you to the skills that amplify or check this one.

Part 2 — Learning Individual Skills

The acquisition sequence

Do not try to memorize the framework. Mental models become useful when they are internalized as intuitions, not stored as definitions. The sequence for acquiring any skill:

Step 1: Read the mental model (5 minutes). Just this paragraph. Ask: what is the core insight? If you cannot state it in one sentence, read it again.

Step 2: Apply it to a past situation (10 minutes). Think of a real decision or problem from your experience — professional or personal — where this model was relevant. How would applying it have changed the analysis? Write a few sentences. This step connects the abstract to the personal.

Step 3: Watch for it in the coming week. Once you have a model in your head, you will begin to see it operating in your environment — in a meeting, in a news story, in a conversation. When you notice it, name it explicitly: "That's a sunk cost trap." "That's a feedback loop." This is the integration phase.

Step 4: Teach it (optional, high-leverage). Explaining a model to someone else forces you to find the gaps in your understanding. If you can explain the model clearly enough that the other person immediately applies it to their own situation, you have internalized it.

The spaced repetition principle

Mental models are perishable. A model you studied three months ago and haven't used will fade. Two practices maintain them:

Weekly review: Pick three skills at random from the card deck. For each, answer in writing: "What problem type is this for? What is the key failure mode? What situation have I been in recently where this applied?" Ten minutes, three skills, once a week.

Retrospective re-reading: After any significant decision or event — a launch, a negotiation, a hiring call, a project failure — open the card deck and find the skills that applied. Did you use them? Could you have? What did the worked example predict that you missed?

Part 3 — Chaining Skills

What chaining is

No real situation is adequately analyzed by a single mental model. The practitioner's advantage is being able to apply multiple models simultaneously, each illuminating a different dimension. This is model chaining — the skill of selecting and sequencing models to build a richer analysis than any one model could produce.

How to chain: the three-step protocol

Step 1: Identify the problem type

Before selecting models, classify the situation by the type of uncertainty it involves:

  • Is this a decision under uncertainty? → Lead with Probabilistic Thinking, Margin of Safety, Two-Way Doors
  • Is this a system I'm trying to change? → Lead with Feedback Loops, Bottlenecks, Equilibrium
  • Is this a human/organizational problem? → Lead with Incentives, Loss Aversion, Maslow
  • Is this a strategic market question? → Lead with Monopoly vs. Competition, Network Effects, Creative Destruction
  • Is this about a plan with high stakes? → Lead with Inversion / Pre-mortem, Assumption Mapping
  • Is this an AI deployment or governance question? → Use the AI Use Case Autonomy Assessment chain directly

Step 2: Apply the primary model

Start with the one model that most directly addresses the core question. Do a full pass with it: what does it reveal? What does it predict? What does it recommend?

Step 3: Apply checking models

Once you have an analysis from the primary model, use the Connections section to find checking models — the skills that either amplify what you found or provide a counterweight. Apply each one. Ask: does this check the conclusion from the primary model, or extend it? If two models produce conflicting conclusions, investigate the conflict — it is usually the most informative part of the analysis.

Reading the Connections sections

The Connections section of each skill is a curated chaining map. For example, Inversion's connections point to Second-Order Effects ("inversion exposes first-order failure; second-order thinking asks what happens after the failure cascades") and Confirmation Bias ("inversion is the structural antidote to confirmation bias"). This tells you: when you are doing inversion analysis, the next models to apply are Second-Order Effects and a Confirmation Bias check.

A chaining worked example

Situation: You are deciding whether to launch a new pricing model that changes from per-seat to usage-based.

Primary model — Trade-offs: What is being traded? Revenue predictability (per-seat is predictable, usage-based is variable) vs. customer alignment (usage-based aligns cost with value). This frames the decision correctly.

Adding Second-Order Effects: Usage-based pricing incentivizes customers to minimize usage. What does that do to product engagement over time? It may reduce power users who would otherwise have been advocates.

Adding Incentives: How does the pricing model change the sales team's incentives? Usage-based is harder to forecast and creates unpredictable commissions — potential internal resistance.

Adding Loss Aversion: Existing customers will experience the change as a potential loss (their current spend may increase under usage-based if they are heavy users). Frame the transition carefully; losing existing heavy users is a real risk.

Adding Anchoring: How you announce the new pricing sets the anchor. Leading with "new pricing can save you money if you're a light user" anchors on savings; leading with "pricing now scales with your use" anchors on flexibility. The anchor affects adoption.

Checking with Pre-mortem: It is 12 months later and the pricing change has failed. Most likely cause: heavy users who were previously profitable churn because their bills increased, and the usage-based model doesn't attract enough new customers to compensate.

Result: A richer analysis than any single model produced. You now know the trade-offs, the second-order risks, the internal friction, the customer communication strategy, and the specific failure mode to protect against. Each model contributed a distinct, non-redundant insight.

Part 4 — Using the Pre-built Chains

What the chains are

The 17 pre-built chains are curated skill sequences for recurring high-stakes situations. They are not algorithms — they do not guarantee a conclusion. They are structured interrogation protocols that ensure the most important dimensions of each situation are considered in the right order.

The chains fall into three types:

Decision chains — for individual high-stakes choices. Run these yourself or with a small group before committing.

Diagnostic/design chains — for organizational or systemic situations. These are structured methodologies, closer to a consulting engagement design than a personal decision tool. They produce a written output.

AI governance chains — structured pre-deployment assessments for agentic AI systems. These should be run before any AI system goes into production at meaningful autonomy.

How to run a chain

Read the full chain before starting. Understand all the steps. Some steps generate information that is required for later steps; doing them in the wrong order reduces the quality of the analysis.

Do the work, don't just read the questions. Each step requires a concrete answer. Write it down. A chain that is read but not written produces the feeling of rigor without the substance. The writing is where the thinking happens.

Spend the most time on the steps that challenge your assumptions. You will naturally move faster through the steps that confirm your intuition and slower through the ones that create friction. Reverse this: the step where you most want to skip ahead is almost always the most important one.

Identify which steps you lack evidence for. If you cannot answer a step with evidence (only with hope or assumption), that step is a priority test — run an experiment, conduct an interview, or seek an expert before proceeding.

Run the chain with at least one other person for high-stakes decisions. Cognitive biases (confirmation bias, loss aversion, sunk cost) are much harder to self-correct than to flag in another person's analysis. A second person running the same chain independently, then comparing answers, surfaces most of the important divergences.

Part 5 — Building a Daily Practice

The core principle

Mental models become fast and automatic through practice under conditions that resemble the situations you want to use them in. Reading the framework is necessary but not sufficient. You need reps — moments of deliberate application in real situations with feedback.

Daily habits (10–15 minutes total)

Morning (5 minutes): Set the lens. Before your most important meeting or decision of the day, open the card deck and search for the models most relevant to it. Read the mental model and the heuristics. Set an intention: "In this meeting, I am going to watch for the incentive misalignments and name them."

Evening (5–10 minutes): Retrospective. After a significant decision, conversation, or event, ask: which models applied? Did you use them? What did you miss? What would have changed if you had applied X before the meeting? Two to three sentences in a notebook is sufficient. This is your feedback loop.

Weekly habits

Weekly model card: Once a week, pick one skill you have not studied recently. Read its full deep-dive file. Spend ten minutes writing a short application: "Where is this model operating in my current work? What would I do differently if I took it seriously?"

Chain practice: Once a month, take a real decision you're currently facing and run the relevant pre-built chain. Document each step. At the end, identify the two or three insights that would not have emerged without the chain.

Part 6 — Common Mistakes in Applying Mental Models

These are the failure modes of the framework itself — not the failure modes of individual skills, but the failure modes of using a collection of mental models as a practice.

Using models as vocabulary, not tools

The most common failure: becoming fluent in the language of mental models (able to name them, describe them in conversation, identify them in others' reasoning) without developing the ability to actually apply them under pressure in real time. The test is not "can I explain this model?" but "did I use it in my last hard decision?"

Fix: Track application, not comprehension. Keep a simple log of which models you applied this week. If the count is zero, the learning is not translating to practice.

One-model thinking

Applying a single model with high confidence and mistaking its partial analysis for a complete one. The practitioner who can only apply one model sees every problem through that model's lens. The practitioner who applies three models and finds them in tension has a much richer analysis.

Fix: Force yourself to apply at least three models to every significant decision, including at least one that challenges the conclusion of the first.

Seeking models that confirm the decision you've already made

Confirmation bias operating through the framework: you want to take a particular action, so you search for the model that supports it and stop there. The framework becomes a post-hoc rationalization tool rather than an analytical one.

Fix: Always apply Inversion or run a Pre-mortem. These are structural disconfirmation tools — they cannot be run honestly without generating challenges to your preferred conclusion.

Paralysis through completeness

Believing that every decision requires exhaustive model application before action. The framework has 270 skills; applying all of them to any given decision would take weeks. This is not the intent.

Fix: Match the rigor of the analysis to the reversibility and stakes of the decision. Two-Way Doors gives you the principle: reversible decisions deserve quick analysis and fast action; irreversible ones deserve deeper analysis. Most decisions are more reversible than they feel.

Forgetting that models are maps

All mental models are simplifications. The most dangerous failure mode of this entire framework is treating a model's output as a fact rather than a useful approximation. Every conclusion from model application should be held as a hypothesis, not a certainty.

Fix: After every model-chain analysis, ask: "What would I see in the real world if this model's conclusion were wrong?" That question converts model output into a testable prediction.

How to Get Started Right Now

The single most effective first step is not to read more of the framework — it is to apply one model to one real situation today.

Pick one model from Tier 1 (listed above). Open its card in the deck. Read the mental model paragraph. Then look at your calendar or your current project list and find one situation where it applies. Write two sentences about what it reveals. That is the practice. Repeat across the deck over time until retrieval is automatic.
