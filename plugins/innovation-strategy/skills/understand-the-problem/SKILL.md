# Understand The Problem

Apply this toolkit to the user’s situation. Ask for missing context before concluding. Write answers in the deliverable format below.

## Protocol

### 1. Jobs to Be Done

**Hold in mind:**
An experienced practitioner thinks about every product and feature decision through the lens of the job: what is the customer trying to accomplish, in what specific circumstance, and what are the functional, social, and emotional dimensions of that goal?

**The struggling moment.** Moesta's key contribution is identifying the struggling moment as the actual unit of demand. Jobs don't arise from demographics or abstract needs — they arise from a specific, felt moment of disruption: the point at which a customer's current situation becomes intolerable enough that they are willing to bear the cost, effort, and anxiety of changing. This is not a rational assessment ("my current solution is suboptimal") but an experiential one ("I can't keep doing it this way"). Without a struggling moment, there is no active job being hired for, regardless of how good the product is or how well-understood the job category. Demand begins with push, not pull.

**The four forces.** Whether a customer switches from their current solution to a new one is governed by four forces pulling in opposite directions:


**Do:**
- **Find the struggling moment before selling the solution.** In interviews, ask: "Tell me about the moment you first realized something had to change." Push for specificity — a date, a situation, a feeling. The struggling moment is where the job becomes real. Abstract dissatisfaction is not a struggling moment; a specific event that caused enough friction to trigger search behavior is.
- **Map all four forces, not just the pull.** For any significant adoption decision, explicitly ask: What is pushing the customer out of their current situation? What is pulling them toward the new one? What are they anxious about? What makes staying feel easier than switching? Diagnose which forces are weakest before deciding what to address.
- **Interview around the purchase decision, not the product.** Ask: What were you doing when you first thought you needed this? What did you try before? What made you finally decide to switch? This reconstructs the timeline — first thought, passive looking, active looking, deciding, first use — and reveals where in that sequence the job sharpened and what resolved the anxiety.
- **Identify all three dimensions of the job.** Functional (what task is being accomplished), social (how does this affect how others perceive the customer), and emotional (how does it make the customer feel). Products that address all three build deeper loyalty and are harder to replace.
- **Define your real competition by the job.** Ask: "What else could someone hire to do this same job?" Include non-consumption — doing nothing, doing it themselves — as a competitor. The strongest competition is often the customer's current workaround, not a rival product.
- **Design features around job completion, not feature parity.** The question is not "what do competitors have?" but "what does a customer need to get the job done reliably and satisfyingly?" — and "what reduces their anxiety enough to make the switch?"

**Avoid:**
- **Focusing only on pull, ignoring push.** Building a better product and investing in messaging that makes it more attractive addresses only one of the four forces. If the customer's push is weak — if there is no active struggling moment — a stronger pull will not create demand. The customer will appreciate the product and not buy it.
- **Defining the job too narrowly.** "Help me drill a quarter-inch hole" misses the job (hang something on the wall) and the emotional layer (make my space feel like home). Narrow job definitions produce incrementally better drills rather than stud finders, hooks, or command strips.
- **Defining the job too broadly.** "Help me be happier" is too abstract to generate product insight. The job must be specific enough to reveal what "done" looks like and what failure looks like.
- **Ignoring the switch moment.** Most product teams talk to current satisfied users. The most actionable insight comes from recent switchers — people who just adopted the product or who just left for a competitor. Their memories of the struggling moment are freshest.
- **Mistaking interest for intent.** A customer who has a job, likes the product, and has not switched is not a near-conversion waiting to be nudged. They have strong anxiety or inertia. Treating them as a pull problem (more features, better pricing) when it is an anxiety or inertia problem leads to wasted effort.
- **Feature-washing.** Reframing existing feature roadmaps as "jobs" without changing the underlying analysis. The framework produces genuine insight only when it genuinely changes how customer needs are investigated — specifically, when it surfaces the struggling moment and the four forces rather than confirming pre-existing assumptions.

### 2. Customer Development

**Hold in mind:**
The practitioner treats every assumption about customers — their problem, their needs, their willingness to pay, their buying process — as a falsifiable hypothesis that requires evidence, not intuition. The customer is not a person to sell to; they are an evidence source. The question is not "will you buy this?" (leading, generates false positives) but "tell me about the last time you experienced [problem]" (open, generates genuine evidence).

**Do:**
- **Get out of the building.** The central principle. No amount of internal analysis, competitor research, or desk research substitutes for direct customer contact. The assumptions that kill companies are most often the ones no one tested because no one spoke to customers.
- **Distinguish problem interviews from solution interviews.** In early customer discovery, talk only about the problem — don't mention your solution. The goal is to understand the problem with perfect fidelity before designing any solution. Mentioning the solution prematurely activates social desirability bias.
- **Listen for "I've tried to solve this" evidence.** Customers who have actively tried to solve the problem (built workarounds, paid for imperfect alternatives, searched extensively) are experiencing genuine pain. Customers who "might find it useful" are not your early adopters.
- **Use "the mom test" (Rob Fitzpatrick).** Customers lie to be polite. Design your questions so that honest answers are socially safe: ask about past behavior (what they have done) rather than future intention (what they would do), ask about cost/pain (what they've already spent on solutions), and ask who else has the problem.
- **Look for the customer who is already solving the problem imperfectly.** The person hacking together a solution from spreadsheets and duct tape is your beachhead customer.

**Avoid:**
- **Validation theater.** Running interviews with leading questions, hearing what you want to hear, and declaring customer validation complete. This is confirmation bias operationalized as a process.
- **Talking only to early adopters.** Early adopters have different needs, tolerance for friction, and willingness to pay than mainstream customers. Validate with mainstream customers before scaling.
- **Conflating customer development with sales.** Customer development conversations are research; treating them as sales opportunities corrupts the data and damages relationships.

### 3. Design Thinking

**Hold in mind:**
An experienced practitioner understands that design thinking's deepest value is in the problem space, not the solution space. Most organizations spend most of their innovation energy generating and evaluating solutions to a problem that was never properly understood. Design thinking inverts this: the empathize and define stages are where the highest-leverage work happens, because correctly defining the problem makes solution generation straightforward. The classic design thinking insight — "you're not designing a blood test kit, you're designing the experience of a sick person waiting for news" — illustrates why shallow problem definition produces solutions that are technically correct and humanly wrong. Prototyping in design thinking is also fundamentally different from prototyping in engineering: design thinking prototypes are tools for thinking, not demonstrations of a solution. A paper sketch that tests whether users understand a navigation concept in 20 minutes is more valuable than a polished mockup that confirms assumptions over two weeks. The point of the prototype is to fail quickly and specifically — to learn exactly which part of the current solution is wrong so the problem definition can be refined.

**Do:**
- **Spend more time on problem definition than feels right.** The empathize and define stages are consistently under-invested. A team that cannot write a specific, testable "How Might We" statement after empathy work has not yet understood the problem.
- **Interview for behavior, not opinions.** "What would you do if..." produces rationalization. "Tell me about the last time you..." produces real behavior. Observation of actual behavior is more valuable still. Design thinking's empathy stage is only as good as the quality of the field data it collects.
- **Prototype the assumption, not the solution.** Before building anything, identify the single most important assumption embedded in your current solution. Prototype that assumption — the minimum representation that would test it — rather than a working version of the solution.
- **Use constraints to force creativity in ideation.** "How might we redesign the waiting room experience with no budget?" produces more creative solutions than open-ended ideation. Constraints focus divergent energy.
- **Reframe failure as data, not defeat.** A prototype that fails has succeeded in providing learning. The question after every failed test is not "should we abandon this?" but "what specifically did we learn, and how does that update the problem definition?"

**Avoid:**
- **Skipping empathy to get to ideation.** Teams know what the solution is before they start; the empathy stage becomes validation theater. The result is innovation that solves an imagined problem.
- **One-shot process thinking.** Running design thinking as a single workshop and expecting a polished solution. Design thinking is iterative — the first round of prototyping is not supposed to produce the answer but to refine the problem definition.
- **Prototyping the solution rather than the assumption.** Building a functional prototype of the preferred solution rather than a minimal test of the riskiest assumption. This conflates design thinking with traditional development and loses the learning efficiency.
- **Treating the methodology as the output.** Organizations that run design sprints to signal innovation capability without genuine commitment to acting on the findings. Participants experience the process; the results go unimplemented.
- **Confusing stakeholder needs with user needs.** Design thinking centers the end user; most organizational processes center the stakeholder. Mixing the two produces solutions that satisfy internal requirements but fail the humans they were meant to serve.

### 4. Continuous Discovery

**Hold in mind:**
Think of customer understanding as a live signal rather than a stored fact. In a stable environment, episodic discovery is sufficient — you study the territory once and navigate from the map. In environments that move (which includes virtually all innovation contexts), the map becomes outdated faster than you can act on it. The team that discovered customers' needs six months ago and has been building against that understanding is increasingly navigating by an outdated map.

Continuous discovery installs a feedback loop at the team level: every week, at least one team member speaks with at least one customer. The findings update the opportunity space. The opportunity space informs what to build and test next. What gets built and tested produces new questions for the next round of interviews. The loop runs indefinitely, not until "we know enough."

**Do:**
- **The team does the interviews, not a research team.** The point of continuous discovery is that the people making build decisions maintain direct contact with customers. When discovery is mediated through a research team, the nuance that shapes good product decisions gets filtered out. A designer who heard a customer describe a frustrating workaround in their own words has different intuitions than one who read a researcher's synthesis of twenty similar stories.
- **Distinguish what customers say from what they do.** Continuous discovery is behavior-first: the interview surfaces what customers are trying to accomplish and what they actually do, not what they say they want or would use. "I would definitely use that" is an opinion. "I spent three hours last Tuesday trying to figure out how to do X" is a behavior. Only behaviors are evidence.
- **Don't interview to validate — interview to discover.** Teams under pressure to confirm existing plans run interviews that confirm existing plans. Continuous discovery works when the team enters each interview genuinely open to hearing something that changes the current direction. A weekly interview that only reinforces existing beliefs is a signal that the questions are too narrow or the participant pool is too homogeneous.
- **Share raw interview observations, not just conclusions.** When one team member interviews a customer, the whole team should hear the key stories directly — ideally through a short debrief with quotes and verbatim observations — not just a synthesized takeaway. Direct exposure to customer language builds shared intuition faster than summary.
- **Track interview-to-insight conversion.** Count how many interviews produce at least one new insight (a finding that updates the opportunity space) per week. A rate below 50% suggests the team is either interviewing the wrong customers, asking too narrow questions, or has genuinely saturated a portion of the space and should shift focus.

**Avoid:**
- **Discovery as a pre-launch phase.** The most common failure is treating continuous discovery as a label applied to the discovery work done before development starts. This is episodic discovery with a new name. The test: is the team interviewing customers this week, right now, regardless of where the product or innovation program is in its lifecycle?
- **Researcher-mediated discovery.** When all customer contact is routed through a UX research team, product teams lose the direct exposure to customer reality that makes discovery generative. Research teams produce excellent synthesis but cannot replace the team member who heard the customer's words and felt the friction in real time.
- **Interview fatigue and scheduling drift.** Weekly cadences degrade without deliberate maintenance. The second customer interview is always harder to schedule than the first; the tenth is harder than the fifth. Without infrastructure (a standing panel, protected calendar time, a recruiting norm), continuous discovery reverts to episodic within two to three months.
- **Confusing continuous discovery with continuous user testing.** Usability testing evaluates whether customers can use a product that has been built. Discovery interviews explore what customers are trying to accomplish before anything is built. Running usability tests on a weekly cadence is not continuous discovery — it is continuous usability testing, which answers a different question.

### 5. Opportunity Solution Trees

**Hold in mind:**
Most teams work as a linear path: we have a goal, we think of a solution, we build it, we measure whether it worked. When it doesn't work, the implicit conclusion is that the goal was wrong or the opportunity was wrong — and a new path begins from scratch. The tree reveals what this linear logic hides: there are typically many opportunities beneath any outcome, many solutions beneath any opportunity, and many experiments beneath any solution. Failing one branch does not invalidate the tree. It produces learning about that branch and shifts attention to the next.

The tree is not a planning tool — it is a learning navigation tool. It doesn't tell you which branch to pursue; it makes all the branches visible so that experiment results move you through the tree intelligently rather than randomly.

**Do:**
- **Derive opportunities from discovery, not meetings.** Opportunities that come from customer interviews and observation are grounded in actual behavior. Opportunities generated in internal brainstorming sessions are hypotheses about customer needs, not observations of them. Label them differently on the tree — it changes how you hold them.
- **Hold at least three solutions per opportunity before testing any.** The single-solution trap is the most common failure mode the tree is designed to prevent. If a team has only one solution for an opportunity, it is likely in love with the solution rather than committed to the opportunity. Generating three forces genuine exploration of the solution space.
- **Let experiments inform which branch to pursue, not intuition.** The tree creates the temptation to pick the most promising-looking branch. Resist it. Design cheap experiments that distinguish between branches and let results navigate. The branch that looked least promising often turns out to be richest.
- **Update the tree as you learn.** The tree is not a document produced once — it is a living map of the current state of exploration. New opportunities discovered in interviews get added. Solutions that fail get marked and explained. Opportunities that turn out not to connect to the outcome get pruned. A static tree is a plan; a dynamic tree is a learning system.
- **Use it across teams to share the opportunity space.** In a multi-team innovation program, each team can own branches of a shared tree. This makes cross-team learning visible: team A's experiment on one solution informs team B's work on an adjacent opportunity. The tree becomes the structure for the learnings library.

**Avoid:**
- **Confusing solutions with opportunities.** "Add a recommendation feature" is a solution. "Customers don't know what to buy next" is an opportunity. Teams that put solutions at the opportunity level lock themselves into a solution before they've understood the problem. The tree only works if the distinction is maintained rigorously.
- **Pruning the tree based on opinion rather than evidence.** Leadership reviews where promising-looking opportunities are cut because they feel unlikely, or where solutions are eliminated because they conflict with current strategy, destroy the tree's value. Pruning decisions should be based on experiment results, not intuition about which branches are worth exploring.
- **Treating the tree as a backlog.** Product backlogs are prioritized lists of things to build. Opportunity solution trees are maps of things to learn. Treating the tree as a backlog imports the wrong operating logic — teams start optimizing for throughput rather than learning quality.
- **One opportunity per outcome.** A tree with one opportunity beneath the outcome is a linear plan with extra visual complexity. The value of the tree comes entirely from representing multiple paths simultaneously.

## Deliverable format

Produce a markdown document with these sections:

### Customer segment and context (who, doing what, under what conditions)

### Job-to-be-done statement (functional, emotional, social dimensions)

### Opportunity map (pains, gains, unmet needs — using OST structure)

### Assumptions that must be true for this opportunity to be real

### Recommended next discovery move (interview, observation, or prototype)
