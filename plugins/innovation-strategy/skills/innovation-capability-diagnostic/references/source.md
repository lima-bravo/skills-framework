# Innovation Capability Diagnostic
*Take an honest inventory before you prescribe*

**Category:** [Pre-built Chains](../index.html#pre-built-chains) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../index.html)**

---

## When to Use

Use this chain at the start of any innovation engagement — before recommending structure, programs, or investment. It is also useful when an organization believes it is "doing innovation" but isn't seeing results, and needs to understand why. The output is a clear picture across six dimensions: cognitive constraints, portfolio balance, structural conditions, exploration/exploitation balance, capacity protection, and pipeline health.

---

## Step 1 — Dominant Logic: What does this organization believe without question?

Before looking at structures or processes, surface the shared mental model. Ask leaders: who is our customer, how do we create value, what does good performance look like, what would we never do? Write the answers down. The items that feel too obvious to say are the dominant logic. Then ask: which of these beliefs might be limiting what we see as possible? Look for what the organization consistently fails to fund — resource allocation is a revealed logic, not a stated one. The dominant logic is both the starting point for diagnosis and the primary reason incumbents struggle to innovate.

---

## Step 2 — Three Horizons: Where is investment and attention actually going?

Map current initiatives, projects, and resource allocations across the three horizons: H1 (optimize and defend the core), H2 (build emerging businesses), H3 (explore genuinely new options). Be empirical — use actual budget and headcount data, not strategic intention. Most incumbents discover they are running 95%+ in H1 with nominal commitments to H2 and H3 that are unfunded or part-time. The healthy long-term ratio varies by industry, but anything below 10% in H2+H3 combined signals a portfolio that is not regenerating itself. Identify which H2 and H3 bets exist and whether they have real resources, protected leadership, and clear learning milestones.

---

## Step 3 — Ambidextrous Organization: Is the structure set up to run both exploit and explore?

Examine how exploration efforts are organized relative to the core. Are they structurally separated with distinct leadership, metrics, and management systems? Or are they embedded inside business units that run on exploit metrics? Look for the telltale signs of structural failure: innovation labs that have been running for two or more years without deploying anything into the core; exploration teams staffed with people's "spare time"; innovation budgets that are renegotiated every quarter under operational pressure. Assess all three models — structural separation, contextual, cyclical — and determine which the organization is nominally claiming and which it is actually practicing.

---

## Step 4 — Exploration vs. Exploitation: Is the balance intentional or accidental?

Distinguish between organizations that have consciously chosen their explore/exploit balance and those that have drifted to an extreme. Drift toward exploitation is the default failure mode — every quarterly cycle pulls resources toward the known and measurable. Drift toward exploration is rarer but occurs in organizations that have romanticized innovation without connecting it to commercial outcomes. The diagnostic question is not "are we exploring?" but "do we have a deliberate mechanism for maintaining the balance, and is it working?" Look at how the tension between the two modes is resolved when they conflict — who wins, and why.

---

## Step 5 — Organizational Slack: Is there real protected capacity for innovation?

Examine whether exploration has genuine protected capacity or only nominal assignment. Ask: what percentage of knowledge worker time is genuinely available for non-operational work? Is that time protected by policy and management behavior, or is it consumed by meetings, escalations, and quarterly priorities? Look at management incentives — are leaders rewarded for exploration outcomes, or only for operational performance? An organization where every capable person runs at 100% utilization has no real innovation capacity regardless of its stated ambitions. The slack diagnosis often reveals the gap between the innovation narrative and the operating reality.

---

## Step 6 — Innovation Funnel: What is the shape and health of the pipeline?

Assess the end-to-end innovation pipeline: idea generation (volume, diversity, source), development (how ideas are selected, resourced, and progressed), and commercialization (how validated ideas reach the market or core business). Look for where the funnel is broken. Common failure patterns: too few ideas entering (dominant logic is filtering before generation); ideas entering but not progressing (no selection process, no resources); ideas progressing but not deploying (integration gap between explore and exploit). A healthy funnel has explicit stage criteria, fast kill decisions, and a track record of ideas that made it through. A broken funnel has a long list of initiatives that are technically "in progress" but haven't moved in months.

---

## Synthesizing the Diagnosis

The six steps produce a picture across six dimensions. Summarize your findings by naming the primary constraint — the one factor that, if addressed, would most unlock the others. Common primary constraints: dominant logic filtering out genuine exploration before it starts; structural absence of ambidexterity (everything measured on H1 metrics); slack squeezed to zero by operational pressure; integration gap blocking the funnel's exit. The primary constraint determines where to intervene first. Trying to fix all six simultaneously diffuses attention and produces superficial change.

**Key signals that the diagnosis is complete:** you can name what the dominant logic protects; you have actual resource allocation data across horizons; you have identified whether separation or integration is the structural problem; you know where in the funnel value is being lost.
