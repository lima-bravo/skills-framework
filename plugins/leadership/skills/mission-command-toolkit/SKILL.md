# Mission Command Toolkit

Apply this toolkit to the user’s situation. Ask for missing context before concluding. Write answers in the deliverable format below.

## Protocol

### 1. Mission Command

**Hold in mind:**
Think of Mission Command as the difference between a GPS app and a printed turn-by-turn list. The list fails the moment you take a wrong turn; the GPS re-routes because it knows the destination.

Three layers make Mission Command work:
1. **Clarity of intent** — what does success look like at the end state?
2. **Boundaries** — what constraints (resources, ethics, timing) are non-negotiable?
3. **Trust and competence** — subordinates must be capable enough to fill in the gaps.

### 2. Leader's Intent

**Hold in mind:**
Think of a Leader's Intent as a compass bearing, not a route map. A route map tells you which roads to take; it fails the moment a road is blocked. A compass bearing tells you which direction success lies; it remains valid regardless of which specific path you take to get there. The team's job is to plan the route. The leader's job is to set the bearing.

The three parts have a specific logic: purpose anchors *why* (so the team can prioritise), key tasks anchor *what cannot be skipped* (so the team knows where to hold firm), and end state anchors *what done looks like* (so the team knows when to stop and can judge whether their plan gets them there).

**Avoid:**
**Confusing key tasks with the full task list** — a Leader's Intent that lists every task is not a Leader's Intent; it is a brief. Key tasks are the two or three actions whose absence makes success impossible. If everything is critical, nothing is.

**Describing end state in outputs rather than outcomes** — "deliver a slide deck" is an output. "The leadership team has a shared view of options and is ready to decide" is an end state. The difference matters when the team must improvise: output descriptions don't guide judgment in novel situations; outcome descriptions do.

**Including method** — "we will use the waterfall approach and present at the steering committee on the 15th" belongs in the plan, not the intent. When the method is baked into the intent, the team cannot adapt without feeling they are violating the brief.

**Treating it as optional for short projects** — the value of Leader's Intent is highest when the situation is uncertain, not when the work is long. A one-week project in a fast-changing environment benefits as much from clear intent as a six-month programme.

### 3. Brief-Back Protocols

**Hold in mind:**
Think of the comprehension gap and the interpretation gap as two separate sieves in a production line. The first sieve (Confirmation Brief) catches words and concepts that were lost in transmission — the team that heard "brand awareness" when the leader said "conversion rate." The second sieve (Back Brief) catches logical inferences that were made incorrectly — the team that understood the goal correctly but built a plan that cannot deliver it given their assumptions about resources, timeline, or constraints.

Most organisations run without either sieve, discover the blockage at the end, and call it a communication problem. The sieves do not prevent communication problems; they catch them early, when they cost minutes rather than weeks.

### 4. OODA Loop

**Hold in mind:**
The **Orient** step is the decisive stage — it's where incoming information is filtered and interpreted through mental models, previous experience, and cultural assumptions. Two people observing the same event will orient differently, and that difference determines the quality of every decision that follows.

Executives who seek disconfirming information and hold their mental models loosely are doing high-quality Orient work. Those who filter data through fixed beliefs are degrading the center of their own decision loop.

## Deliverable format

Produce a markdown document with these sections:

### Commander's intent (purpose, end-state, key constraints — one paragraph)

### Delegated tasks and decision authority per role

### Brief-back summary (what each lead will do and why — in their words)

### OODA cadence (observe-orient-decide-act cycle and trigger conditions)

### Red lines and escalation protocol
