# Mission Command Toolkit

Apply this toolkit to the user’s situation. Ask for missing context before concluding. Write answers in the deliverable format below.

## Protocol

### 1. Mission Command

**Hold in mind:**
Think of Mission Command as the difference between a GPS app and a printed turn-by-turn list. The list fails the moment you take a wrong turn; the GPS re-routes because it knows the destination.

Three layers make Mission Command work:
1. **Clarity of intent** — what does success look like at the end state?
2. **Boundaries** — what constraints (resources, ethics, timing) are non-negotiable?
3. **Trust and competence** — subordinates must be capable enough to fill in the gaps.

**Do:**
- **Brief the why before the what.** State purpose and end state first; the task list comes after and stays short. A team that knows why can improvise when the plan breaks; a team that only knows what cannot.
- **Define the non-negotiable boundaries explicitly.** Name the constraints — budget, ethics, timing, brand — that may not be crossed, then leave everything inside them to the team's judgment. Unstated boundaries get discovered by violation.
- **Calibrate delegation to competence, not hierarchy.** Mission Command assumes the person closest to the problem can decide; where that judgment is genuinely absent, build it before delegating rather than pretending the doctrine works regardless.
- **Verify intent was received — don't assume it.** Pair every intent briefing with a brief-back: have the team restate purpose and end state in their own words before they plan.
- **Resist re-taking control when the plan deviates.** Deviation from the plan is expected; only deviation from the intent should pull you back in.

**Avoid:**
- **Delegating method while leaving intent vague.** The worst of both worlds: the team is told how but not why, so it executes precisely toward the wrong end state the moment conditions change.
- **"Empowerment" without competence or boundaries.** Handing autonomy to a team that lacks the judgment or information to use it is abdication, not Mission Command — confident movement in the wrong direction.
- **Punishing good-faith improvisation that didn't work.** The first time a leader second-guesses a defensible call made under uncertainty, the team learns to wait for orders, and the doctrine is dead.
- **Confusing Mission Command with absence of leadership.** Setting clear intent and hard boundaries is more demanding than issuing orders, not less.

### 2. Leader's Intent

**Hold in mind:**
Think of a Leader's Intent as a compass bearing, not a route map. A route map tells you which roads to take; it fails the moment a road is blocked. A compass bearing tells you which direction success lies; it remains valid regardless of which specific path you take to get there. The team's job is to plan the route. The leader's job is to set the bearing.

The three parts have a specific logic: purpose anchors *why* (so the team can prioritise), key tasks anchor *what cannot be skipped* (so the team knows where to hold firm), and end state anchors *what done looks like* (so the team knows when to stop and can judge whether their plan gets them there).

**Do:**
- **Lead with purpose, then key tasks, then end state — and stop.** The three-part structure is the discipline; adding method collapses it back into a brief and removes the team's ability to adapt.
- **Make it short enough to recall from memory under pressure.** If someone two levels down cannot hold the intent without notes, it cannot guide judgment when the plan breaks and the radio goes quiet.
- **Describe the end state as an outcome, not an output.** "The leadership team is ready to decide between two costed options" guides improvisation; "deliver a slide deck" does not.
- **Name only the two or three key tasks whose failure would be fatal.** If everything is critical, nothing is, and the team loses the signal about where to hold firm versus where to flex.
- **Test the intent on someone who wasn't briefed.** Hand it to an outsider and ask what they would do if the plan failed and they couldn't reach you; hesitation or a wrong answer is a communication problem to fix before execution, not a competence problem.

**Avoid:**
**Confusing key tasks with the full task list** — a Leader's Intent that lists every task is not a Leader's Intent; it is a brief. Key tasks are the two or three actions whose absence makes success impossible. If everything is critical, nothing is.

**Describing end state in outputs rather than outcomes** — "deliver a slide deck" is an output. "The leadership team has a shared view of options and is ready to decide" is an end state. The difference matters when the team must improvise: output descriptions don't guide judgment in novel situations; outcome descriptions do.

**Including method** — "we will use the waterfall approach and present at the steering committee on the 15th" belongs in the plan, not the intent. When the method is baked into the intent, the team cannot adapt without feeling they are violating the brief.

**Treating it as optional for short projects** — the value of Leader's Intent is highest when the situation is uncertain, not when the work is long. A one-week project in a fast-changing environment benefits as much from clear intent as a six-month programme.

### 3. Brief-Back Protocols

**Hold in mind:**
Think of the comprehension gap and the interpretation gap as two separate sieves in a production line. The first sieve (Confirmation Brief) catches words and concepts that were lost in transmission — the team that heard "brand awareness" when the leader said "conversion rate." The second sieve (Back Brief) catches logical inferences that were made incorrectly — the team that understood the goal correctly but built a plan that cannot deliver it given their assumptions about resources, timeline, or constraints.

Most organisations run without either sieve, discover the blockage at the end, and call it a communication problem. The sieves do not prevent communication problems; they catch them early, when they cost minutes rather than weeks.

**Do:**
- **Run both checkpoints — they catch different failures.** The Confirmation Brief closes the comprehension gap ("did you understand what I said?"); the Back Brief closes the interpretation gap ("does your plan match what I meant?"). A team can pass the first and still fail the second.
- **In the Confirmation Brief, listen — don't confirm or correct yet.** Have the team restate purpose, key tasks, and end state in their own words while you listen for what is missing, distorted, or overweighted; correcting too early masks the gap you are trying to measure.
- **In the Back Brief, flag assumptions — don't redesign the plan.** The leader's output is a list of flags ("the plan assumes X — is that validated?"), not a better plan; method belongs to the team, and taking it back destroys the ownership the protocol depends on.
- **Frame both as a drill applied to every briefing.** Making it routine regardless of the briefing's quality removes the implied insult ("I think you didn't listen") and the implied micromanagement ("I don't trust your plan") that otherwise make both checkpoints socially costly.

**Avoid:**
- **Skipping the brief-back to protect face.** Asking "tell me what you heard" feels like accusing the listener of inattention or the briefer of poor communication, so both parties avoid it — and misalignment surfaces at delivery instead, when correction is most expensive.
- **Treating accurate read-back as proof of a good plan.** A team can repeat the intent perfectly and still build a plan that cannot deliver it; comprehension and interpretation are independent, which is exactly why both checkpoints exist.
- **The leader redesigning the plan during the Back Brief.** Crossing from flagging assumptions to fixing the plan converts an assumption-surfacing step into approval-seeking and strips the team of ownership.
- **Running them without psychological safety.** Teams surface honest comprehension gaps and plan doubts only if being wrong in front of the leader is safe; in a blame culture both briefs produce a confident, sanitised account rather than a useful one.

### 4. OODA Loop

**Hold in mind:**
The **Orient** step is the decisive stage — it's where incoming information is filtered and interpreted through mental models, previous experience, and cultural assumptions. Two people observing the same event will orient differently, and that difference determines the quality of every decision that follows.

Executives who seek disconfirming information and hold their mental models loosely are doing high-quality Orient work. Those who filter data through fixed beliefs are degrading the center of their own decision loop.

**Do:**
- **Invest in Orient, not just speed.** Cycling fast through a distorted orientation only reaches wrong decisions faster; most decisive advantage comes from orienting better, not acting quicker.
- **Actively seek disconfirming information.** The Orient stage degrades silently when you ingest only data that confirms the current picture; build in a deliberate search for what would prove you wrong.
- **Operate inside the opponent's tempo, don't just match it.** The advantage is relative — act before they can respond to your last move, so each orientation they form is already stale.
- **Hold your mental models loosely.** The moment a model becomes identity, new data stops updating it; treat every orientation as provisional.
- **Re-enter the loop deliberately when the situation shifts.** A Tactical Pause is a forced fresh Observe–Orient cycle; use it when you sense the picture has changed but execution momentum is carrying you forward.

**Avoid:**
- **Skipping Orient under pressure.** Jumping from Observe straight to Act — pattern-matching to the last similar situation without checking whether this one differs.
- **Analysis paralysis in Orient.** The opposite failure: orienting forever and never deciding, surrendering tempo to anyone willing to act.
- **Confirmation bias as Orient corruption.** Filtering observations through fixed beliefs so the loop confidently converges on the wrong picture — Boyd's single biggest degrader.
- **Mistaking activity for tempo.** Cycling rapidly through low-quality decisions feels fast but creates no advantage; tempo counts only if each action is good enough to force the opponent to re-orient.

## Deliverable format

Produce a markdown document with these sections:

### Commander's intent (purpose, end-state, key constraints — one paragraph)

### Delegated tasks and decision authority per role

### Brief-back summary (what each lead will do and why — in their words)

### OODA cadence (observe-orient-decide-act cycle and trigger conditions)

### Red lines and escalation protocol
