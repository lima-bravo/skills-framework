# Attention Budget
*Personal WIP discipline — the individual analogue of flow*

**Category:** [Delivery & Flow](../index.html#delivery-flow) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../index.html)**

---

## Definition

An attention budget is the explicit limit a knowledge worker — particularly a leader or consultant — places on the number of concurrent commitments their attention is split across. It treats focus as a finite resource subject to Little's Law: the more open loops, the longer each one takes to close, and the more cognitive overhead is consumed by context switching. The concept synthesizes Cal Newport's *Deep Work* (2016), David Allen's *Getting Things Done* (2001), and Reinertsen's individual-WIP framing into a single operational rule: budget attention before time, because attention is the binding constraint.

---

## Mental Model

A leader who is "on" seven initiatives, six clients, and four programs simultaneously is the cognitive equivalent of a server running at 99% utilization across seven processes — context switches dominate, latency on any single task is enormous, and small interruptions trigger disproportionate damage. Cutting to three concurrent commitments has the same effect at the personal level that WIP limits have at the team level: latency drops, quality rises, the work that survives the cut is the work that matters. The leader doesn't do less work; they finish more of it.

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Cap concurrent commitments.** A working number for many senior practitioners is 3–5 active workstreams; above that, throughput drops.
- **Distinguish active from passive.** A "watching brief" is not on the budget; an active workstream is.
- **Audit your calendar.** Time blocked is a proxy for attention; if more than ~20% of the week is recurring committee meetings, the attention budget is already overspent.
- **Treat saying no as a leadership skill.** Each new "yes" is a debit; ambient pressure to say yes inflates the budget invisibly.
- **Schedule deep work blocks.** Two-to-four-hour blocks are the unit of cognitively expensive work. Anything less is interrupted work pretending to be focus.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **"Multi-tasking" self-deception.** Most concurrent leadership is sequential interruption. The cost is paid in latency and quality.
- **Calendar tetris as a strategy.** Filling every slot does not maximize output; it maximizes context-switch tax.
- **Treating focus time as a perk.** It is operational infrastructure; without it, the system runs slower.
- **Padding the budget for "small" items.** A 30-minute item with 4 days of background ambient thinking is not small. Count it.

---

## Worked Example

A consulting partner running eight workstreams reports chronic delays and team frustration. An attention audit reveals 22 active commitments on the calendar, 14 of which the partner is the named decision-maker on. A 90-day reset caps concurrent active commitments at four; the remaining 18 are either transferred to other partners, paused with explicit owners, or killed. Within six weeks: response time on the four active commitments drops 4x, the partner reports having time to think for the first time in two years, and client satisfaction on the surviving four rises. The partner had not lacked time; they had lacked attention.

---

## Common Failure Modes (cont.)

- **Treating focus-loss as a personal failure.** It is a systems problem. Diagnose at the system level — the operating model that creates this many concurrent demands — not the individual level.
