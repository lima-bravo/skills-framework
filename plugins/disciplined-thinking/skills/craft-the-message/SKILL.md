# Craft The Message

Apply this toolkit to the user’s situation. Ask for missing context before concluding. Write answers in the deliverable format below.

## Choose a branch

Pick the branch that best matches the situation, then apply its models:

- **Design the narrative** — Narrative Instinct, Framing, Grice's Maxims
- **Calibrate for the audience** — Audience, Grice's Maxims, Ladder of Inference

## Protocol

### 1. Narrative Instinct

**Hold in mind:**
Think of narrative instinct as a compression algorithm for experience. The world arrives as a stream of loosely connected events; the algorithm converts it into a compact, transmissible story with actors, causes, and meaning. Like all compression, it discards information to achieve the reduction — and what gets discarded is exactly the complexity, randomness, and systemic causation that most resists the story format. The output is a story that feels complete and true, but is a lossy encoding of reality. The danger isn't that we tell stories; it's that we forget the compression happened.

**Do:**
- **After explaining why something happened, ask: "does this require a protagonist?"** If your explanation centres on a person's intention or character, check whether a systems-level explanation fits the same facts without requiring anyone to have wanted this outcome.
- **Require quantitative or structural corroboration before accepting a causal narrative.** A compelling story is not evidence. Ask: what evidence would distinguish this narrative from an alternative one? If the answer is "none — the story fits everything," the story is unfalsifiable and therefore uninformative.
- **In strategy work, name the narrative you're inside.** Every organisation operates inside a founding story or industry story. Naming it makes it possible to ask whether it still matches reality, rather than treating it as a transparent window onto truth.
- **Use pre-mortems to disrupt narrative completion.** The narrative instinct wants a success story; the pre-mortem forces the imagination of a failure arc, surfacing failure paths that the success narrative would have suppressed.
- **In presentations and proposals, recognise that narrative is a tool of persuasion as well as sense-making.** A compelling story can make a weak case feel strong. When you're being persuaded by a narrative, slow down and ask for the data behind it.

**Avoid:**
**The post-hoc hero story.** After a success, the organisation constructs a narrative in which visionary leadership recognised the opportunity, made the bold decision, and executed flawlessly. This narrative suppresses the luck, the abandoned alternatives, the near-failures, and the conditions that made success possible. Future strategy is then modelled on the hero story rather than the actual causal structure.

**Root cause as a plot device.** Post-mortems and incident analyses often converge on a single "root cause" because narrative requires a source of the action. Real failures are typically multi-causal, with distributed, interacting factors. The single root cause is a narrative convenience that makes the post-mortem report satisfying to write and ineffective to act on.

**Dominant Logic entrenchment.** An organisation's founding narrative ("we succeed by being the premium option") becomes the lens through which all new evidence is filtered. Evidence that fits the narrative is noticed; evidence that contradicts it is reframed. The narrative outlives the conditions that made it true. See: Dominant Logic.

**Strategy as story.** A strategic plan that tells a compelling arc — from current state through inflection to future state — is more likely to be approved than one that accurately represents uncertainty and multiple contingencies. The narrative instinct rewards stories over models, even in contexts where models are more appropriate.

### 2. Grice's Maxims

**Hold in mind:**
Most communication failures are not failures of accuracy — they are failures of calibration. The content is correct but the packaging is wrong: too much for the audience's available attention, too little for the decision they need to make, irrelevant to the actual question, or obscured by structure that serves the writer rather than the reader.

Grice's Maxims make this precise. The Maxim of Quantity sets the detail floor and ceiling: enough to enable the listener's purpose, no more. The Maxim of Relation is the prior filter: before deciding how much to say, decide whether to say it at all. The Maxim of Quality governs confidence calibration — overstatement and understatement are both violations. And Manner is the delivery: clarity and brevity in service of all three.

The practitioner question for every communication is: *what decision or action does this need to enable, and what is the minimum truthful, relevant, and clear content that enables it?* Everything else is noise — and noise is not neutral. It crowds out signal, consumes attention, and, if systematic, trains the audience to stop listening carefully.

**Do:**
- **Calibrate detail to the decision, not to your comfort.** The amount of detail you include should be determined by what the reader needs to decide or act — not by how much analysis you did, how confident you feel, or how long you spent on it. A two-sentence briefing that enables a good decision is better than a ten-page report that produces paralysis or a wave of clarifying questions.
- **Test every section against the Relation maxim before the Quantity maxim.** Ask "is this relevant to the reader's purpose?" before asking "how much of it to include?" Irrelevant content included at any level of detail is a violation of the prior maxim.
- **Notice what your detail level signals, not just what it says.** Excess detail to a senior audience signals insecurity or political hedging. Insufficient detail to an execution team signals that you don't trust or respect their need to understand. Deliberate brevity to a board signals confidence. The signal is often received more strongly than the content.
- **Flout maxims intentionally, not accidentally.** There are legitimate reasons to include more detail than strictly required (to build trust, to pre-empt a known objection, to demonstrate methodology on a contested point). But do it knowingly, not by default. Accidental flouting is noise; deliberate flouting is strategy.
- **Different audiences require different calibrations of the same content.** A briefing to the board, the executive team, and the delivery team on the same initiative should differ substantially in detail level — not because the truth changes, but because the decision each group needs to make is different. One version written for all three is optimised for none.

**Avoid:**
**Detail calibrated to the author, not the reader.** The most common violation: the level of detail reflects how much the author knows, how much effort they spent, or how confident they feel — not what the reader needs. This is the origin of the 40-slide deck sent to a board that wanted an executive summary, and the two-sentence email sent to a team that needed a proper brief.

**Quantity compensating for Quality doubts.** When communicators are uncertain about their conclusion, they often add more supporting detail — hoping that volume will substitute for confidence. This is Quantity flouting in service of Quality anxiety. Experienced readers recognise it; it achieves the opposite of its intent.

**Relevance debt.** Communicators include background, context, and caveats that were relevant to how the analysis was constructed, but are not relevant to the decision being made. The reasoning trace is mistaken for the reasoning. The reader either wades through it or gives up before reaching the point.

**Manner as an afterthought.** Clarity and brevity are treated as style preferences rather than as respect for the reader's attention. Jargon that saves the writer three words but costs the reader ten seconds is a net negative at scale. Passive constructions that obscure agency ("a decision was made") are Manner violations with organisational consequences.

**The completeness trap.** The instinct to include every qualification, limitation, and caveat in the name of intellectual honesty. This is real — omitting material caveats violates Quality. But there is a version of this that tips into Quantity violation: caveats that are technically accurate but not decision-relevant, included because the author feels exposed without them. The discipline is to include caveats that change the decision, not caveats that protect the author.

### 3. Audience

**Hold in mind:**
Seth Godin's formulation: "Who is it for? What is it for?" Every element of the work — vocabulary, length, tone, format, examples, level of detail — should be determined by the answer to these questions. A technical deep-dive written for a senior engineer is a different artefact from a strategic overview written for a CEO, even if the underlying content is the same. The work that tries to serve both typically fails to satisfy either.

**Do:**
- **Name the specific person.** Not "B2B software decision-makers" but "a VP of Engineering at a Series B startup who is currently debating whether to build or buy a data infrastructure solution and has 10 minutes." The more specific the audience model, the better the choices about what to include and exclude.
- **Ask what the audience already knows.** Pitching too basic insults the audience; pitching too advanced loses them. The calibration requires a genuine model of the audience's prior knowledge.
- **Design the first 30 seconds for the audience.** The opening of any communication — a document, a presentation, a product — must answer the audience's implicit question: "Is this for me? Is this worth my time?" Design the opening to answer that question clearly and immediately.
- **The audience is not you.** Designers, writers, and founders often mistake their own perspective for the audience's. The discipline is to genuinely inhabit the audience's perspective — their constraints, vocabulary, concerns, and goals — not to project your own.
- **Test your audience model.** Before committing to audience-specific design choices, check them against actual audience members. Your model of the audience is a hypothesis; the actual audience is the territory.

**Avoid:**
- **Designing for the critics, not the audience.** Creating work that is admired by peers in your discipline but fails to communicate with its intended audience.
- **Assumed audience homogeneity.** Treating the audience as a uniform group rather than a distribution of individuals. Understanding the variance in the audience is as important as understanding the center.

### 4. Framing

**Hold in mind:**
Content enters a frame. The frame determines which reference points, mental schemas, and evaluative criteria the audience applies to the content. Well-chosen frames activate favorable schemas; poorly chosen or adversarially set frames activate unfavorable ones. The practitioner's discipline is to design the frame deliberately — asking "what reference points does this frame activate, and are they the right ones for this audience?"

**Do:**
- **Establish the frame before the content.** The context set in the opening of any communication is the frame through which all subsequent content is interpreted. Open with the frame, then deliver the content.
- **Identify the incumbent frame before attempting to change it.** In a persuasion context, the audience already has a frame. Your job is to understand their current frame and either work within it or deliberately reframe.
- **Use narrative framing over data framing for motivating action.** Data activates the analytical brain; story activates the experiential brain. For inspiring action or change, a well-chosen narrative frame is typically more effective than statistics.
- **Anchor the frame with specifics.** Frames built around specific, vivid details are more durable than those built on abstractions. "A child in this community who can't read by age 8" is a more powerful frame than "early childhood literacy challenges."
- **Test your frame against audience first principles.** The best frames connect content to what the audience already deeply values. Identify those values and design frames that activate them.

**Avoid:**
- **Inadvertent framing.** Every communication has a frame, whether designed or not. Failing to design the frame means accepting whatever the audience imposes. This often works against the communicator.
- **Reframing too early.** Attempting to change an audience's frame before acknowledging the legitimacy of their current frame creates resistance. Lead with acknowledgment; follow with reframing.
- **Frame-content mismatch.** A frame that promises one thing (exciting innovation, urgent crisis) and content that delivers another (incremental improvement, ambiguous data) damages credibility.

### 5. Ladder of Inference

**Hold in mind:**
The ladder explains why smart people looking at the same situation reach opposite conclusions: they have climbed different ladders. Each rung is an inference — a mental step that is invisible to the person making it but determines everything above it. The practitioner's discipline is to "walk back down the ladder" — both in their own thinking and in dialog with others — to identify where the divergence began and whether the selection, interpretation, or assumption at that rung is well-founded.

**Do:**
- **When in conflict, go back to the data.** "What specifically did you observe?" forces the conversation back to the bottom rung, where the gap between perspectives is most likely to be identified. Most conflicts that feel like value disagreements are actually disagreements about data selection or interpretation.
- **Name the rung you're on.** "I'm stating a belief here, not a fact" or "this is my interpretation, not what was said" builds cognitive clarity and models epistemic humility for others.
- **Challenge your own selection filters.** What data have you been noticing, and what have you been ignoring? The selection at rung 2 is often the most consequential and the least visible.
- **Ask "what would I have to believe at each rung for this conclusion to be valid?"** Tracing the ladder upward from a specific conclusion reveals the assumptions at each rung that must be true. Challenging those assumptions challenges the conclusion.
- **Use in facilitated conversations.** When a team is stuck in a conflict, a facilitator who walks both parties back down their respective ladders to observable data often reveals that the disagreement is about an inference made early in the chain, not about values or facts.

**Avoid:**
- **Reflexive loops.** Beliefs at the top of the ladder influence what data we select at the bottom — confirmation bias is the reflexive loop of the ladder. The belief reinforces itself by selecting confirming data. This is the deepest failure mode and the hardest to interrupt.
- **Treating conclusions as data.** Presenting conclusions (rung 5) or beliefs (rung 6) as if they were observations (rung 1). When this occurs, the chain of inference is hidden and cannot be examined.
- **Ladder-walking as delay tactic.** In fast-moving operational contexts, going back to data for every disagreement is impractical. Know when the ladder analysis is worth the time investment (high-stakes, persistent conflict) vs. when to trust the inference and move on.

## Deliverable format

Produce a markdown document with these sections:

### Communication goal: what decision or action should this message produce?

### Audience profile: what do they know, what do they care about, what would stop them acting?

### Grice's audit: is every element necessary? Is anything missing that the audience needs?

### Narrative structure: situation → complication → resolution (or equivalent)

### Framing choice: gain or loss frame? Which reference point serves the goal?

### Ladder of Inference check: are conclusions clearly separated from evidence?

### Revised message (one paragraph lead, supporting structure)
