# Influence And Stakeholders

Apply this toolkit to the user’s situation. Ask for missing context before concluding. Write answers in the deliverable format below.

## Choose a branch

Pick the branch that best matches the situation, then apply its models:

- **Map the landscape before acting** — Power Mapping, Stakeholder Mapping, Overton Window
- **Build the coalition** — Coalition Building, Reciprocity, Commitment & Consistency Bias
- **Make the ask land** — Influence Without Authority, Framing, Overton Window

## Protocol

### 1. Power Mapping

**Hold in mind:**
Every organisation has two structures: the formal structure (the org chart, the RACI, the governance framework) and the informal structure (who people actually go to for a decision, whose approval is silently required, whose objection will kill an initiative regardless of formal authority). Initiatives that only navigate the formal structure succeed if the informal structure happens to align. When it doesn't — which is most of the time in complex organisations — the initiative stalls or fails for reasons that are never officially named.

A power map makes the informal structure visible and workable. It asks: who formally decides? Who actually decides? Who influences the decider? Who has veto power even if they lack formal authority? Whose silence is interpreted as consent, and whose public support will be followed by private opposition? The map is not permanent — power structures shift as people, roles, and priorities change — but at any given moment, it is the terrain on which organisational change actually happens.

**Do:**
- **Map formal authority and informal influence separately, then compare.** Begin with the formal structure (who is the accountable decision-maker per the governance framework?), then layer informal influence (who does the decision-maker go to before deciding? whose opinion are they known to weight heavily?). The gap between the two layers is where most initiatives get stuck.
- **Identify gatekeepers and shapers, not just decision-makers.** Power in organisations flows through three roles: decision-makers (who formally decide), shapers (who influence the decision-maker without deciding), and gatekeepers (who control access to information or to the decision-maker). Failing to map shapers and gatekeepers is the most common power-mapping gap.
- **Look for informal veto holders.** In most organisations, there are individuals who lack formal authority to approve but whose opposition is sufficient to kill. They may sit in legal, finance, risk, or IT; they may be informal advisors to the CEO; they may be respected technical experts whose concerns the organisation takes seriously regardless of hierarchy. Map them explicitly.
- **Update the map when the situation changes.** Power structures are dynamic. A reorganisation, a leadership change, a significant success or failure — any of these can shift informal influence dramatically. A power map built six months ago may be materially wrong today.
- **Use the map to sequence conversations.** The primary practical use of a power map is determining who to talk to, in what order, before an initiative is formally proposed. Talk to shapers before decision-makers; neutralise or convert veto holders before the public discussion; ensure that by the time the decision is formally made, the informal structure has already moved.

**Avoid:**
**Mistaking the org chart for the power map.** The most common failure: assuming that formal authority equals real influence, and planning stakeholder engagement based on hierarchy rather than actual decision dynamics. This produces technically correct engagement (all the right people were consulted) that is strategically wrong (the real influencers were ignored or approached too late).

**Mapping positions rather than people.** Power is personal, not positional. The CHRO in one organisation may be a powerful informal advisor to the CEO; the CHRO in the next organisation may be ignored in all but HR decisions. The map must reflect the actual person, not the role title.

**Ignoring negative space.** A power map typically focuses on who has influence. Equally important: who is conspicuously absent from discussions where they should be present? Absence from key conversations can signal political marginalisation, deliberate exclusion, or a governance gap — all of which create risk for initiatives that depend on those parties.

**Using the map to manipulate rather than to understand.** Power mapping is an analytical tool, not a manipulation playbook. Its purpose is to understand the influence landscape so that legitimate engagement can be sequenced effectively. Using it to bypass important stakeholders, to engineer artificial consensus, or to outmanoeuvre opposition without addressing the substance of their concerns is both ethically problematic and strategically fragile.

### 2. Stakeholder Mapping (Salience Model)

**Hold in mind:**
Most stakeholder management fails by treating all stakeholders as equally important (producing diffuse, diluted engagement) or by focusing exclusively on the most vocal (who are not necessarily the most salient). The salience model provides a principled basis for prioritization: more attributes present → higher salience → more immediate and intensive engagement required. The framework is dynamic: salience changes as power, legitimacy, and urgency shift. A latent stakeholder who gains urgency rapidly elevates in salience.

**Do:**
- **Map stakeholders before any significant decision, initiative, or change.** Unexpected resistance, regulatory problems, and reputational crises are most commonly caused by a stakeholder whose salience was not identified before action was taken.
- **Assess all three dimensions independently.** A regulator has high power and high legitimacy; they may have low urgency in a routine period. A community group may have low power currently but high legitimacy and growing urgency. The attribute profile is more informative than a single overall ranking.
- **Watch for salience shifts.** A dormant stakeholder (low salience) can rapidly become definitive if an event grants them urgency or coalition-building grants them power. Monitor the attribute levels of latent stakeholders regularly.
- **Tailor engagement to salience level.** Definitive stakeholders: active, frequent, direct engagement. Expectant stakeholders: regular communication, two-way dialog. Latent stakeholders: monitoring and periodic informational contact.
- **Don't confuse voice with salience.** The loudest stakeholders are not necessarily the most salient. Map against the three attributes, not against volume of complaint or visibility of presence.

**Avoid:**
- **Focusing on power alone.** Engaging only with powerful stakeholders while ignoring high-legitimacy, high-urgency stakeholders who lack current power. These stakeholders can rapidly acquire power through coalitions, media, or regulatory escalation.
- **Static mapping.** Building a stakeholder map once and treating it as fixed. Salience attributes change; maps must be updated before each major decision.
- **Conflating internal and external stakeholders without appropriate weighting.** Internal stakeholders (employees, board) and external stakeholders (regulators, customers, communities) require different engagement approaches even at the same salience level.

### 3. Influence Without Authority

**Hold in mind:**
Formal authority is a shortcut: it converts "I have ranked power over you" into compliance. Influence without authority takes the longer route: it converts "I understand your situation, I have something valuable to offer, and I have demonstrated trustworthiness" into willing cooperation. The longer route is slower to build but more durable, more transferable, and more effective in environments where the people you need to move don't report to you — which describes almost every complex organisational challenge.

The practitioner who relies exclusively on formal authority is limited to what they can mandate. The practitioner who has built influence without authority can move things their title doesn't reach. This is not a soft skill — it is a strategic capacity, and in matrix organisations, cross-functional teams, and external partnerships, it is often the primary determinant of whether an initiative succeeds.

**Do:**
- **Build credibility before you need it.** Influence is borrowed from a credibility account that must be funded before you can draw on it. The practitioner who shows up needing something from someone they've never invested in will find the account empty. Identify the people you are likely to need to influence and invest in the relationship before the ask arrives.
- **Lead with their agenda before yours.** The most common influence failure is leading with what you need before demonstrating understanding of what the other person needs. Ask what they are working on, what is making it hard, and what would make it easier. Understand their priorities genuinely. Influence that ignores the other person's agenda is pushing against a wall.
- **Use reciprocity deliberately, not manipulatively.** Influence through reciprocity — offering something of genuine value before asking for something in return — is structurally legitimate when the value offered is real. Identify what you can offer that the person actually needs: information, access, advocacy, skills, time. Genuine reciprocity builds the relationship; transactional manipulation destroys it when detected.
- **Make your ask specific and small first.** Influence is cumulative. A first ask that succeeds — because it is small, specific, and easy to comply with — establishes a pattern of cooperation that makes larger asks progressively more natural. Commitment & Consistency Bias works in your favour: once someone has helped you once, they are more likely to help again.
- **Attribute credit generously.** People who are influenced toward your position must see themselves as choosing it, not as being managed into it. Giving away credit for ideas and decisions — even ideas that originated with you — makes the person who receives the credit a genuine advocate rather than a passive complier.

**Avoid:**
**Treating influence as a transaction rather than a relationship.** One-off attempts to influence someone you have not invested in reliably fail or backfire. Influence is a consequence of ongoing relationships, not a technique applied in isolation.

**Confusing activity with influence.** Sending many emails, attending many meetings, presenting many documents — these create the appearance of influencing without the substance. Actual influence is measured by whether decisions and behaviours change, not by the volume of communication.

**Overestimating the power of logic.** In organisational settings, most decisions are not made primarily on analytical merit. The person who produces the best analysis and then wonders why the decision went another way has mistaken the formal decision rationale for the actual decision driver. Influence requires attending to relationships and interests, not just to analytical correctness.

**Using influence to bypass rather than to build.** The most fragile form of influence without authority is the kind that engineers outcomes by circumventing people who have legitimate concerns. Even when it succeeds in the short term, it creates resentment and opposition that surfaces later. Durable influence addresses concerns, it doesn't route around them.

**Neglecting upward influence.** Most practitioners focus influence efforts laterally and downward. Upward influence — shaping the thinking of people senior to you — is often more consequential and requires a different approach: understanding what the senior person is accountable for, what risks concern them, and how your initiative connects to their priorities.

### 4. Coalition Building

**Hold in mind:**
Coalitions succeed when the people inside them have more to gain from staying than from defecting, and when their collective support creates a political reality that opposition cannot reverse. Coalitions fail when they are held together by a single champion (who can be removed), by shared opposition rather than shared purpose (which collapses once the opposition changes), or by relationships that cannot survive the point of maximum resistance.

The foundational insight: the sequencing of who joins the coalition matters as much as who ultimately joins it. Early members shape the coalition's identity and credibility. A coalition that begins with the right early members attracts the right subsequent members; a coalition that begins with the wrong ones signals the wrong things about what the initiative represents. The first three to five members are the coalition's brand.

**Do:**
- **Identify the minimum winning coalition before recruiting broadly.** A coalition does not need everyone — it needs enough of the right people to make the outcome irreversible. Map the decision landscape: whose support is necessary, whose support makes others' support more likely, and whose support is sufficient to neutralise the key opposition. Build to that specification, not to maximum breadth.
- **Recruit early members for credibility, not just support.** The first members of a coalition signal its character. An early member who is trusted across political divides, who brings credibility with the decision-maker, or who represents a constituency that would otherwise oppose is worth ten later members who signal little. Sequence the recruitment to build credibility before building size.
- **Give coalition members genuine ownership, not nominal support.** A coalition member who has been persuaded to support the initiative is fragile — they can be re-persuaded by the opposition. A coalition member who has shaped the initiative, whose ideas are reflected in the design, and whose concerns have been incorporated is robust — they are defending something partly theirs. Genuine ownership requires genuine influence over the outcome.
- **Manage the coalition as a system, not a list.** Coalitions have internal dynamics: members who are drifting need re-engagement, members who are overcommitted need relief, and the relationships between members matter as much as each member's individual support. A coalition that looks fully assembled on a stakeholder map may be fracturing internally.
- **Anticipate the point of maximum resistance and prepare the coalition for it.** Every significant change initiative reaches a point at which opposition is most organised and the coalition is most tested. The coalition that has not been prepared for this moment — that has not discussed what the opposition will argue and agreed on how to respond — is vulnerable to fracture at exactly the wrong time.

**Avoid:**
**Building a coalition of the willing rather than the necessary.** Recruiting people who are easy to persuade rather than people whose support is required produces a large, enthusiastic coalition that lacks the specific influence needed to make the outcome happen. Count support by its strategic value, not its volume.

**Holding the coalition together through a single champion.** A coalition whose members are committed to a person rather than to an initiative collapses if that person leaves, is marginalised, or loses credibility. Build the coalition around a shared purpose with distributed ownership so that no single exit is fatal.

**Announcing the coalition before it is ready.** A coalition that is announced publicly before it is internally aligned will fracture under the pressure of public scrutiny. Recruit privately, align genuinely, then go public with a coalition that can sustain the attention.

**Ignoring the opposition's coalition.** Opposition to significant change is almost always itself a coalition — informal, often unacknowledged, but real. A change initiative that does not map and respond to the opposition's informal coalition is planning on half the board.

**Mistaking silence for support.** People who have not objected publicly are not necessarily in the coalition. In organisations where political risk is high, silence is a risk-management strategy, not a commitment. Coalition members must affirmatively demonstrate their support — silence is not a coalition.

### 5. Framing

**Hold in mind:**
Content enters a frame. The frame determines which reference points, mental schemas, and evaluative criteria the audience applies to the content. Well-chosen frames activate favorable schemas; poorly chosen or adversarially set frames activate unfavorable ones. The practitioner's discipline is to design the frame deliberately — asking "what reference points does this frame activate, and are they the right ones for this audience?"

**Do:**
- **Establish the frame before the content.** The context set in the opening of any communication is the frame through which all subsequent content is interpreted. Open with the frame, then deliver the content.
- **Identify the incumbent frame before attempting to change it.** In a persuasion context, the audience already has a frame. Your job is to understand their current frame and either work within it or deliberately reframe.
- **Use narrative framing over data framing for motivating action.** Data activates the analytical brain; story activates the experiential brain. For inspiring action or change, a well-chosen narrative frame is typically more effective than statistics.
- **Anchor the frame with specifics.** Frames built around specific, vivid details are more durable than those built on abstractions. "A child in this community who can't read by age 8" is a more powerful frame than "early childhood literacy challenges."
- **Test your frame against audience first principles.** The best frames connect content to what the audience already deeply values. Identify those values and design frames that activate them.

**Avoid:**
- **Inadvertent framing.** Every communication has a frame, whether designed or not. Failing to design the frame means accepting whatever the audience imposes. This often works against the communicator.
- **Reframing too early.** Attempting to change an audience's frame before acknowledging the legitimacy of their current frame creates resistance. Lead with acknowledgment; follow with reframing.
- **Frame-content mismatch.** A frame that promises one thing (exciting innovation, urgent crisis) and content that delivers another (incremental improvement, ambiguous data) damages credibility.

### 6. Reciprocity

**Hold in mind:**
Every exchange creates a credit or debit in an invisible social ledger. Acts of generosity create credits; requests create debits; failures to reciprocate create deficits that damage the relationship. The practitioner manages this ledger consciously — not to manipulate, but to understand the dynamics of social and professional exchange, to give before asking, and to be aware of the obligations that gifts create.

**Do:**
- **Give first, without expectation of immediate return.** Reciprocity is not transactional tit-for-tat; it is the willingness to invest without a guaranteed return, trusting that over time giving creates a relationship in which requests are well-received.
- **Be aware of manipulative reciprocity.** The principle is used deliberately by salespeople and negotiators (give something small to create a large obligation). Knowing this lets you receive graciously without feeling compelled to return disproportionately.
- **In negotiation, concessions trigger reciprocity.** Making a concession creates a psychological pressure for the other party to reciprocate. Concession strategy in negotiation should account for this: make calibrated concessions designed to generate reciprocal movement.
- **In organizations, reciprocity drives collaboration.** Teams that help each other get help. Leaders who invest in their team's growth receive discretionary effort. This is not manipulation; it is the basic mechanics of social capital.
- **Understand what constitutes a "gift" in context.** What creates a sense of obligation varies by culture, industry, and relationship. Information, introductions, public recognition, and time can all function as gifts.

**Avoid:**
- **Expecting exact reciprocity.** Keeping precise score destroys the social contract. Reciprocity operates over time and across situations, not transaction-by-transaction. Expecting immediate return converts a gift into a trade.
- **Using reciprocity manipulatively.** Giving specifically to create an obligation for a specific return is a manipulation tactic. When perceived, it damages trust more than the gift earned in goodwill.
- **Failing to reciprocate important investments.** When someone invests significantly in you (mentorship, sponsorship, a major favor), not reciprocating in some meaningful form eventually damages the relationship and your reputation.

### 7. Overton Window

**Hold in mind:**
The window is not fixed. What was unthinkable in one era becomes mainstream in the next, and vice versa. The window moves when the boundary is pushed — by advocates, by changed circumstances, by crises that make previously unacceptable ideas suddenly plausible. Crucially, the window rarely moves by convincing people directly of the previously unthinkable position; it moves by normalising positions just outside the current boundary, which shifts the entire distribution of acceptable discourse.

For practitioners, the Overton Window answers a question that frustrates people in organisations and movements alike: "Why won't anyone engage with this obviously correct idea?" Often the answer is not that the idea is wrong, but that it currently falls outside the window. The response is rarely to argue harder for the radical position — it's to understand where the window is, identify the proposals that are just outside it, and work on normalising those first.

The same logic applies in reverse: when you see an extreme position gaining traction in a conversation, it may be functioning as a boundary-pusher designed to make a less extreme position feel more acceptable. Understanding this makes you less susceptible to the tactic.

**Do:**
- **Diagnose whether resistance is intellectual or political.** If a good idea is being rejected, ask: is this because people think it's wrong, or because it's outside their current window? The interventions are entirely different.
- **Map the current window before proposing.** For any significant organisational or strategic change, sketch the range from "unthinkable" to "current policy." This tells you where to anchor your proposal and which precursor ideas to normalise first.
- **Shift the window before making the ask.** If your target proposal is currently outside the window, introduce a more extreme version first — not to win support for it, but to make your actual proposal feel moderate by comparison.
- **Watch for manufactured window-shifts.** When extreme positions appear suddenly in a conversation, check whether they are genuine positions or are being used to move the window. The tell: the extreme position is floated and then strategically abandoned once the window has shifted.
- **Crises move windows fast.** Events that make the status quo untenable — financial crashes, product failures, competitive losses — can shift the Overton Window dramatically in a short time. Prepare proposals for the post-crisis moment, not just the current one.

**Avoid:**
- **Arguing for the right answer in the wrong window.** Insisting on a technically correct but politically unacceptable position without acknowledging that the window needs to move first. This generates defensiveness rather than progress.
- **Confusing window position with quality of argument.** A weak argument inside the window beats a strong argument outside it for near-term adoption. Accepting this is not cynicism; it's strategic literacy.
- **Pushing the window too hard, too fast.** Aggressive boundary-pushing can trigger a backlash that moves the window in the opposite direction. Incremental normalisation is usually more durable.
- **Forgetting that the window is audience-specific.** The Overton Window varies by audience. What is mainstream in one organisation, geography, or community may be unthinkable in another. A proposal calibrated to one audience can alienate another.

### 8. Commitment & Consistency Bias

**Hold in mind:**
Think of each commitment as a stake driven into the ground with a rope attached to your future self. A single stake is easy to pull up. But the longer you've been walking in one direction, the more stakes you've driven, the more people have watched you walk — and the harder each step backward becomes, not because the direction is right, but because changing direction requires acknowledging all the distance you've already walked in the wrong direction. Consistency bias is the experience of being pulled by your own rope.

**Do:**
- **Separate the decision from the decision-maker's identity.** When reviewing a struggling initiative, frame the question as "is this still the right path?" rather than "was the original decision right?" The first question is about the future; the second triggers consistency defence.
- **Normalise updating explicitly.** In team culture, treat changed minds as evidence of good reasoning, not weakness. Leaders who visibly update on new evidence give others permission to do the same.
- **Use pre-specified kill criteria.** Deciding in advance what evidence would trigger an exit removes the pressure of making a in-the-moment pivot that feels like abandoning a prior commitment. The decision was already made; you're just executing it.
- **Watch for escalation language.** Phrases like "we've come too far to stop now," "we need to protect the investment," or "we can't afford to be seen changing course" are consistency bias speaking. They are never valid reasons to continue.
- **In strategy reviews, ask: "if we hadn't already started this, would we start it now?"** This resets the evaluation to the present evidence rather than the prior commitment.

**Avoid:**
**Sunk cost conflation.** Consistency bias and sunk cost reasoning are closely related and mutually reinforcing. Resources already spent are used as reasons to continue (sunk cost), and the identity attached to the decision amplifies the resistance to changing course (consistency bias). Together, they can sustain failing initiatives far beyond rational endpoints.

**Public commitment lock-in.** A strategy, product direction, or initiative that has been announced publicly becomes significantly harder to reverse than one still in development. Leaders feel (correctly) that reversals will be interpreted as failures of judgement. The result is that decisions get locked in at the moment of public announcement rather than when the evidence is strongest.

**Defensive briefing.** When new evidence challenges a prior decision, teams brief against it rather than updating on it. Arguments are generated to explain why the new evidence doesn't actually challenge the prior view. The prior view is protected; the learning is discarded.

**Identity-level entrenchment.** When a belief becomes part of a leader's or team's identity ("we are a premium brand," "we are data-driven," "we move fast") it becomes almost impossible to revise, because doing so would require revising who they are. The belief is no longer evaluated on evidence; it is defended as self-definition.
