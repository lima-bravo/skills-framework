# OKRs
*Objectives and Key Results — outcome-anchored alignment*

**Category:** [Delivery & Flow](../index.html#delivery-flow) &nbsp;|&nbsp; **[← Card Deck](../index.html)**

---

## Definition

OKRs — Objectives and Key Results — are a goal-setting structure pairing a qualitative aspiration (the Objective) with 2–5 quantitative outcome measures (the Key Results) that signal whether the Objective is being achieved. Developed by Andy Grove at Intel and popularized by John Doerr (*Measure What Matters*, 2018), the structure forces three disciplines simultaneously: stating intent in customer/business language, defining outcome-based success measures, and exposing both vertically across the organization. The point is alignment without micromanagement: leaders set "what" and "how well"; teams own "how."

---

## Mental Model

OKRs are an alignment protocol, not a performance management tool. The Objective answers "what are we trying to achieve?" — directional, ambitious, and qualitative. The Key Results answer "how will we know we got there?" — measurable, outcome-anchored, and uncomfortable when at full ambition. The structure works when leaders trust teams to choose the work and teams trust leaders to mean what they wrote. It fails when either side mistakes OKRs for the activity (the Key Results become "ship X" rather than "X moves the dial") or treats them as commitments (then they get sandbagged).

---

## Practitioner Heuristics

- **Outcome KRs, not activity KRs.** "Reduce cycle time from 18 to 8 days" is an outcome; "implement a new ticket system" is an activity. The latter is an initiative under the former.
- **Stretch is the default.** A 70% achievement on a stretch OKR signals real ambition; 100% achievement signals the goal was sandbagged.
- **3–5 KRs per Objective, max.** More fragments focus; fewer leaves blind spots.
- **Cascade, but lightly.** Team OKRs should ladder to org Objectives; identical KRs at three levels signals fake alignment.
- **Decouple from performance reviews.** The moment OKRs are tied to compensation, ambition drops and gaming rises.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **Output disguised as outcome.** "Ship five major features" is an output; if the underlying outcome (e.g. activation rate) isn't named, the OKR isn't doing its job.
- **Too many.** An organization with 28 OKRs and 92 KRs has not aligned; it has built a project list.
- **OKRs forever-static.** Cascading once per year produces stale priorities by Q2.
- **Treating OKRs as a planning tool only.** They are an operating mechanism — weekly progress, mid-quarter resets, retrospectives.
- **Confusion with KPIs.** KPIs measure ongoing health; OKRs target change. Health metrics are not OKRs.

---

## Worked Example

A 600-person enterprise software business introduces OKRs to replace a feature roadmap. Year-1 mistakes: 14 objectives, KRs that are mostly feature ships, no mid-quarter check-ins. Re-baseline: 3 organizational objectives, each with 3–4 outcome KRs (e.g. "lift net retention from 102% to 115%"). Teams self-organize initiatives under the KRs; weekly the leadership team reviews KR movement, not initiative status. Year-2 outcomes: 2 of 3 objectives hit 70%+ on stretch KRs; the third triggers a strategic pivot mid-year because the KR is not moving and the diagnosis is structural. The CEO reports the strategic-pivot conversation as the single most valuable thing OKRs did.
